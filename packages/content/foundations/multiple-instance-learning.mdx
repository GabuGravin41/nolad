### Setting

A **bag** $B = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ of **instances** carries a single label $y$, and the labels of the individual instances are unknown. A pathology slide is a bag of tiles, a crop of a lumbar MRI is a bag of slices, and a CT series is a bag of images. The standard assumption for binary labels is that a bag is positive if and only if at least one of its instances is positive:

$$
y = \max_{i} y_i, \qquad y_i \in \{0, 1\} \text{ unobserved}.
$$

A multiple instance learning (MIL) model encodes each instance as an embedding $h_i = f(x_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, pools the embeddings into a bag vector $z \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, and classifies $z$. The pooling operator determines how evidence from individual instances is combined.

### Pooling operators

**Max pooling**, $z_c = \max_i h_{i,c}$ for each dimension $c$, matches the assumption that one positive instance suffices. Its gradient with respect to $h_{i,c}$ is 1 for the instance attaining the maximum and 0 for all others, so each training step updates the encoder through one instance per dimension.

**Mean pooling**, $z = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i h_i$, passes gradient to every instance equally, but dilutes the evidence from a few positive instances. If one tile out of $n = 1000$ has a strong response $a$ in some dimension and all others have a background value $b$, the pooled value is $b + (a - b)/1000$.

**Generalised mean (GeM)** pooling, for non-negative embeddings and $p \ge 1$,

$$
z_c = \left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} h_{i,c}^{\,p}\right)^{1/p},
$$

equals mean pooling for $p = 1$ and tends to max pooling as $p \to \infty$, because the largest term dominates the sum of $p$-th powers. The exponent $p$ can be learned.

**Attention pooling** (Ilse et al., 2018) computes a weight for each instance with a small network,

$$
e_i = w^\top \tanh(V h_i), \qquad
a_i = \frac{\exp(e_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} \exp(e_j)}, \qquad
z = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i\, h_i ,
$$

with learned parameters $V \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ and $w \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$. The weights $a_i$ are positive and sum to 1, so $z$ is a weighted average of the instance embeddings. The weights indicate which instances contributed most to the prediction.

**Top-$k$ and bottom-$k$ selection (CHOWDER)** (Courtiol et al., 2018) computes a scalar score $s_i = u^\top h_i$ for every instance, keeps the $k$ largest and the $k$ smallest scores, and classifies the bag from these $2k$ numbers with a small multilayer perceptron. The winners of UBC-OCEAN used CHOWDER with $k = 10$ on embeddings from a pathology foundation model and report that it performed as well as more recent MIL architectures.

### Ordered instances

When the instances have an order, such as slices through a vertebra or frames in time, a bidirectional LSTM or GRU can process the sequence of embeddings before pooling, so that each embedding also reflects its neighbours. The winner of the RSNA lumbar spine competition used this design: a CNN for each slice, a bidirectional LSTM across the slices, attention pooling, and an auxiliary loss that encourages the attention weights to peak at the annotated slice.
