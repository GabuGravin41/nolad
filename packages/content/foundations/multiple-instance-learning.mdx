### The setting

A **bag** $B = \{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ of **instances** has one label $y$. A pathology slide is a bag of tiles; a lumbar MRI crop is a bag of five slices; a CT series is a bag of images. The standard assumption: the bag is positive if at least one instance is positive. Instance labels are unknown.

A MIL model encodes each instance to an embedding $h_i = f(x_i)$, pools the embeddings into one bag vector $z$, and classifies $z$. The choice of pooling is the design decision.

### Pooling operators

- **Max pooling**, $z = \max_i h_i$ (per dimension): matches the "one positive instance suffices" assumption but passes gradient to only one instance.
- **Mean pooling**, $z = \frac1n\sum_i h_i$: stable, but a small lesion is diluted by many normal tiles.
- **Generalised mean (GeM)**, $z = \left(\frac1n\sum_i h_i^{\,p}\right)^{1/p}$: interpolates between mean ($p=1$) and max ($p\to\infty$), with $p$ learned.
- **Attention pooling** (Ilse et al., 2018):

$$
a_i = \frac{\exp\big(w^\top\tanh(Vh_i)\big)}{\sum_j\exp\big(w^\top\tanh(Vh_j)\big)}, \qquad z = \sum_i a_i h_i .
$$

A small network scores each instance; the softmax turns scores into weights; the bag vector is the weighted average. The weights show which instances drove the prediction, which makes the model partly interpretable.

- **Top-$k$ and bottom-$k$ selection (CHOWDER)** (Courtiol et al., 2018): score every tile with a linear layer, keep the $k$ highest and $k$ lowest scores, and classify from those $2k$ numbers. The UBC-OCEAN winners used CHOWDER with $k=10$ on foundation-model embeddings and found it as good as newer MIL architectures.

### Adding order

When instances are ordered (slices through a vertebra, frames in time), a bidirectional LSTM or GRU can process the sequence before pooling, so each instance's embedding reflects its neighbours. The lumbar spine winner's "LSTM-MIL" did exactly this: CNN per slice, BiLSTM across slices, attention pooling, with an auxiliary loss asking the attention to peak at the correct slice.
