### Sequences and hidden states

A sequence input is a list of vectors $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T$, with $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$ for each step $t$. The steps may be time samples of a signal, frames of a video, or slices of a CT scan in order. A **recurrent neural network** (RNN) maintains a **hidden state** $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$, a vector that summarises the inputs up to step $t$. The initial state $h_0$ is usually the zero vector.

### The simple recurrent network

At each step the network combines the new input with the previous state:

$$
h_t = \tanh\big(W_x x_t + W_h h_{t-1} + b\big),
$$

where $W_x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_x}$ and $W_h \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_h}$ are weight matrices, $b \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$ is a bias, and $\tanh$ is applied to each component. The same $W_x$, $W_h$ and $b$ are used at every step, so the number of parameters does not depend on $T$ and the network accepts sequences of any length. An output at step $t$ is computed from $h_t$, for example by a linear layer.

### Vanishing and exploding gradients

Training uses **backpropagation through time**: the recurrence is unrolled into a feed-forward computation over $T$ steps and differentiated with the chain rule. A loss at step $t$ depends on an earlier state $h_{t-k}$ through the product of the step-to-step Jacobians.

*One step.* Write $a_t = W_x x_t + W_h h_{t-1} + b$, so that $h_t = \tanh(a_t)$. The derivative of $\tanh$ is $1 - \tanh^2$, so

$$
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}} = \mathrm{diag}\big(1 - h_t^{2}\big)\,W_h ,
$$

where $h_t^{2}$ is squared component-wise and $\mathrm{diag}(v)$ is the diagonal matrix with $v$ on its diagonal.

*$k$ steps.* By the chain rule,

$$
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}} = \prod_{s=t-k+1}^{t} \mathrm{diag}\big(1 - h_s^{2}\big)\,W_h .
$$

*Bound.* Each diagonal factor has entries in $[0, 1]$, so its operator norm is at most 1. With $\lVert W_h \rVert$ the operator norm of $W_h$,

$$
\Big\lVert \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}} \Big\rVert \le \lVert W_h \rVert^{k} .
$$

If $\lVert W_h \rVert = 0.9$, the bound after $k = 50$ steps is $0.9^{50} \approx 0.005$, and the gradient from 50 steps back is negligible. If the largest singular value of $W_h$ exceeds 1 and the $\tanh$ units are not saturated, the product can instead grow; for a factor of 1.1 per step, $1.1^{50} \approx 117$. The first case is called **vanishing** and the second **exploding** gradients. Exploding gradients are commonly limited by gradient clipping. Vanishing gradients motivated the gated cells below.

### LSTM

The long short-term memory cell (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997; the forget gate was added by Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins, 2000) keeps a second state, the **cell state** $c_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$, which is updated additively. Write $[x_t, h_{t-1}] \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x + d_h}$ for the concatenation of the input and the previous hidden state, $\sigma(a) = 1/(1 + e^{-a})$ for the logistic sigmoid, and $\odot$ for component-wise multiplication. Each gate is a vector with entries in $(0,1)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_t &= \sigma(W_f[x_t, h_{t-1}] + b_f) &&\text{forget gate}\\
i_t &= \sigma(W_i[x_t, h_{t-1}] + b_i) &&\text{input gate}\\
o_t &= \sigma(W_o[x_t, h_{t-1}] + b_o) &&\text{output gate}\\
\tilde c_t &= \tanh(W_c[x_t, h_{t-1}] + b_c) &&\text{candidate cell state}\\
c_t &= f_t\odot c_{t-1} + i_t\odot\tilde c_t\\
h_t &= o_t\odot\tanh(c_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

Each $W_\bullet \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times (d_x + d_h)}$ and $b_\bullet \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$. The forget gate sets the fraction of each component of $c_{t-1}$ that is kept, the input gate the fraction of the candidate that is written, and the output gate the fraction of the cell state that is exposed as $h_t$.

Along the direct path through the cell state, holding the gates fixed,

$$
\frac{\partial c_t}{\partial c_{t-1}} = \mathrm{diag}(f_t).
$$

No weight matrix appears in this factor. If the forget gate stays near 1, the product over many steps stays near 1: for $f = 0.99$ in every component, $0.99^{50} \approx 0.61$. Information and gradient can therefore pass over many more steps than in the simple recurrent network.

### GRU

The gated recurrent unit (Cho et al., 2014) has a single state $h_t$ and two gates:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_t &= \sigma(W_z[x_t, h_{t-1}] + b_z) &&\text{update gate}\\
r_t &= \sigma(W_r[x_t, h_{t-1}] + b_r) &&\text{reset gate}\\
\tilde h_t &= \tanh(W[x_t,\; r_t\odot h_{t-1}] + b) &&\text{candidate state}\\
h_t &= (1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1} + z_t\odot\tilde h_t .
\end{aligned}
$$

The new state is a component-wise interpolation between the old state and the candidate. The reset gate controls how much of the old state enters the candidate. Libraries differ in whether $z_t$ multiplies the old state or the candidate; the two conventions are equivalent after replacing $z_t$ by $1 - z_t$.

### Parameter count

An LSTM has four blocks of the form $(W_\bullet, b_\bullet)$ and a GRU three, each with $d_h(d_x + d_h) + d_h$ parameters. For $d_x = d_h = 256$, one block has $256 \times 512 + 256 = 131{,}328$ parameters, so

$$
\text{LSTM: } 4 \times 131{,}328 = 525{,}312, \qquad \text{GRU: } 3 \times 131{,}328 = 393{,}984 .
$$

The GRU has three quarters of the LSTM's parameters. Empirical comparisons, beginning with Chung et al. (2014), have found the two cells to perform similarly on many tasks, with neither consistently better.

### Bidirectional networks

A **bidirectional** RNN runs one recurrence from $t = 1$ to $T$ and a second, with separate weights, from $t = T$ to $1$. The output at step $t$ is the concatenation $[\overrightarrow{h}_t, \overleftarrow{h}_t] \in \mathbb{R}^{2 d_h}$, which depends on inputs on both sides of $t$. This requires the whole sequence to be available before any output is produced, which holds for a stored CT series or a recorded accelerometer trace and does not hold for real-time streaming.

### Use in the reports

In most of the reports an RNN is applied to learned features. A convolutional network converts each slice or frame into an embedding vector, and a recurrent network reads the sequence of embeddings. In the 2019 intracranial haemorrhage competition, the first-place team added a GRU over slice embeddings and the second-place team trained LSTMs over them, and similar CNN-plus-RNN designs appear in solutions to the cervical spine fracture, pulmonary embolism, abdominal trauma and lumbar spine competitions. In the sleep-state detection competition, the winners' base model placed a residual GRU between convolutional downsampling and upsampling layers and read the accelerometer signal directly.
