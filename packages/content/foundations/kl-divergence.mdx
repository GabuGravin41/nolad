### Definitions

Let $P = (P_1, \dots, P_K)$ be a target distribution and $Q = (Q_1, \dots, Q_K)$ a predicted distribution over $K$ classes, with $P_k, Q_k \ge 0$ and $\sum_k P_k = \sum_k Q_k = 1$. The **entropy** of $P$ and the **cross-entropy** of $Q$ relative to $P$ are

$$
H(P) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} P_k \log P_k, \qquad H(P, Q) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} P_k \log Q_k,
$$

with the convention $0 \log 0 = 0$. The **Kullback–Leibler divergence** is

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} P_k \log\frac{P_k}{Q_k} = H(P, Q) - H(P).
$$

With natural logarithms these quantities are measured in nats; with base-2 logarithms, in bits.

**Worked example.** For $P = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1)$ and $Q = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = 0.7\ln\tfrac{0.7}{0.5} + 0.2\ln\tfrac{0.2}{0.3} + 0.1\ln\tfrac{0.1}{0.2}
\approx 0.2355 - 0.0811 - 0.0693 = 0.0851 \ \text{nats}.
$$

### Non-negativity

$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $Q = P$. Sum over the classes with $P_k > 0$. Since the logarithm is concave, Jensen's inequality gives

$$
-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = \sum_{k:\,P_k > 0} P_k \log\frac{Q_k}{P_k}
\;\le\; \log \sum_{k:\,P_k > 0} P_k\,\frac{Q_k}{P_k}
= \log \sum_{k:\,P_k > 0} Q_k \;\le\; \log 1 = 0 .
$$

Equality in Jensen's inequality requires $Q_k / P_k$ to be constant over the classes with $P_k > 0$, and the last step requires $Q$ to put no mass elsewhere; together these give $Q = P$.

### Minimising KL divergence is minimising cross-entropy

$H(P)$ does not depend on $Q$. For a model that outputs $Q$, minimising $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$ over the model parameters is therefore the same problem as minimising $H(P, Q)$. If $Q = \mathrm{softmax}(z)$ for logits $z$, the gradient of the cross-entropy with respect to the logits is (see the softmax cross-entropy primer)

$$
\frac{\partial H(P, Q)}{\partial z_k} = Q_k - P_k .
$$

### Asymmetry and near-zero predictions

The divergence is not symmetric: in general $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) \ne D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,P)$. In $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$, a class with $P_k > 0$ and $Q_k \to 0$ contributes $P_k \log(P_k/Q_k) \to \infty$, while a class with $P_k = 0$ contributes nothing regardless of $Q_k$. When the target is a distribution of expert votes, as in the HMS brain-activity competition, a prediction of near-zero probability for a class chosen by even a few experts is heavily penalised. For example, with $P_k = 0.2$, predicting $Q_k = 0.001$ contributes $0.2 \ln 200 \approx 1.06$ nats from that class alone. The HMS winners used ensembles of diverse models and output functions that avoid exact zeros for this reason.
