### Definitions

An **ensemble** of $M$ models $f_1, \dots, f_M$ predicts, for an input $x$, the average

$$
\bar f(x) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} f_m(x).
$$

A **weighted ensemble** uses weights $w_m \ge 0$ with $\sum_m w_m = 1$ and predicts $\sum_m w_m f_m(x)$. The members may differ in architecture, input resolution, input representation, training data or only in the random seed.

**Test-time augmentation (TTA)** applies one model to several transformed copies of the same input (flips, rotations, small shifts) and averages the results. It is an ensemble whose members share their weights.

### Variance of an averaged prediction

Let $y \in \mathbb{R}$ be the target and let model $m$ predict $\hat y_m = y + \varepsilon_m$, where the error $\varepsilon_m$ is a random variable with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Assume every pair of distinct errors has the same correlation $\rho$, so that $\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon_m, \varepsilon_n) = \rho\,\sigma^2$ for $m \ne n$. The error of the average is $\bar\varepsilon = \frac1M\sum_m \varepsilon_m$.

A constant factor comes out of a variance squared:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\bar\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{M^2}\,\mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varepsilon_m\Big).
$$

The variance of a sum is the sum of the $M$ variances plus the $M(M-1)$ covariances between distinct pairs:

$$
\mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varepsilon_m\Big) = M\sigma^2 + M(M-1)\,\rho\,\sigma^2 .
$$

Substituting and dividing by $M^2$:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\bar\varepsilon) = \frac{\sigma^2}{M} + \frac{M-1}{M}\,\rho\,\sigma^2 .
$$

Collecting the terms in $\rho$:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\bar\varepsilon) = \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{M}\,\sigma^2 .
$$

As $M$ grows, the second term tends to zero and the first remains. The variance of the ensemble therefore cannot fall below $\rho\,\sigma^2$, however many members are added. With $\rho = 0$ the variance is $\sigma^2/M$; with $\rho = 1$ averaging has no effect.

Worked numbers, with $\sigma^2 = 1$ and $M = 10$:

- $\rho = 0.8$ (for example, the same architecture trained with ten seeds): $0.8 + 0.2/10 = 0.82$.
- $\rho = 0.4$ (members that differ more): $0.4 + 0.6/10 = 0.46$.

If every member has the same systematic error (bias) $b$, the averaged error also has mean $b$. Averaging reduces the variance of the errors and leaves a shared bias unchanged.

Members that differ in architecture, resolution, input representation or training data tend to have less correlated errors than members that differ only in the random seed, and by the formula above they reduce the variance more. The winning team in the HMS brain-activity competition wrote that diversity among its models mattered because the competition metric, a KL divergence, is sensitive to extreme errors.

### Combining predictions on different scales

Models trained with different losses or sampling schemes produce scores on different scales, and a plain average is then dominated by the model with the widest range. Three remedies appear in the reports.

**Rank averaging.** For $N$ test cases, let $s_m(i)$ be the score of model $m$ for case $i$, and let $r_m(i) \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ be its rank among model $m$'s scores (1 for the lowest). The ensemble score is the average of the normalised ranks $r_m(i)/N$. For example, with scores $(0.90, 0.60, 0.10)$ from model A and $(0.020, 0.030, 0.010)$ from model B, the plain average $(0.460, 0.315, 0.055)$ follows model A almost entirely. The ranks are $(3, 2, 1)$ and $(2, 3, 1)$, and their average $(2.5, 2.5, 1)$ gives both models equal influence. ROC AUC depends only on the order of the scores, so rank averaging is suited to it. The ISIC 2024 winner combined 150 gradient-boosted models by averaging their percentile ranks with equal weights.

**Quantile matching.** Sort the $n$ values predicted by model A, $a_{(1)} \le a_{(2)} \le \dots \le a_{(n)}$, and replace the value of model B with rank $k$ by $a_{(k)}$. The mapped values of B keep B's ordering and have exactly A's distribution, and the two sets of values can then be averaged. The CZII cryo-ET winners applied this mapping per particle class to the voxel-wise outputs of their segmentation and detection models before averaging them.

**Stacking.** A second-stage model learns how to combine the members' predictions. It is trained on **out-of-fold (OOF)** predictions: in $K$-fold cross-validation each training example receives a prediction from the model trained on the other $K-1$ folds, which never saw that example. Predictions made on a model's own training data are more accurate than its predictions on new data, and a second stage trained on them would give too much weight to the members that overfit most. The HMS winners fitted a non-negative linear regression per target class, which chooses weights $w_m \ge 0$ to minimise

$$
\sum_{i} \Big( y_i - \sum_{m} w_m\, s_m(i) \Big)^2 .
$$

### Test-time augmentation

Let $T_1, \dots, T_K$ be transformations of the input. For an output that does not change under the transformations, such as the class of a whole image, the TTA prediction is

$$
\hat p(x) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} f\big(T_k x\big).
$$

For an output defined on the input grid, such as a segmentation mask, each prediction must be mapped back to the original geometry before averaging:

$$
\hat p(x) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} T_k^{-1} f\big(T_k x\big).
$$

For a horizontal flip of an image of width $W$, $T$ moves column $j$ to column $W + 1 - j$, and $T^{-1} = T$.

When outputs have a left–right meaning, a flip also exchanges the meaning of the outputs. If $\Pi$ is the permutation that swaps each left output with its right counterpart, the prediction on the flipped input enters the average as $\Pi f(Tx)$. The second-place team in the RSNA intracranial aneurysm competition used 8 TTA variants with this exchange of labels.

TTA helps when the model has learned to be approximately invariant to the transformations, usually because they were used as training augmentations. The copies share one set of weights, so their errors are usually strongly correlated, and by the variance formula above the reduction is correspondingly limited.

### Inference cost

An ensemble of $M$ models, each applied with $K$ TTA copies, needs $M K$ forward passes per input. At 0.5 s per pass, four models with eight TTA copies take $4 \times 8 \times 0.5 = 16$ s per input. Under a fixed time limit, members and copies compete with input resolution for the same budget. The second-place team in the RSNA mammography competition chose a single model at high resolution over an ensemble at lower resolution, and the second-place team in the aneurysm competition reduced its ensemble to two folds to stay within the time limit.
