### The imbalance problem

In ISIC 2024 about one lesion in a thousand is malignant. With plain cross-entropy the gradient is dominated by hundreds of thousands of easy negatives, each contributing a little, and the rare positives barely move the model.

### Focal loss

Lin et al. (2017) multiplied the cross-entropy of each example by a factor that vanishes as the example becomes easy. Writing $p_t$ for the probability assigned to the true class ($p_t = p$ if $y=1$, $1-p$ if $y=0$),

$$
\ell_{\text{focal}} = -\,\alpha_t\,(1-p_t)^{\gamma}\log p_t .
$$

With $\gamma = 0$ this is weighted cross-entropy. With $\gamma = 2$, an example classified with $p_t = 0.9$ has its loss multiplied by $(0.1)^2 = 0.01$, while a hard example with $p_t = 0.1$ keeps $0.81$ of its loss.

Focal loss does not produce calibrated probabilities; its optimum is not at the true frequency. Solutions scored by log loss therefore usually avoid it or recalibrate afterwards.

### Sampling instead of reweighting

Most winners handle imbalance by changing what the model sees rather than the loss:

- **Oversampling positives**, so that every batch contains at least one. The mammography winner used a positive-to-negative ratio of 1:7 and stressed that every batch should hold a positive.
- **Undersampling negatives** each epoch (ISIC 2024, 1:3 to 1:5), which also makes epochs much faster.
- **Balanced batches** (1:1) for image models whose outputs are later recalibrated by a downstream model.

Sampling changes the prior the model learns. A model trained on 1:1 batches will output probabilities around 0.5 for ambiguous cases even if the true rate is 0.1%. That is harmless for ranking metrics such as AUC and harmful for log loss unless corrected. Bayes' rule gives the correction: if positives were sampled at rate $r_1$ and negatives at $r_0$, the model's odds must be multiplied by $r_0/r_1$ to recover the odds under the original distribution.
