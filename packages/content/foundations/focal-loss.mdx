### Class imbalance

In a binary problem with $N$ examples, of which $N_+$ are positive, the **prevalence** is $\pi = N_+/N$. In the ISIC 2024 skin-lesion data, about 393 of about 401,000 training images were malignant, so $\pi \approx 0.001$: one positive per thousand examples.

A classifier outputs $p \in (0,1)$, the predicted probability that the label $y \in \{0,1\}$ equals 1. The binary cross-entropy of one example is

$$
\ell_{\mathrm{CE}}(y, p) = -\big[\, y \log p + (1-y)\log(1-p) \,\big],
$$

and training minimises the sum over all examples.

Suppose a model assigns $p = 0.01$ to each of 400,000 negatives and $p = 0.3$ to each of 393 positives. Each negative is almost correctly classified and contributes $-\log 0.99 = 0.0101$. Each positive contributes $-\log 0.3 = 1.204$. The totals are

$$
400{,}000 \times 0.0101 \approx 4{,}020 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 393 \times 1.204 \approx 473 .
$$

The easy negatives account for about 89% of the loss, although improving them further would hardly change the model's decisions.

### Focal loss

Write $p_t$ for the probability that the model assigns to the true class:

$$
p_t = \begin{cases} p & \text{if } y = 1, \\ 1 - p & \text{if } y = 0. \end{cases}
$$

In both cases the cross-entropy equals $-\log p_t$. Lin et al. (2017), working on dense object detection, multiplied it by a factor that tends to zero as the example becomes easy:

$$
\ell_{\mathrm{focal}}(y, p) = -\,\alpha_t\,(1-p_t)^{\gamma}\log p_t .
$$

Here $\gamma \ge 0$ is the **focusing parameter**, and $\alpha_t = \alpha$ for positives and $1 - \alpha$ for negatives, with $\alpha \in [0,1]$ a fixed class weight. With $\gamma = 0$ the focal loss is class-weighted cross-entropy. Lin et al. used $\gamma = 2$ and $\alpha = 0.25$.

Worked numbers with $\gamma = 2$ and $\alpha_t = 1$:

- An easy example with $p_t = 0.9$ has cross-entropy $0.105$. The factor $(1 - 0.9)^2 = 0.01$ reduces it to $0.00105$.
- A hard example with $p_t = 0.1$ has cross-entropy $2.303$. The factor $(1-0.1)^2 = 0.81$ reduces it to $1.865$.

The ratio of the hard to the easy loss rises from about 22 under cross-entropy to about 1,770 under focal loss. In the imbalanced example above, each negative ($p_t = 0.99$) is multiplied by $10^{-4}$ and each positive ($p_t = 0.7$) by $0.49$. The negatives then contribute $0.40$ in total and the positives $232$.

### Calibration under focal loss

A model is **calibrated** if, among inputs for which it predicts $p$, the fraction of positives is $p$. Consider an input whose true probability of being positive is $q$. The expected loss of predicting $p$ is

$$
E(p) = -\,q\,(1-p)^{\gamma}\log p \;-\; (1-q)\,p^{\gamma}\log(1-p),
$$

taking $\alpha_t = 1$. For $\gamma = 0$, setting the derivative $-q/p + (1-q)/(1-p)$ to zero gives $p = q$, so cross-entropy is minimised by the true probability. For $\gamma > 0$ the minimiser lies between $q$ and $0.5$. Minimising $E$ numerically for $q = 0.9$ gives $p = 0.774$ at $\gamma = 1$ and $p = 0.694$ at $\gamma = 2$; for $q = 0.3$ it gives $0.383$ and $0.422$. A model trained with focal loss therefore produces probabilities that are too close to 0.5. The ranking of inputs is not affected, but a metric such as log loss penalises the distortion, and the probabilities have to be recalibrated on held-out data if they are used as probabilities.

### Resampling

Imbalance can also be addressed by changing how often each class is presented during training.

- **Oversampling positives.** The RSNA mammography winner used a sampler with a positive-to-negative ratio of 1:7 and ensured that each batch contained at least one positive.
- **Undersampling negatives.** A random subset of negatives is drawn in each epoch, which also shortens the epoch. The second-place team in ISIC 2024 undersampled negatives to ratios of 1:3 or 1:5.
- **Balanced batches.** The ISIC 2024 winner trained its image models on batches with equal numbers of positives and negatives, and used their predictions as input features to a gradient-boosted model.

### Correcting the prior after resampling

Resampling changes the class prior that the model learns but not the appearance of each class. Suppose each positive is included in training with relative rate $r_1$ and each negative with relative rate $r_0$. The class-conditional densities $P(x \mid y)$ are unchanged, and the class probabilities become proportional to $r_1 \pi$ and $r_0(1-\pi)$.

By Bayes' rule, the odds of the positive class given $x$ under the original distribution are

$$
O(x) = \frac{P(y=1 \mid x)}{P(y=0 \mid x)} = \frac{P(x \mid y=1)\,\pi}{P(x \mid y=0)\,(1-\pi)} .
$$

Under the resampled distribution the priors are multiplied by $r_1$ and $r_0$, so

$$
O_s(x) = \frac{P(x \mid y=1)\,r_1\pi}{P(x \mid y=0)\,r_0(1-\pi)} = \frac{r_1}{r_0}\,O(x).
$$

A model trained on the resampled data estimates $O_s(x)$. The odds under the original distribution are recovered as

$$
O(x) = \frac{r_0}{r_1}\,O_s(x), \qquad p = \frac{O(x)}{1 + O(x)} .
$$

Worked example: with $\pi = 0.001$, balanced batches correspond to $r_1/r_0 = 999$. A prediction $p_s = 0.5$ has odds $O_s = 1$, so $O = 1/999$ and $p = 0.001$. A prediction $p_s = 0.9$ has $O_s = 9$, so $O = 9/999 = 0.00901$ and $p = 0.00893$.

On the logit scale the correction subtracts the constant $\log(r_1/r_0)$ from every prediction. The transformation is strictly increasing, so it does not change the ranking of the inputs, and ROC AUC is unaffected. Log loss and other metrics that use the probabilities themselves require the correction.
