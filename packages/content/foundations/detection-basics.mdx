### Boxes and intersection over union

A box detector outputs, for many candidate positions, a class probability and box coordinates. For two axis-aligned boxes $A = [x_1, x_2] \times [y_1, y_2]$ and $B = [x_1', x_2'] \times [y_1', y_2']$, the overlap has width and height

$$
w = \max\big(0,\ \min(x_2, x_2') - \max(x_1, x_1')\big), \qquad
h = \max\big(0,\ \min(y_2, y_2') - \max(y_1, y_1')\big),
$$

and the **intersection over union** is

$$
\mathrm{IoU}(A, B) = \frac{wh}{|A| + |B| - wh},
$$

where $|A|$ and $|B|$ are the box areas. A prediction is counted as matching a true box if their IoU exceeds a threshold, typically 0.5.

**Example.** Two $10 \times 10$ boxes offset by 5 pixels horizontally overlap in a $5 \times 10$ region: $\mathrm{IoU} = 50 / (100 + 100 - 50) = 1/3$.

### Non-maximum suppression and box fusion

Neighbouring positions of a detector respond to the same object. **Non-maximum suppression (NMS)** removes these duplicates:

1. Sort the predictions by confidence.
2. Keep the most confident remaining prediction.
3. Delete every remaining prediction whose IoU with it exceeds a threshold.
4. Repeat from step 2 until no predictions remain.

**Weighted box fusion (WBF)** instead replaces each cluster of overlapping boxes by their confidence-weighted average. It is used to combine the outputs of several models, where boxes predicted by several models merge into one box.

### Heatmap-based point detection

When all objects of a class have about the same size, as with protein complexes in a tomogram, a box adds little information to its centre. The model can instead predict, at every voxel $\mathbf{x}$, the probability that an object centre lies there. Training targets are either single voxels at the centres or Gaussian blobs around each centre $\mathbf{c}$,

$$
H(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{c} \rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
$$

with $\sigma$ related to the object size. At inference the detections are the local maxima of the predicted map. A voxel is a local maximum if it equals the maximum of the map over a window around it, which a max-pooling operation computes for all voxels at once and which serves as NMS.

### Offsets and point similarity

A detector that predicts on a grid coarser than the input (for example at half resolution) can still localise precisely if it also predicts, in each output cell, the offset from the cell centre to the object centre.

Matching predicted points to true points requires a similarity between two points in place of box IoU. The CZII cryo-ET winners used the object keypoint similarity from human-pose estimation,

$$
\mathrm{OKS}(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{g}) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{g} \rVert^2}{2r^2}\right),
$$

where $r$ is the particle radius. It equals 1 at the true centre. At a distance of half a radius it equals $e^{-0.25/2} = e^{-1/8} \approx 0.88$; a prediction within half a radius of the true centre was counted as correct by the competition metric, so an OKS threshold of 0.88 reproduces that rule.
