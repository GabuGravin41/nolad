A **backbone** is the part of an image network that converts an input image into feature maps. A task-specific **head** is attached to it: a pooling layer and a linear layer for classification, or a decoder for segmentation. Most solutions in the reports use a published backbone with published pretrained weights. The architectures below are built from convolutional layers, described in the foundation on convolutions.

### Residual connections (ResNet)

In a plain deep network, each block computes $y = F(x)$ from its input $x$. He et al. (2016) observed that very deep plain networks trained worse than shallower ones, and added the input of each block to its output:

$$
y = x + F(x),
$$

where $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$ have the same shape and $F$ is a few convolutional layers. The connection that carries $x$ past $F$ is the **residual** or **skip connection**.

**Effect on the gradient.** Let $\mathcal{L}$ be the loss. By the chain rule, with $J_F = \partial F/\partial x$ the Jacobian of the block and $I$ the identity,

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y}\,\big(I + J_F\big) = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y}\,J_F .
$$

The first term passes the gradient through the block unchanged. Through a stack of $B$ blocks the gradient is multiplied by the product $\prod_{b}(I + J_{F_b})$, whose expansion contains the identity, so there is always a path along which the gradient is not attenuated by the blocks. In a plain network the product is $\prod_b J_{F_b}$, which can shrink towards zero as $B$ grows. A block that has nothing useful to add can learn $F \approx 0$ and pass its input on unchanged.

He et al. trained residual networks with 50, 101 and 152 layers. ResNet-50 has about 25.6 million parameters.

### Normalisation layers

A normalisation layer rescales activations to zero mean and unit variance over some set of values, then applies a learned scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$. For a set of values $x_1, \dots, x_M$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$,

$$
\hat x_m = \frac{x_m-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}, \qquad y_m = \gamma\,\hat x_m + \beta ,
$$

with a small $\epsilon > 0$. For example, the values $1, 2, 3, 6$ have mean 3 and variance $(4+1+0+9)/4 = 3.5$, so with $\epsilon \approx 0$ they normalise to $-1.07,\ -0.53,\ 0,\ 1.60$. The normalisation layers differ in which values form the set.

- **Batch normalisation** uses, for each channel, all values of that channel in the current mini-batch: $B \times H \times W$ values for a batch of $B$ feature maps of size $H \times W$. At inference it uses running averages of $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ collected during training. When $B$ is small, the batch statistics are noisy estimates.
- **Group normalisation** divides the $C$ channels of one example into $G$ groups and normalises each group over its channels and spatial positions. It does not depend on the batch.
- **Layer normalisation** normalises over all channels of one example (or, in transformers and ConvNeXt, over the channels at each spatial position).

The HuBMAP winner trained with a batch size of 1 and group normalisation with 32 groups in the decoder, and reports that this worked better than a batch size of 4 with batch normalisation.

### Squeeze-and-excitation (SE)

An **SE block** (Hu et al., 2018) rescales the channels of a feature map $x \in \mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$ by weights computed from the map itself.

1. **Squeeze.** Average each channel over space: $\bar x_c = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i,j} x_c[i,j]$, giving $\bar x \in \mathbb{R}^C$.
2. **Excitation.** Pass $\bar x$ through two linear layers, $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{(C/r)\times C}$ and $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{C\times (C/r)}$, with a reduction ratio $r$, a ReLU between them and a sigmoid $\sigma$ at the end:

$$
s = \sigma\big(W_2\,\mathrm{ReLU}(W_1\,\bar x)\big) \in (0,1)^C .
$$

3. **Scale.** Multiply each channel by its weight: $y_c = s_c\,x_c$.

The block adds $2C^2/r$ weights. With $C = 256$ and $r = 16$, the ratio used by Hu et al., this is $2 \times 65{,}536/16 = 8{,}192$. **SE-ResNeXt** networks combine SE blocks with ResNeXt blocks, which use grouped convolutions; SE-ResNeXt-50 appears in the 2019 pneumothorax and 2020 pulmonary embolism solutions.

### EfficientNet

A network can be enlarged by adding layers (depth), channels (width) or input pixels (resolution). Tan and Le (2019) scaled all three together with a single coefficient $\phi \ge 0$:

$$
\text{depth} \propto \alpha^{\phi},\qquad \text{width} \propto \beta^{\phi},\qquad \text{resolution} \propto \gamma^{\phi},
$$

with $\alpha = 1.2$, $\beta = 1.1$ and $\gamma = 1.15$ found by a small search.

**Why the constraint $\alpha\beta^2\gamma^2 \approx 2$.** The cost of a convolutional layer is proportional to $C_{\mathrm{in}}\,C_{\mathrm{out}}\,H\,W$. Multiplying the width by $\beta$ multiplies both $C_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{out}}$ by $\beta$, so the cost grows by $\beta^2$. Multiplying the resolution by $\gamma$ multiplies $H$ and $W$ by $\gamma$, a factor $\gamma^2$. Multiplying the depth by $\alpha$ multiplies the number of layers by $\alpha$. The total cost therefore grows by $(\alpha\beta^2\gamma^2)^{\phi}$ per unit of $\phi$, and

$$
\alpha\beta^2\gamma^2 = 1.2 \times 1.21 \times 1.3225 \approx 1.92 \approx 2,
$$

so each unit increase of $\phi$ roughly doubles the computation. The family runs from EfficientNet-B0 (about 5.3 million parameters) to B7 (about 66 million). Its building block is the inverted residual block of MobileNetV2 with a depthwise convolution and an SE block.

The BirdCLEF 2024 competition allowed only CPU inference within 120 minutes, and the winning team used EfficientNet-B0 and RegNetY-800MF, which are small enough for that budget.

### ConvNeXt

Liu et al. (2022) modified the ResNet design using choices taken from vision transformers: $7\times7$ depthwise convolutions, layer normalisation, the GELU activation, fewer normalisation and activation layers, and an inverted bottleneck in which the channel count expands by 4 inside each block.

A **depthwise convolution** applies one $k\times k$ filter to each channel separately, without mixing channels. On $C$ channels it has $k^2C$ weights, compared with $k^2C^2$ for a standard convolution from $C$ to $C$ channels. With $C = 96$, a $7\times7$ depthwise layer has $49 \times 96 = 4{,}704$ weights and a standard $3\times3$ layer has $9 \times 96^2 = 82{,}944$. Channel mixing is done by the $1\times1$ convolutions that follow.

ConvNeXt models appear in several winning solutions: ConvNeXt-Small in the mammography and lumbar spine winners, and ConvNeXt-Atto among the HMS winner's models. The lumbar spine winners report that larger ConvNeXt variants performed worse than ConvNeXt-Small, and the HMS winning team report that smaller models performed better in their experiments.

### Pretraining

Backbones are usually initialised from weights trained on ImageNet-1k (about 1.28 million training photographs in 1,000 classes) or on the larger ImageNet-21k (about 14 million images in about 21,000 classes). Medical images differ from photographs, but the early layers of an ImageNet-trained network detect edges, textures and simple shapes that occur in both, and fine-tuning from these weights usually converges faster and generalises better on small datasets than training from random initialisation. The foundation on transfer learning describes this in more detail.

Pretrained weights carry licences, and a licence restriction applies to every model fine-tuned from those weights. In the HuBMAP competition the pretrained SegFormer weights used by the winner were licensed for non-commercial use only.
