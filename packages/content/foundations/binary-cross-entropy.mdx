### From a score to a probability

For a binary label $y \in \{0, 1\}$, the last layer of a network produces a real number $z \in \mathbb{R}$, the **logit**. The **sigmoid** function maps it to a probability $p \in (0,1)$:

$$
p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} .
$$

Solving for $z$ gives the inverse,

$$
z = \log\frac{p}{1-p},
$$

the logarithm of the odds $p/(1-p)$. A logit of 0 corresponds to $p = 0.5$, a logit of 2 to $p = 1/(1+e^{-2}) \approx 0.881$, and a logit of $-2$ to $p \approx 0.119$.

<Plot series={[{fn: "sigmoid", label: "σ(z)"}]} x={[-8, 8]} y={[0, 1]} xLabel="logit z" yLabel="probability p" markers={[{x: 0, label: "p = 0.5"}]} />

The derivative of the sigmoid, used below, is

$$
\sigma'(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2} = \sigma(z)\,\big(1-\sigma(z)\big) .
$$

### From likelihood to loss

Model the label as a Bernoulli random variable that equals 1 with probability $p$. The probability of the observed value $y$ is

$$
P(y \mid p) = p^{\,y}(1-p)^{1-y},
$$

which equals $p$ when $y=1$ and $1-p$ when $y=0$. For $N$ independent examples with labels $y_i$ and predicted probabilities $p_i$, the likelihood of all the labels is the product

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{N} p_i^{\,y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i} .
$$

The logarithm is increasing, so the log-likelihood has the same maximiser as the likelihood, and it turns the product into a sum:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\,y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big] .
$$

Maximising this is the same as minimising its negative. Dividing by $N$ gives the mean loss per example:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\,y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big].
$$

This is **binary cross-entropy (BCE)**, also called **log loss**. Logarithms are natural logarithms throughout.

For a positive example ($y = 1$) the loss is $-\log p$:

| Predicted $p$ | Loss $-\log p$ |
| --- | --- |
| 0.99 | 0.010 |
| 0.9 | 0.105 |
| 0.5 | 0.693 |
| 0.1 | 2.303 |
| 0.001 | 6.908 |

One positive example predicted at $p = 0.001$ costs as much as $6.908/0.105 \approx 66$ positive examples predicted at $p = 0.9$.

### The minimiser is the true frequency

Consider many examples that the model cannot distinguish, of which a fraction $\pi \in (0,1)$ are positive. If the model predicts the same probability $q \in (0,1)$ for all of them, the mean loss is

$$
L(q) = -\big[\pi\log q + (1-\pi)\log(1-q)\big].
$$

Its derivative is

$$
L'(q) = -\frac{\pi}{q} + \frac{1-\pi}{1-q} .
$$

Setting $L'(q) = 0$ and multiplying by $q(1-q)$ gives $-\pi(1-q) + (1-\pi)q = 0$, which simplifies to $q = \pi$. The second derivative, $\pi/q^2 + (1-\pi)/(1-q)^2$, is positive, so this is the minimum.

For $\pi = 0.2$:

- $L(0.2) = -[0.2\log 0.2 + 0.8\log 0.8] = 0.322 + 0.179 = 0.500$,
- $L(0.1) = -[0.2\log 0.1 + 0.8\log 0.9] = 0.461 + 0.084 = 0.545$,
- $L(0.5) = \log 2 = 0.693$.

A loss that is minimised in expectation by the true probability is called a **proper scoring rule**. Training with log loss therefore pushes outputs towards calibrated probabilities.

Log loss penalises overconfidence heavily, and competitions scored by it reward measures that keep predictions away from 0 and 1. Clipping predictions to $[0.01, 0.99]$ bounds the loss of any single example by $-\log 0.01 = 4.61$, at a cost of $-\log 0.99 = 0.010$ on each example that would otherwise have been predicted with certainty. Averaging the predictions of several models also helps: $-\log$ is convex, so by Jensen's inequality the loss of an averaged prediction is at most the average of the individual losses.

### The gradient with respect to the logit

For one example, write the loss as a function of the logit, $\ell(z) = -[\,y\log\sigma(z) + (1-y)\log(1-\sigma(z))\,]$. Using $\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)$,

$$
\frac{d}{dz}\log\sigma(z) = 1-\sigma(z), \qquad \frac{d}{dz}\log\big(1-\sigma(z)\big) = -\sigma(z) .
$$

Substituting these,

$$
\frac{d\ell}{dz} = -\big[\,y\,(1-\sigma(z)) - (1-y)\,\sigma(z)\,\big] .
$$

Expanding the bracket, the terms $y\,\sigma(z)$ cancel:

$$
\frac{d\ell}{dz} = \sigma(z) - y = p - y .
$$

The gradient is the prediction error. It is bounded between $-1$ and $1$, and it is largest when the prediction is confidently wrong.

### Numerically stable computation

Computing $\sigma(z)$ first and then taking logarithms fails for large $|z|$. For $z = 20$, the exact value is $1 - p \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-9}$, but $p$ rounds to exactly 1 in 32-bit arithmetic, so for $y = 0$ the computed loss $-\log(1-p)$ is infinite, although the true loss is about 20. The loss can instead be written directly in terms of $z$. From $\log\sigma(z) = -\log(1+e^{-z})$ and $\log(1-\sigma(z)) = -z - \log(1+e^{-z})$,

$$
\ell(z) = (1-y)\,z + \log\big(1+e^{-z}\big) .
$$

For $z < 0$ the exponential $e^{-z}$ can overflow. Rewriting $\log(1+e^{-z}) = -z + \log(1+e^{z})$ for negative $z$ gives a single expression valid for all $z$:

$$
\ell(z) = \max(z, 0) - y\,z + \log\big(1+e^{-|z|}\big) ,
$$

in which the exponential never exceeds 1. PyTorch's `BCEWithLogitsLoss` computes this form, which is why solutions pass raw logits to the loss rather than probabilities.

### Multi-label problems

When several labels can be true at once, each label $j$ receives its own logit $z_j$, its own sigmoid and its own binary cross-entropy, and the losses are summed or averaged over labels. A head CT slice can show both subdural and subarachnoid haemorrhage, and the aneurysm competition had 14 binary labels per series. This differs from multi-class classification, where exactly one class is true and a softmax is used.

### Use in the reports

Binary cross-entropy is the standard training loss for binary and multi-label outputs in the reports. Several RSNA competitions also used a weighted version of log loss as the leaderboard metric, described in the foundation on weighted log loss.
