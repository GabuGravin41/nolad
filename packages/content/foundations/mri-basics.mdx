### The signal

Hydrogen nuclei (mostly in water and fat) align with a strong magnetic field. A radio-frequency pulse tips them; as they relax back, they emit a signal. Two relaxation times characterise each tissue:

- **T1**, the recovery of alignment along the field, is short in fat and long in water.
- **T2**, the decay of the transverse signal, is long in water and fluid and shorter in most solid tissue.

A pulse sequence sets timing parameters, the repetition time $\mathrm{TR}$ and the echo time $\mathrm{TE}$, that make the image intensity depend mainly on one of these. For a spin-echo sequence the signal of a tissue is approximately

$$
S \approx \rho\,\big(1 - e^{-\mathrm{TR}/T_1}\big)\,e^{-\mathrm{TE}/T_2},
$$

where $\rho$ is the proton density. A short $\mathrm{TR}$ and short $\mathrm{TE}$ make the first factor differ strongly between tissues and the second close to 1 (T1 weighting); a long $\mathrm{TR}$ and long $\mathrm{TE}$ make the first factor close to 1 and the second differ (T2 weighting).

**Example.** With $\mathrm{TR} = 500$ ms and $\mathrm{TE} = 15$ ms, and approximate values at 1.5 T of $T_1 = 250$ ms, $T_2 = 80$ ms for fat and $T_1 = 3000$ ms, $T_2 = 2000$ ms for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), the first factor is $1 - e^{-2} \approx 0.86$ for fat and $1 - e^{-1/6} \approx 0.15$ for CSF, while the second is $e^{-15/80} \approx 0.83$ and $e^{-15/2000} \approx 0.99$. Fat is therefore much brighter than CSF (T1 weighting). With $\mathrm{TR} = 4000$ ms and $\mathrm{TE} = 100$ ms, the first factors become about 1.0 and 0.74, and the second $e^{-100/80} \approx 0.29$ and $e^{-100/2000} \approx 0.95$: CSF is now brighter than fat (T2 weighting).

| Sequence | Bright | Dark | Typical use in these reports |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T1-weighted | fat, contrast agent | fluid | spine anatomy, foramina (sagittal T1) |
| T2-weighted | fluid (CSF, oedema) | — | spinal canal, discs (sagittal and axial T2) |
| STIR | fluid, oedema (fat suppressed) | fat | spine |
| T1 post-contrast | enhancing tissue, vessels | — | aneurysm detection |
| MR angiography (time of flight) | flowing blood | static tissue | aneurysm detection |

MRI intensities have no absolute scale, unlike CT values in Hounsfield units. The same tissue can have different values on different scanners and in different parts of one image. Solutions therefore normalise each volume, for example to zero mean and unit variance or to the range 0–1, instead of applying fixed windows.

### Planes and series

Studies contain several series acquired in different planes: **sagittal** (side view, splitting left from right), **axial** (horizontal slices) and **coronal** (front view). In the lumbar spine competition, canal stenosis is best seen on sagittal T2, foraminal narrowing on sagittal T1, and subarticular stenosis on axial T2. The winners built separate localisation stages to find the relevant slices and positions in each series.

### Practical difficulties

Slice spacing, orientation and field of view vary between scanners; some series are stored as multi-frame DICOM files; slices can be missing. The top aneurysm solutions included extensive preprocessing to load every test series, and the winning pipeline returned average predictions for any series it could not process.
