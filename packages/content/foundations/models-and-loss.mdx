### Models and parameters

A **model** is a function $f_\theta$ that maps an input $x$ (an image, a signal, a sequence) to an output $\hat y = f_\theta(x)$ (a probability, a mask, a number). The vector $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{P}$ collects the model's **parameters**, that is, every weight and bias it contains. Training changes $\theta$ and nothing else. A small convolutional network has a few million parameters; the language model in the AIMO report has about fourteen billion.

### Loss functions and empirical risk

A **loss function** $\ell(\hat y, y) \ge 0$ measures the error of a prediction $\hat y$ against the correct answer $y$. It is small for good predictions and large for bad ones. Two examples:

$$
\ell_{\mathrm{sq}}(\hat y, y) = (\hat y - y)^2, \qquad
\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\hat p, y) = -\big[\,y \log \hat p + (1 - y)\log(1 - \hat p)\,\big],
$$

the squared error for a real-valued target and the binary cross-entropy for a label $y \in \{0, 1\}$ and a predicted probability $\hat p \in (0, 1)$.

Given a training set $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, the **empirical risk** is the average loss,

$$
\hat R_N(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\big(f_\theta(x_i), y_i\big),
$$

and training searches for parameters that make it small:

$$
\theta^\star = \arg\min_{\theta}\ \hat R_N(\theta).
$$

This procedure is called empirical risk minimisation.

### Expected risk and overfitting

The quantity of interest is the loss on new cases drawn from the distribution $\mathcal{D}$ that produces real inputs and labels, the **expected risk**

$$
R(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \mathcal{D}}\ \ell\big(f_\theta(x), y\big).
$$

For fixed $\theta$ and training examples drawn independently from $\mathcal{D}$, $\hat R_N(\theta)$ is an unbiased estimate of $R(\theta)$: taking expectations term by term,

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\hat R_N(\theta)\big] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{E}\,\ell\big(f_\theta(x_i), y_i\big) = \frac{1}{N}\cdot N \cdot R(\theta) = R(\theta).
$$

This no longer holds for $\theta^\star$, because $\theta^\star$ was chosen using the same training examples. Typically $\hat R_N(\theta^\star) < R(\theta^\star)$. When the gap is large the model has **overfitted**: it fits properties of the training examples that do not recur in new data. The expected risk is therefore estimated on held-out data that were not used to choose $\theta$ (see the cross-validation primer), and the estimate is valid only if the held-out data come from the same distribution as the cases the model will be used on.

### Loss, metric and decision rule

Three quantities are often confused:

- The **loss** is minimised by the optimiser. It must be differentiable with respect to $\theta$ almost everywhere, because training uses gradients.
- The **metric** is the score reported by a competition or used in practice. Many metrics (accuracy, AUC, F-scores, kappa) are piecewise constant in $\theta$, so their gradient is zero almost everywhere and they cannot be minimised by gradient descent. A loss is chosen whose minimiser is close to the maximiser of the metric.
- The **decision rule** converts a model output into an action, for example "flag the scan if $\hat p > 0.3$". The threshold is usually chosen after training, on held-out data.

### Probabilistic outputs

Most models in these reports output probabilities. A probability can be used for ranking (AUC), thresholded later (F-scores), averaged over models (ensembles) and calibrated. Losses derived from the likelihood of the data train models whose outputs estimate conditional probabilities. For the binary cross-entropy, the expected loss at an input $x$ with $q = P(y = 1 \mid x)$ is

$$
L(\hat p) = -\big[\,q \log \hat p + (1 - q)\log(1 - \hat p)\,\big],
\qquad
L'(\hat p) = -\frac{q}{\hat p} + \frac{1 - q}{1 - \hat p},
$$

which is zero at $\hat p = q$; since $L''(\hat p) = q/\hat p^2 + (1-q)/(1-\hat p)^2 > 0$, this is the minimum. A model that minimises the expected cross-entropy therefore outputs $\hat p = P(y = 1 \mid x)$, provided the model class can represent that function.

### Measures against overfitting

The solutions in these reports use several measures to keep the gap between $\hat R_N$ and $R$ small and to estimate $R$ reliably:

- **Held-out validation.** The metric is measured on data never used for training. Splits are made by patient, subject or experiment instead of by image or sample, so that near-duplicate examples do not appear on both sides of the split.
- **Regularisation.** Data augmentation, dropout, weight decay and early stopping restrict how closely the model can fit individual training examples.
- **Ensembling.** Averaging models trained with different seeds, data splits or architectures reduces the variance of the prediction, because their errors are partly independent.
