title: "Learning-rate schedules, warm-up and cosine decay"
kind: ml
summary: "The learning rate is usually varied during training, rising briefly at the start and falling towards zero at the end. The shape of that curve affects final accuracy."
---

A constant learning rate is rarely best. Large steps early make fast progress; small steps late let the parameters settle into a good region instead of bouncing around it. Three shapes recur in the winning solutions.

**Linear warm-up.** Start from a tiny rate and increase linearly to the peak over the first few hundred steps or epochs. At initialisation, Adam's second-moment estimates are unreliable and gradients can be large; warm-up avoids early steps that wreck pretrained weights.

**Cosine decay.** After warm-up, follow half a cosine from the peak $\eta_{\max}$ to a floor $\eta_{\min}$:

$$
\eta(t) = \eta_{\min} + \tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\left(1+\cos\frac{\pi\,(t - t_w)}{T - t_w}\right), \qquad t_w \le t \le T.
$$

The curve is flat near the peak, falls fastest in the middle, and flattens again near the end.

**One-cycle.** Rise to the peak over a fixed fraction of training (for example the first 5%) and then anneal to nearly zero. The Ribonanza winners used this with a peak of $2.5\times10^{-3}$.

Reduce-on-plateau schedules, which lower the rate when the validation score stops improving, appear in older solutions such as the 2019 pneumothorax winner.

The peak learning rate interacts with batch size: larger batches give less noisy gradients and usually tolerate larger rates. When a report quotes "lr = 1e-3 with an effective batch of 32", both numbers matter for reproducing the result.
