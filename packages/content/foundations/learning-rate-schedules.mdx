### Why the learning rate is varied

In gradient descent the parameters are updated as $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta_t\, g_t$, where $g_t$ is the (stochastic) gradient and $\eta_t$ the learning rate at step $t$. Large steps reduce the loss quickly at the start of training. Near the end, the gradient noise makes the parameters move around a region of low loss with an amplitude that grows with $\eta_t$; reducing $\eta_t$ reduces that amplitude. Most schedules therefore start low, rise to a peak and decrease towards zero.

### Linear warm-up

Over the first $t_w$ steps the learning rate rises linearly from near zero to its peak $\eta_{\max}$:

$$
\eta(t) = \eta_{\max}\,\frac{t}{t_w}, \qquad 0 \le t \le t_w .
$$

At the start of training, the second-moment estimates of Adam are based on few gradients, and gradients of a freshly attached output layer can be large. Small initial steps avoid large changes to pretrained weights before these estimates stabilise.

### Cosine decay

After warm-up, the rate follows half a period of a cosine from $\eta_{\max}$ to a floor $\eta_{\min}$ at the final step $T$:

$$
\eta(t) = \eta_{\min} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min})\left(1 + \cos\frac{\pi\,(t - t_w)}{T - t_w}\right), \qquad t_w \le t \le T .
$$

At $t = t_w$ the cosine is 1 and $\eta = \eta_{\max}$; at $t = T$ it is $-1$ and $\eta = \eta_{\min}$; halfway, it is 0 and $\eta$ is the midpoint of the two. The derivative,

$$
\eta'(t) = -\frac{\pi\,(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min})}{2\,(T - t_w)}\,\sin\frac{\pi\,(t - t_w)}{T - t_w},
$$

is zero at both ends and largest in magnitude halfway, so the rate stays near its peak for a while, falls fastest in the middle of training and levels off at the end.

### One-cycle schedule

The one-cycle schedule rises to the peak over a fixed fraction of training (for example the first 5%) and then decreases to nearly zero, usually along a cosine. The winners of Stanford Ribonanza used it with a peak learning rate of $2.5 \times 10^{-3}$.

### Reduction on plateau

A reduce-on-plateau schedule multiplies the learning rate by a factor (for example 0.5) when the validation score has not improved for a set number of epochs. It appears in older solutions, such as the 2019 pneumothorax winner.

### Learning rate and batch size

With batch size $B$, the mini-batch gradient averages $B$ per-example gradients, and its variance decreases approximately as $1/B$. Larger batches therefore tolerate larger learning rates; a common heuristic scales the learning rate linearly with $B$ over a moderate range. Reproducing a reported result requires both numbers, for example a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ at an effective batch size of 32. The effective batch size includes gradient accumulation: $B_{\mathrm{eff}} = B \times (\text{accumulation steps}) \times (\text{number of GPUs})$.
