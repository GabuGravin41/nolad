### K-fold cross-validation

The training data are split into $K$ disjoint folds $F_1, \dots, F_K$ (commonly $K = 4$ or 5). For each $k$, a model $f^{(-k)}$ is trained on all folds except $F_k$ and evaluated on $F_k$. Every example $i$ then has exactly one **out-of-fold (OOF)** prediction,

$$
\hat y_i^{\mathrm{OOF}} = f^{(-k(i))}(x_i),
$$

where $k(i)$ is the fold containing $i$, from a model that did not see it during training. The metric computed on all OOF predictions estimates performance on new data. The OOF predictions are also used to train second-stage models (stacking), to choose thresholds and to detect label errors.

### Grouped splits

If a patient contributes several images and some of them fall in the training folds and others in the validation fold, the model can score well by recognising the patient instead of the disease. The validation score then overestimates performance on new patients and becomes unreliable for choosing between models. The medical solutions in these reports split by patient (`GroupKFold`); other domains use the corresponding unit: signer for sign language, experiment for tomograms, building block for chemistry, sequence cluster for RNA.

**Stratification** additionally keeps the class balance equal across folds, which matters when positives are rare. The "triple stratified leak-free" folds used in both ISIC competitions group by patient and stratify by both the target and the number of images per patient.

### Duplicates

Datasets assembled from several sources contain near-duplicates. PANDA solutions used perceptual image hashes to find duplicate biopsies and kept each group of duplicates within one fold. The winners note that their label-denoising method depends on this: if a duplicate were in a training fold, the out-of-fold prediction for its copy would reproduce the copy's label, including a wrong one.

### The public leaderboard

Kaggle scores submissions on a public subset of the test set during the competition and on a private subset at the end. When the public subset is small, its score is noisy, and choosing models by it selects models that fit its noise. For a metric that averages $n$ independent per-example scores with standard deviation $\sigma$, the standard error of the average is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. For an accuracy of 0.65 on $n = 50$ problems, $\sigma = \sqrt{0.65 \times 0.35} \approx 0.48$ and the standard error is $0.48/\sqrt{50} \approx 0.067$, about 3.4 problems. Examples in these reports:

- **Tiny test sets:** 50 problems in AIMO, about 500 slides in each PANDA split, around 10,000 skin lesions with a few dozen melanomas in SIIM-ISIC 2020.
- **Large changes between public and private rankings**, in which teams that selected models by public score dropped many places.

Measures used by the winners: relying on cross-validation when it is stable; comparing models over pairs of seeds; limiting the number of settings evaluated on the leaderboard; paired statistical tests over repeated cross-validation (the ISIC 2024 winner accepted a change only if a paired t-test over 10 repetitions supported it); and setting predictions for test rows that duplicate training data to zero before comparing submissions (Ribonanza).
