### From tissue to image

A tissue sample, such as a biopsy core or a surgical resection, is fixed, embedded in paraffin, cut into sections a few micrometres thick and stained. **Haematoxylin** stains nuclei blue-purple and **eosin** stains cytoplasm and extracellular matrix pink (H&E staining). The slide is scanned at 20× or 40× magnification, about 0.5 or 0.25 µm per pixel.

At 0.25 µm per pixel, a tissue section of 25 mm × 25 mm occupies

$$
\frac{25\,000\ \mu\text{m}}{0.25\ \mu\text{m}} = 100\,000 \text{ pixels}
$$

along each side, or $10^{10}$ pixels in total; at three bytes per pixel, 30 GB uncompressed.

Scanners store **whole-slide images (WSIs)** as pyramids (in formats such as SVS, NDPI and TIFF): the full-resolution image and successively downsampled levels, so that software can read any region at any magnification without loading the whole file. When a competition releases slides as single flat PNG files, as UBC-OCEAN did, decoding a whole image can exhaust memory; the UBC-OCEAN winners wrote C code that reads a PNG row by row.

**Tissue microarrays (TMAs)** contain small cores of tissue from many patients on one slide.

### Tiling

1. Detect tissue on a low-resolution thumbnail, usually by thresholding the saturation channel with Otsu's method, which separates stained tissue from the white background.
2. Divide the tissue region into square tiles, for example of 224 or 256 pixels, at a fixed magnification.
3. Keep a subset of tiles, such as the $k$ tiles with the most tissue or a random sample, to fit the computing budget.

At 0.5 µm per pixel, a 256-pixel tile covers $128 \times 128$ µm, and a tissue region of 10 mm × 10 mm contains about $(10\,000 / 128)^2 \approx 6\,100$ tiles.

The magnification must be the same for all tiles: at equal pixel size, a 40× tile shows a quarter of the tissue area of a 20× tile, with cells twice as large in pixels. The UBC-OCEAN winners resized 40× TMA images to match 20× whole-slide tiles and describe magnification as key information.

### Stain variation

The same tissue stained in two laboratories can differ considerably in colour. Two approaches reduce the effect: colour augmentation during training and stain normalisation, which maps images to a reference stain (methods of Reinhard, Macenko and Vahadane; see the HuBMAP report for the equations). The results differ between competitions: the fourth-place team in HuBMAP reports stain normalisation as its main improvement, while the UBC-OCEAN winners saw no gain from it on top of a pathology foundation model.

### Grading and subtyping

Pathologists grade tumours by tissue architecture and cell morphology. In prostate cancer, Gleason patterns 3, 4 and 5 describe increasingly disorganised glands; the two most prevalent patterns form the Gleason score (for example 3 + 4), which maps to an ISUP grade group from 1 to 5. Agreement between pathologists is imperfect, so training labels contain noise; this is a central topic of the PANDA report.
