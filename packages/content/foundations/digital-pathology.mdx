### From tissue to image

A tissue sample (a biopsy core, a resection) is fixed, embedded in paraffin, cut into sections a few micrometres thick and stained. **Haematoxylin** colours nuclei blue-purple; **eosin** colours cytoplasm and extracellular matrix pink. The H&E slide is scanned at 20× or 40× magnification, roughly 0.5 or 0.25 µm per pixel. A single slide can be 100,000 × 100,000 pixels.

Scanners store **whole-slide images (WSIs)** as pyramids (SVS, NDPI, TIFF): the full resolution plus successively downsampled levels, so software can read any region at any zoom without loading the whole file. When competitions release slides as flat PNGs, as UBC-OCEAN did, loading one can exhaust memory; the winners wrote C code that reads a PNG row by row.

**Tissue microarrays (TMAs)** are small cores from many patients arranged on one slide, often scanned at 40×.

### Tiling

1. Detect tissue on a low-resolution thumbnail, typically by Otsu thresholding the saturation channel, which separates stained tissue from white background.
2. Cut the tissue region into tiles (for example 224 or 256 pixels) at a fixed magnification.
3. Keep a subset (the top-$k$ by tissue content, or a random sample) to fit the compute budget.

Magnification must be consistent: a 20× tile and a 40× tile of the same size show different amounts of tissue and different cell sizes. The UBC-OCEAN winners resized 40× TMA tiles to match 20× WSI tiles and called magnification "key information".

### Stain variation

The same tissue stained in two laboratories can differ markedly in colour. Remedies include colour augmentation during training and stain normalisation (Reinhard, Macenko, Vahadane methods) that maps images to a reference stain. Results vary: the HuBMAP fourth-place team called stain normalisation decisive; the UBC-OCEAN winners saw no gain from it on top of a foundation model.

### Grading and subtyping

Pathologists grade by architecture and cell morphology. In prostate cancer, Gleason patterns 3, 4 and 5 describe increasingly disordered glands; the two most common patterns form the Gleason score (e.g. 3+4) and map to an ISUP grade group from 1 to 5. Agreement between pathologists is imperfect, so training labels are noisy, a central theme of the PANDA report.
