### Precision and recall

With $\mathrm{TP}$, $\mathrm{FP}$ and $\mathrm{FN}$ the numbers of true positives, false positives and false negatives,

$$
P = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}}, \qquad R = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}.
$$

Precision is the fraction of flagged cases that are positive; recall is the fraction of positive cases that are flagged. A screening test is usually operated at high recall (few missed cancers), and a test that leads to surgery at high precision (few unnecessary operations).

### The F-beta score

$$
F_\beta = \frac{(1+\beta^2)\,P\,R}{\beta^2 P + R}.
$$

$F_1$ is the harmonic mean of $P$ and $R$. Larger $\beta$ gives more weight to recall. Equivalently, $1/F_\beta$ is a weighted mean of $1/P$ and $1/R$:

$$
\frac{1}{F_\beta} = \frac{1}{1 + \beta^2}\cdot\frac{1}{P} + \frac{\beta^2}{1 + \beta^2}\cdot\frac{1}{R},
$$

as can be checked by putting the right-hand side over the common denominator $(1+\beta^2)PR$.

<Deep title="The relative cost of errors implied by β" kind="math">

Substitute $1/P = (\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP})/\mathrm{TP}$ and $1/R = (\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN})/\mathrm{TP}$ into the weighted mean above:

$$
\frac{1}{F_\beta} = \frac{(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}) + \beta^2(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN})}{(1 + \beta^2)\,\mathrm{TP}}.
$$

Inverting:

$$
F_\beta = \frac{(1+\beta^2)\,TP}{(1+\beta^2)\,TP + \beta^2 FN + FP}.
$$

In the denominator a false negative has weight $\beta^2$ and a false positive weight 1, so a false negative costs $\beta^2$ times as much as a false positive. With $\beta = 4$ (CZII cryo-ET), a missed particle costs 16 times as much as a spurious detection, and the optimal submission includes many uncertain detections. With $\beta = 0.5$ (Vesuvius ink detection), a false positive costs 4 times as much as a missed pixel; the organisers chose this weighting because spurious ink makes letters harder to read.

### Probabilistic F1

The RSNA mammography competition used a probabilistic F1 score in which a prediction $p_i$ on a positive case counts as $p_i$ true positives and on a negative case as $p_i$ false positives: $\mathrm{pTP} = \sum_{i: y_i = 1} p_i$ and $\mathrm{pFP} = \sum_{i: y_i = 0} p_i$, while the recall denominator is the number of positive cases. Small probabilities on the many negative cases add up to a large false-positive mass, so binarising the predictions at a suitable threshold usually scored higher than submitting probabilities. The winner's choice between thresholds 0.31 and 0.34 changed his private score by 0.02 (the breast cancer report gives the derivation).

### Average precision

Sort the predictions by decreasing confidence. With $P_k$ and $R_k$ the precision and recall of the first $k$ predictions,

$$
\mathrm{AP} = \sum_k (R_k - R_{k-1})\,P_k ,
$$

which approximates the area under the precision–recall curve. The terms with $R_k > R_{k-1}$ are those where prediction $k$ is a true positive, so AP is the mean of the precision values at the ranks of the true positives. For a random ranking the expected AP is close to the prevalence of positives, whereas the expected AUC is 0.5; AP is dominated by the correctness of the highest-ranked predictions. It is the metric of Leash BELKA (per protein and split) and, averaged over time tolerances, of the sleep-state competition. For detection tasks, mean average precision (mAP) averages AP over classes, with a prediction counted as correct only if it overlaps a true object sufficiently.

Several winners used the following property: appending predictions with lower confidence than all existing ones does not change any existing term $P_k$, because the order of the earlier predictions is unchanged, and it can only add terms for new true positives. Such predictions therefore cannot lower AP.
