### Next-token prediction

A decoder-only transformer assigns a probability to each possible next token given all previous tokens. By the chain rule of probability, the probability of a text $t_1, \dots, t_T$ factorises as

$$
P(t_1, \dots, t_T) = \prod_{k=1}^{T} P(t_k \mid t_1, \dots, t_{k-1}),
\qquad
\log P(t_1, \dots, t_T) = \sum_{k=1}^{T} \log P(t_k \mid t_1, \dots, t_{k-1}).
$$

Training maximises this log-likelihood over a corpus, which is the same as minimising the cross-entropy of each next token.

**Sampling and temperature.** Given logits $z_j$ for the vocabulary, the model samples the next token from

$$
p_j(\tau) = \frac{e^{z_j/\tau}}{\sum_{l} e^{z_l/\tau}},
$$

where $\tau > 0$ is the temperature. As $\tau \to 0$ the distribution concentrates on the token with the largest logit (greedy decoding); $\tau = 1$ gives the model's distribution; larger $\tau$ flattens it and gives more varied samples.

### Supervised fine-tuning on worked solutions

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) continues training a pretrained model on examples in the target format with the same next-token loss. A model fine-tuned on problems paired with step-by-step solutions (chain of thought) learns to write reasoning before the final answer. Longer reasoning costs more tokens, and therefore more time at inference. In the AIMO report, the NemoSkills team generated 3.2 million solutions with DeepSeek-R1 and QwQ-32B, kept those whose final answers were correct, and fine-tuned a 14-billion-parameter model on them.

### Tool-integrated reasoning

Arithmetic and enumeration of cases are error-prone when carried out in text. In **tool-integrated reasoning (TIR)** the model writes a block of code, generation pauses, a sandbox executes the code, and its output is appended to the context before generation resumes. The model can then compute sums exactly or test a conjecture on many cases.

### Majority voting, maj@k and pass@k

Suppose $k$ solutions are sampled independently, each correct with probability $\pi$.

**pass@k** is the probability that at least one of the $k$ is correct:

$$
\text{pass@}k = 1 - (1 - \pi)^{k}.
$$

For $\pi = 0.3$ and $k = 16$, $\text{pass@}16 = 1 - 0.7^{16} \approx 0.997$. It measures what a selector that always recognised the correct answer could achieve.

**maj@k** returns the most frequent final answer among the $k$ samples. If wrong answers are spread over many values, each individually less likely than the correct one, the correct answer is the most frequent one with probability approaching 1 as $k$ grows. With two outcomes only (the correct answer with probability $\pi$ and one wrong answer with probability $1 - \pi$) and odd $k$, the majority is correct with probability

$$
\sum_{j = (k+1)/2}^{k} \binom{k}{j}\, \pi^{j} (1 - \pi)^{k - j},
$$

which tends to 1 if $\pi > 1/2$ and to 0 if $\pi < 1/2$. In problems with many possible wrong answers, majority voting can succeed even when $\pi < 1/2$, as long as no single wrong answer is more likely than the correct one.

### Long contexts and model merging

Two further techniques appear in the AIMO report. Raising the base of the rotary position embedding (RoPE) lowers its rotation frequencies, which allows the model to be fine-tuned on reasoning traces of tens of thousands of tokens. **Model merging** interpolates the weights of two models fine-tuned from the same base model (see the weight-averaging primer).
