## Clinical background

The competition description cites the World Health Organization's estimate that low back pain affected 619 million people in 2020 and is the leading cause of disability worldwide. Much chronic back and leg pain is associated with degenerative changes of the lumbar spine, collectively called spondylosis. Intervertebral discs lose water and height and bulge, facet joints enlarge and ligaments thicken. These changes narrow the spinal canal, the subarticular recesses and the neural foramina through which the nerve roots run. A compressed nerve root can cause pain, numbness or weakness in the leg.

MRI shows the discs, the nerve roots and the spaces around them without ionising radiation. A radiologist examines each of the five lumbar disc levels, from L1/L2 to L5/S1, and reports for each level whether the spinal canal and the exits of the nerve roots are narrowed, and how severely. These grades inform decisions about physiotherapy, injections and surgery.

<Deep title="The three kinds of narrowing" kind="domain">

- **Spinal canal stenosis (SCS).** Narrowing of the central canal that contains the bundle of nerve roots. It is best seen on sagittal T2-weighted images (a side view in which fluid is bright), where the canal appears as a bright column that narrows at an affected level.
- **Neural foraminal narrowing (NFN)**, left and right. Each nerve root leaves the spine through an opening, the foramen, between adjacent vertebrae on each side. Narrowing is best seen on sagittal T1-weighted images taken off the midline, through the foramina.
- **Subarticular stenosis (SS)**, left and right. Narrowing of the lateral recess, the channel just medial to the foramen in which the nerve root descends. It is best seen on axial T2-weighted images at the level of the disc.

Each is graded normal/mild, moderate or severe. Five conditions (SCS, left and right NFN, left and right SS) at five levels give 25 grades per study.
</Deep>

## Data

The host collected studies from eight sites on five continents. Each study has three series: sagittal T1-weighted, sagittal T2-weighted or STIR, and axial T2-weighted. The labels are the 25 severity grades. For training studies only, a coordinates file (`label_coordinates.csv`) gives, for each labelled condition and level, the series, the slice (the DICOM instance number) and the $(x, y)$ pixel position that the annotating radiologist marked. The test studies come without coordinates, so a solution that uses the location of the finding has to predict it.

## Evaluation metric

Each scored row is one study, condition and level. The prediction for a row is a probability vector $q = (q_1, q_2, q_3)$ over the three grades, and the row's loss is the log loss $-\log q_{y}$ for true grade $y$. Each row is weighted by its true grade:

| True grade | Weight $w$ |
| --- | --- |
| Normal/mild | 1 |
| Moderate | 2 |
| Severe | 4 |

The metric also generates an "any severe spinal" target for each study, equal to 1 if any of the five spinal-canal levels is severe. Its prediction is the maximum predicted severe probability over the five levels, scored by a weighted binary log loss, with a scalar weight of 1.0 for the term as a whole. The final score is the average of the weighted log losses and this term. Rows for which the lowest vertebrae were not visible were not scored.

<Foundation id="weighted-log-loss" />

<Deep title="The prediction that minimises a class-weighted log loss" kind="math">

Consider a group of rows that a model cannot tell apart, in which the true grades occur with probabilities $\pi = (\pi_1, \pi_2, \pi_3)$, $\pi_k \ge 0$, $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$. Predicting the same vector $q$ for all of them gives an expected weighted loss proportional to

$$
L(q) = -\sum_{k=1}^{3} w_k\,\pi_k \log q_k ,
$$

where $w = (1, 2, 4)$. The normalising sum of weights in the metric depends only on the labels, so it does not affect which $q$ minimises the loss.

Minimise $L$ subject to $\sum_k q_k = 1$ with a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$. Setting the derivative with respect to $q_k$ to zero gives

$$
-\frac{w_k \pi_k}{q_k} + \lambda = 0 ,
$$

so $q_k = w_k \pi_k / \lambda$. Summing over $k$ and using $\sum_k q_k = 1$ gives $\lambda = \sum_j w_j \pi_j$. With the intermediate quantity $Z = \sum_j w_j \pi_j$,

$$
q_k^\star = \frac{w_k\,\pi_k}{Z} .
$$

Worked example: for $\pi = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1)$, the weighted products are $(0.7, 0.4, 0.4)$ and $Z = 1.5$, so

$$
q^\star = (0.467,\ 0.267,\ 0.267) .
$$

The optimal severe probability is 0.267, compared with a true frequency of 0.1. A model trained with unweighted cross-entropy estimates $\pi$, and the metric scores its predictions as too low for the rare grades. Solutions correct for this either by training with the metric's weights or by adjusting the predictions afterwards.
</Deep>

## Winning solution

The winner is NANACHI. It was the author's first published write-up, and every model was trained on Google Colab with a T4 GPU and the high-memory runtime. The author credits two community contributions as starting points: hengck23's public notebooks and discussion, which include a method for selecting axial slices, and a coordinate dataset by Brendan Artley, used to pretrain the coordinate models.

The solution has two stages. The first predicts, for test studies, the slices and coordinates that radiologists supplied for the training studies. The second grades crops taken around those predicted points.

### Stage 1a: slice selection

In a sagittal series the relevant slice depends on the condition (near the midline for the canal, lateral for the foramina) and on the level. A 3D ConvNeXt reads the whole series, with slices sorted by DICOM metadata, intensities scaled to $[0, 1]$ and the depth padded to 32 slices. For each level there are two heads:

- **Classification:** a softmax over the 32 slice indices, trained with cross-entropy.
- **Regression:** a vector $(x', y', z') = (x/W,\ y/H,\ z/32) \in [0,1]^3$, trained with L1 loss, where $W$ and $H$ are the image width and height and $z$ is the slice index. Only $z'$ is used; $x'$ and $y'$ are auxiliary targets.

Accuracy of the slice prediction on sagittal T2 images for the spinal canal:

| Head | Exact slice | Off by 1 | Off by 2 | Off by more than 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Classification | 71.08% | 27.04% | 1.43% | 0.44% |
| Regression | 67.48% | 30.59% | 1.61% | 0.31% |

For the classification head, $71.08 + 27.04 = 98.12\%$ of predictions are within one slice, and for the regression head $67.48 + 30.59 = 98.07\%$. The two heads were each trained on five folds, and the ten predictions for each level are combined by their median.

<Deep title="Classification and regression heads for the same slice index" kind="detail">

The cross-entropy loss of the classification head treats the 32 slice indices as unordered classes: predicting slice 13 when the answer is 12 costs as much as predicting slice 30. The L1 loss of the regression head uses the order of the slices, and its error grows with the distance from the correct slice. The two heads are trained with different losses, and the table shows that their error distributions differ: the classification head is more often exactly right, the regression head more often off by one.

The median is insensitive to a single estimate far from the others. Let $x_{(1)} \le \dots \le x_{(10)}$ be ten estimates sorted in increasing order. The median is $\tfrac12\big(x_{(5)} + x_{(6)}\big)$. If one estimate is replaced by an arbitrary value, the new median still lies between $\tfrac12\big(x_{(4)} + x_{(5)}\big)$ and $\tfrac12\big(x_{(6)} + x_{(7)}\big)$, whereas the mean moves by one tenth of the change in that estimate.
</Deep>

For axial series the author used hengck23's published method to select the slice for each level.

### Stage 1b: point localisation

A 2D model receives a three-channel image taken at the median of the predicted slices for the levels L1/L2 to L5/S1, resized to 512 × 512, and regresses $(x/W,\ y/H)$ for every level with L1 loss. ConvNeXt-Base and EfficientNetV2-L models were trained and their predictions averaged. Both were pretrained on Brendan Artley's landmark dataset, which the author reports gave slightly better models than ImageNet initialisation alone. Axial points were located with the same kind of model.

### Stage 2: cropping

Each slice is resized to 512 × 512. Around each predicted point, the author crops a fixed window from five consecutive slices centred on the predicted slice. The window extends by different numbers of pixels in each direction depending on the condition and series:

| Condition | Series | Left | Right | Up | Down |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Canal (SCS) | sagittal T2 | 96 | 32 | 40 | 40 |
| Canal (SCS) | axial T2 | 96 | 96 | 96 | 96 |
| Foramen (NFN) | sagittal T1 | 96 | 64 | 32 | 32 |
| Foramen, right | axial T2 | 144 | 48 | 96 | 96 |
| Foramen, left | axial T2 | 48 | 144 | 96 | 96 |
| Subarticular, right / left | axial T2 | 144 / 48 | 48 / 144 | 96 | 96 |

All values are in pixels of the 512 × 512 image. The sagittal canal window, for example, is $96 + 32 = 128$ pixels wide and $40 + 40 = 80$ pixels high. For the axial canal crop, the centre is taken from either the left or the right subarticular point, chosen at random, and shifted by ±20 pixels.

### Augmentation of the localisation errors

Before cropping, each training sample is shifted:

- the point by up to ±10 pixels in $x$ and in $y$;
- the slice index by up to ±2, with the probability of each shift set to the error rate measured for the stage-1 models.

At test time the grader receives crops chosen by the stage-1 models, which are one slice off in about 27–31% of cases according to the table above. With this augmentation the grader is trained on crops that are displaced in the same way and with the same frequency. The author describes this augmentation as crucial for robustness.

After cropping, the augmentations are random brightness and contrast (probability 0.25) and shift-scale-rotate (shift 0.1, scale ±0.1, rotation 20°, probability 0.5).

### Stage 3: grading

The five slices of a crop form a bag in the sense of multiple-instance learning. Each slice passes through a CNN encoder (ConvNeXt-Small or EfficientNetV2-S), which produces a feature vector of dimension $d$. A two-layer bidirectional LSTM reads the five vectors in slice order, so that the representation of each slice depends on its neighbours. Attention pooling then assigns a weight to each slice and forms the weighted sum of the slice representations, from which a linear head produces three logits.

<Foundation id="recurrent-networks" />

<Foundation id="multiple-instance-learning" />

<Deep title="Attention pooling" kind="math">

Let $h_1, \dots, h_5 \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ be the LSTM outputs for the five slices, and let $a \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}$ be the parameters of the attention layer. The score of slice $i$ is

$$
e_i = a^{\top} \tanh(h_i) + b ,
$$

the weights are the softmax of the scores,

$$
\alpha_i = \frac{\exp(e_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{5} \exp(e_j)} , \qquad \alpha_i > 0, \quad \sum_{i=1}^5 \alpha_i = 1 ,
$$

and the pooled vector is

$$
z = \sum_{i=1}^{5} \alpha_i\, h_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d} .
$$

This follows the attention-based MIL pooling of Ilse et al. (2018), in the simpler form without a hidden layer. If one slice has a score 2 higher than the other four, which are equal, its weight is $e^{2}/(e^{2} + 4) = 7.39/11.39 \approx 0.65$, and the other four share the remaining 0.35.
</Deep>

<Deep title="The LSTM and attention module in the write-up" kind="code">

```python title="LSTMMIL as published in the 1st-place write-up"
class LSTMMIL(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, input_dim):
        super(LSTMMIL, self).__init__()
        self.lstm = nn.LSTM(input_dim, input_dim//2, num_layers=2, batch_first=True,
                            dropout=0.1, bidirectional=True)
        self.aux_attention = nn.Sequential(nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(input_dim, 1))
        self.attention = nn.Sequential(nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(input_dim, 1))

    def forward(self, bags):
        batch_size, num_instances, input_dim = bags.size()
        bags_lstm, _ = self.lstm(bags)
        attn_scores = self.attention(bags_lstm).squeeze(-1)
        aux_attn_scores = self.aux_attention(bags_lstm).squeeze(-1)
        attn_weights = torch.softmax(attn_scores, dim=-1)
        weighted_instances = torch.bmm(attn_weights.unsqueeze(1), bags_lstm).squeeze(1)
        return weighted_instances, aux_attn_scores
```

- `bags` has shape `(batch, 5, d)`: five slice embeddings of dimension $d$.
- The LSTM has hidden size $d/2$ in each direction, so the concatenation of both directions has dimension $d$ again. It has two layers, with dropout 0.1 between them.
- `self.attention` applies $\tanh$ and a linear map to give one score per slice, the $e_i$ above. `softmax` over the five slices gives the weights $\alpha_i$.
- `torch.bmm` multiplies the weights, with shape `(batch, 1, 5)`, by the features, with shape `(batch, 5, d)`. The result, after `squeeze(1)`, is the pooled vector $z$ with shape `(batch, d)`.
- `aux_attn_scores` come from a second, separate attention layer. The write-up states that these scores are passed to a cross-entropy loss as an auxiliary loss over the slice position. A natural target for this loss is the position, among the five slices, of the slice the radiologist annotated; this reading is Nolad's and is not spelled out in the write-up.
</Deep>

<Source href="https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rsna-2024-lumbar-spine-degenerative-classification/writeups/avengers-1st-place-solution">NANACHI, 1st place write-up</Source>

The author reports the following progression on the public leaderboard: the baseline scored 0.37; attention-based MIL pooling improved it to 0.35; the bidirectional LSTM, the auxiliary losses and ensembling improved it to 0.33.

### Changes that did not improve the score

The write-up lists the following:

- Mamba or self-attention in place of the bidirectional LSTM.
- Sharing weights between the auxiliary and the main attention layers.
- Other combinations of series, for example sagittal T1 for the canal.
- Longer training. The best numbers of epochs were 7 for ConvNeXt-Small and 14 for EfficientNetV2-S.
- Larger models. ConvNeXt-Large scored worse than ConvNeXt-Base, which scored worse than ConvNeXt-Small.
- Vision transformers. The author suspects that the cause was his own setup rather than the architecture.

## Second place: YOLOX crops, weight sharing across levels and label cleaning

The second-place team (kevin:), Brendan Artley, Ian Pan and Yuji Ariyasu) combined the members' separate pipelines with a weighted average. Yuji Ariyasu's pipeline differs from the winner's in several choices:

- **Separate small models.** Axial and sagittal series, and the different targets, have separate models, each predicting three classes.
- **Weight sharing across levels and sides.** One model grades a crop regardless of its level or side. This gives each model five times as many training crops (one per level) or ten times (levels × sides) as a model for a single level. For conditions other than the canal, axial crops use only the half of the image on the relevant side, so that left and right are treated as the same task.
- **Axial pipeline.** Slice selection with hengck23's code, a YOLOX detector for the region, and a ConvNeXt-Small classifier.
- **Sagittal pipeline.** A 2.5D slice classifier from the `timm` library, a YOLOX detector for each level trained with Brendan Artley's data, rotation of the image so that the disc level is horizontal, cropping, and a MIL model on five images with a ConvNeXt-Small encoder. For the foramina, the five slices are centred between the spinal-canal and subarticular slices. Some models take T1 and T2 images in separate channels.
- **Removal of noisy labels.** With the out-of-fold predictions of the team's ensemble (cross-validation loss 0.3687), the team removed training samples whose label and prediction differed by 0.8 or more, with class-dependent coefficients for the rarer moderate and severe grades, and retrained. This improved both the public and the private score by about 1%.
- **Post-processing for the any-severe term.** In each study, the highest spinal-canal severe probability among the five levels was multiplied by 1.25.

<Foundation id="soft-labels" />

<Deep title="Effect of scaling the maximum severe probability" kind="math">

The any-severe term uses the prediction $m = \max_{\ell} p_{\mathrm{sev}}(\ell)$, where $p_{\mathrm{sev}}(\ell)$ is the predicted probability that level $\ell \in \{1, \dots, 5\}$ of the spinal canal is severe. Two effects make $m$ lower than the value that minimises this term.

**Class weights.** Suppose a study with a severe level carries weight $w_1 = 4$ in this term and a study without one carries weight $w_0 = 1$, and let $\pi$ be the probability that the study has a severe level. By the result derived under "Evaluation metric", with two classes, the loss is minimised by

$$
q^\star = \frac{w_1 \pi}{w_1 \pi + w_0 (1-\pi)} = \frac{4\pi}{1 + 3\pi} .
$$

For $\pi = 0.1$ this is $q^\star = 0.4 / 1.3 \approx 0.31$, about three times $\pi$.

**Maximum of several probabilities.** Let $A_\ell$ be the event that level $\ell$ is severe. The event "any level is severe" is the union $A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_5$. Since the union contains each $A_\ell$, and by the union bound,

$$
\max_\ell P(A_\ell) \;\le\; P\Big(\bigcup_{\ell} A_\ell\Big) \;\le\; \min\Big(1,\ \sum_\ell P(A_\ell)\Big).
$$

If the model assigns 0.3 to each of two levels, for example L3/L4 and L4/L5, and near zero elsewhere, then $m = 0.3$, while the probability that at least one is severe lies between 0.3 and 0.6; it is $1 - 0.7^2 = 0.51$ if the two levels are independent.

Both effects push the optimal prediction above $m$. Multiplying $m$ by 1.25 is a single-parameter correction applied only to the any-severe prediction; the per-level predictions are unchanged.
</Deep>

## Comparison with other competitions

In the two top solutions described here, the grade is predicted from a small crop around a location that is first predicted by a separate model, and the localisation models are trained on the coordinates provided only for training studies. The winners of the 2022 cervical spine fracture competition and the 2025 intracranial aneurysm competition also located anatomy first (vertebrae and cerebral vessels) and classified afterwards. In the 2019 intracranial haemorrhage competition, findings can occur anywhere in the head, and the winning solutions classified whole slices.

### Considerations for deployment

1. **Localisation.** The slice-selection stage depends on how series are acquired and ordered at a site, and should be validated on local data first. The grader sees only crops, and its inputs depend less on acquisition order.
2. **Visibility of intermediate results.** A wrong slice or level leads to a grade for the wrong anatomy. Displaying the selected slice and point allows a radiologist to check them.
3. **Grading thresholds.** The boundaries between grades reflect the annotating radiologists. A site with different reporting conventions should measure agreement with its own readers.
4. **Code.** The winner released the training notebook for each model, and the weights are available through the inference notebook's datasets.
