## Clinical background

An intracranial aneurysm is a localised dilation of the wall of an artery inside the skull. The competition description estimates that about 3% of people have one. Most never cause symptoms. Rupture releases blood into the subarachnoid space around the brain (subarachnoid haemorrhage), and a large fraction of patients die or are left disabled. The description cites roughly 500,000 deaths a year worldwide, half of them in people younger than 50, and states that up to half of aneurysms are diagnosed only after rupture.

An unruptured aneurysm can be monitored, or treated by surgical clipping or by endovascular coiling. Most unruptured aneurysms are found incidentally, on scans acquired for headache, stroke or trauma. Aneurysms a few millimetres across are easily overlooked when a radiologist reports many scans in a session. An algorithm that reads every head scan and marks suspicious arteries could serve as a second reader.

<Deep title="Anatomy: the Circle of Willis and the 13 label locations" kind="domain">

Blood reaches the brain through four arteries in the neck: the left and right **internal carotid arteries (ICA)** anteriorly, and the two **vertebral arteries** posteriorly, which join to form the **basilar artery**. At the base of the brain these vessels are connected in a ring, the **Circle of Willis**. Each carotid divides into an **anterior cerebral artery (ACA)** and a **middle cerebral artery (MCA)**. The two ACAs are connected by the **anterior communicating artery (ACom)**, and each carotid is connected to the posterior circulation by a **posterior communicating artery (PCom)**.

Aneurysms form mostly at branch points of this ring, where the flowing blood impinges on the vessel wall. The 13 location labels follow the anatomy:

| Location | Sides |
| --- | --- |
| Infraclinoid ICA (below the anterior clinoid process) | left, right |
| Supraclinoid ICA (above it) | left, right |
| MCA | left, right |
| ACA | left, right |
| PCom | left, right |
| ACom | single |
| Basilar tip | single |
| Other posterior circulation | single |

The ring varies between people: segments may be absent or hypoplastic, and the two sides may differ. A model that assumes a complete, symmetric ring will fail for these patients.
</Deep>

### Imaging modalities

The data contain four kinds of scan:

- **CT angiography (CTA).** CT acquired while iodinated contrast fills the arteries, which then appear bright, at roughly 200–500 Hounsfield units.
- **MR angiography (MRA),** usually time-of-flight. Flowing blood appears bright without injected contrast.
- **T1-weighted MRI after contrast.** Vessels enhance, as do other structures; some arteries show a dark flow void.
- **T2-weighted MRI.** Arteries appear dark against bright cerebrospinal fluid.

An aneurysm therefore has a different intensity profile in each modality. Slice thickness, orientation and in-plane resolution also vary between hospitals and scanners, and several test series were multi-frame DICOM files with missing header fields.

## Data

Each training series is a DICOM series with 14 binary labels: one for the presence of any aneurysm, and one for the presence of an aneurysm at each of the 13 locations. A separate file gives the image coordinates of the centre of each annotated aneurysm. For a subset of series the host also provided segmentations of the arteries of the Circle of Willis. There are no aneurysm masks and no aneurysm sizes.

The hidden test series were processed by a submitted notebook without internet access, within 12 hours on a Kaggle machine with two T4 GPUs. An evaluation API served one series at a time.

## Evaluation metric

For each label $j \in \{\mathrm{AP}, 1, \dots, 13\}$, where AP denotes "aneurysm present", the organisers computed the ROC AUC over all test series, $\mathrm{AUC}_j$. The score is the weighted mean with weight $w_{\mathrm{AP}} = 13$ and $w_i = 1$ for each location:

$$
\text{Score}
= \frac{w_{\mathrm{AP}}\,\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{AP}} + \sum_{i=1}^{13} w_i\,\mathrm{AUC}_i}{w_{\mathrm{AP}} + \sum_{i=1}^{13} w_i}
= \frac{13\,\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{AP}} + \sum_{i=1}^{13}\mathrm{AUC}_i}{26}.
$$

Dividing numerator and denominator by 13 separates the two halves:

$$
\text{Score} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{AP}} + \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{13}\sum_{i=1}^{13}\mathrm{AUC}_i .
$$

Detection of any aneurysm and localisation to an artery therefore carry equal weight.

<Foundation id="roc-auc" />

AUC depends only on the ranking of the series by predicted score. Predictions need not be calibrated, and assigning a constant score to an unreadable series changes that series' rank but leaves the relative order of all other series unchanged.

Each location AUC is computed over all test series, and most series have no aneurysm at a given location. For rare locations the number of positive series in the test set is small, and the AUC has a correspondingly large variance. The top two write-ups both report differences between public and private scores; the second-place team measured a consistent gap of 3–4 AUC points.

### Stage 0: preprocessing

About 60 training series were excluded because of orientation anomalies, corrupted files or implausible slice spacing. The remaining series were processed as follows.

1. Within a series, slices whose matrix size and pixel spacing differ from the majority are removed, as are slices whose spacing to their neighbours is an outlier.
2. DICOM is converted to NIfTI with `dcm2niix`. If conversion fails, the files are rewritten with `gdcmconv --raw` and conversion is repeated.
3. Every volume is reoriented to a common anatomical orientation with nnU-Net's reorienting reader.
4. Intensities are normalised per volume: $x' = (x - \mu)/\sigma$, where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of the volume.

No CT window is applied. After step 4, CT volumes (in Hounsfield units) and MR volumes (in arbitrary scanner units) share the same intensity scale, which allows a single set of models to process all modalities.

### Stage 1: coarse vessel segmentation

A 3D nnU-Net trained at an isotropic spacing of 1 mm segments three coarse vessel groups: the posterior circulation with the basilar artery, the middle cerebral arteries, and all remaining vessels. It is applied with a sliding window over the whole head. The binarised output is clustered with DBSCAN, and the centre of the largest cluster defines a cube with 140 mm sides.

<Foundation id="unet" />

<Deep title="DBSCAN on foreground voxels" kind="math">

DBSCAN (Ester et al., 1996) has two parameters: a radius $\varepsilon$ and a minimum count $m$. For a set of points $X$ (here, the coordinates of foreground voxels), the $\varepsilon$-neighbourhood of $x$ is

$$
N_\varepsilon(x) = \{\, y \in X : \lVert x - y\rVert \le \varepsilon \,\}.
$$

A point is a **core point** if $|N_\varepsilon(x)| \ge m$. Two core points belong to the same cluster if they are connected by a chain of core points, each within $\varepsilon$ of the next. A non-core point within $\varepsilon$ of a core point joins that core point's cluster as a **border point**. All other points are labelled noise.

Isolated false-positive voxels have few neighbours, so they are labelled noise, while the densely connected vessel tree forms one large cluster. Taking the centre of the largest cluster gives a robust estimate of the location of the Circle of Willis. The parameter values are given in the winner's repository.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Cost of the coarse-to-fine design" kind="detail">

The fine spacing used in Stage 2 is $0.80 \times 0.45 \times 0.44$ mm, a voxel volume of

$$
0.80 \times 0.45 \times 0.44 = 0.158 \ \text{mm}^3 .
$$

At 1 mm isotropic spacing, one voxel covers 1 mm³, so the fine grid has $1/0.158 \approx 6.3$ times as many voxels per unit volume as the coarse grid.

The 140 mm cube at the fine spacing contains

$$
\frac{140}{0.80} \times \frac{140}{0.45} \times \frac{140}{0.44} = 175 \times 311 \times 318 \approx 1.73 \times 10^{7}
$$

voxels. A whole head of about $200 \times 240 \times 200$ mm contains $9.6 \times 10^6$ mm³, which is $9.6 \times 10^6$ voxels at 1 mm and about $6.1 \times 10^7$ voxels at the fine spacing. The coarse pass over the whole head therefore processes about one sixth of the voxels that a fine pass would, and the fine models process a cube with less than a third of the voxels of the whole head at their resolution.
</Deep>

### Stage 2: fine vessel segmentation

Two further nnU-Nets with residual encoders (`nnUNetResEncUNetMPlans`, `3d_fullres`) are applied inside the cube at a spacing of 0.80 × 0.45 × 0.44 mm:

- **Model 2:** Dice loss + cross-entropy + Skeleton Recall loss with weight 1.
- **Model 3:** Tversky loss + cross-entropy + Skeleton Recall loss with weight 3.

Arteries are thin tubular structures with few voxels, so a voxel-overlap loss penalises a break in an artery only slightly. The Skeleton Recall loss (Kirchhoff et al., 2024) penalises the prediction for missing the centreline of the true vessels and therefore favours connected predictions. The Tversky loss in Model 3 weights false negatives more heavily than false positives, so Model 3 includes uncertain vessel voxels that Model 2 omits. The two models produce different errors, and the classifier receives the masks of both.

<Foundation id="dice-and-overlap" />

<Deep title="Tversky and Skeleton Recall losses" kind="math">

Let $p_v \in [0,1]$ be the predicted foreground probability at voxel $v$ and $g_v \in \{0,1\}$ the ground truth. Soft counts of true positives, false positives and false negatives are

$$
\mathrm{TP} = \sum_v p_v\, g_v, \qquad
\mathrm{FP} = \sum_v p_v\,(1 - g_v), \qquad
\mathrm{FN} = \sum_v (1 - p_v)\, g_v .
$$

**Tversky loss** (Salehi et al., 2017). With weights $\alpha, \beta \ge 0$,

$$
\mathrm{TI} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \alpha\,\mathrm{FN} + \beta\,\mathrm{FP}}, \qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tversky}} = 1 - \mathrm{TI}.
$$

For $\alpha = \beta = \tfrac12$ the index equals the soft Dice coefficient:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \tfrac12\mathrm{FN} + \tfrac12\mathrm{FP}}
= \frac{2\,\mathrm{TP}}{2\,\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN} + \mathrm{FP}} .
$$

Choosing $\alpha > \beta$ makes a missed vessel voxel cost more than a false one, which raises recall.

**Skeleton Recall loss** (Kirchhoff et al., 2024). Let $G$ be the ground-truth mask and $S = \mathrm{skel}(G)$ its skeleton, a centreline one voxel thick, computed once per training case and slightly dilated for tolerance. The skeleton recall is the mean predicted probability on the skeleton,

$$
\mathrm{SR} = \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{v \in S} p_v ,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}} = 1 - \mathrm{SR},
$$

computed per class and averaged over classes. A gap in a predicted vessel removes every skeleton voxel in the gap from the sum, whereas an error in the vessel's width affects few skeleton voxels. Because $S$ is precomputed from the labels, the term adds little training cost.

The Stage 2 losses are

$$
\mathcal{L}_{2} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} + 1\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}},
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{3} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tversky}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} + 3\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}} .
$$
</Deep>

The fine models were trained with stronger augmentation than the nnU-Net default, with left–right mirroring disabled. Mirroring maps a left MCA to a right MCA, and without an exchange of the corresponding labels it would corrupt the side-specific targets. Low-resolution simulation (downsampling followed by upsampling of training volumes) was added for robustness to thick-slice scans.

At inference, the segmentation from Model 2 defines a bounding box around the vessels. The box, enlarged by a margin, is the region of interest (ROI) for the classifier. The masks from both fine models are passed to Stage 3.

### Stage 3: per-artery classification

The classifier receives the ROI resampled to 128 × 256 × 256 voxels, together with the vessel masks.

**Backbone.** The backbone is an nnU-Net that had already been trained for vessel segmentation. In the author's ablation, training the classifier without this initialisation lowered the score from 0.902 to 0.794, the largest effect of any component. The last decoder block, the most expensive, was removed without loss of accuracy.

**Masked pooling per location.** For each of the 13 locations, the decoder feature map is averaged over the voxels inside that location's vessel mask. This yields one feature vector per artery. The vectors obtained with the masks of the two fine segmentation models are concatenated.

<Deep title="Masked average pooling" kind="math">

Let $F \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times D \times H \times W}$ be the feature map with $C$ channels on a grid $\Omega$ of $D \times H \times W$ voxels, and let $M_k \in [0,1]^{D \times H \times W}$ be the mask of location $k$, resampled to the grid of $F$. Write $F(v) \in \mathbb{R}^{C}$ for the feature vector at voxel $v$. The pooled vector for location $k$ is

$$
z_k = \frac{\sum_{v \in \Omega} M_k(v)\, F(v)}{\max\!\Big(\sum_{v \in \Omega} M_k(v),\ \epsilon\Big)} \in \mathbb{R}^{C}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, 13 .
$$

For a binary mask this is the mean of the feature vectors over the voxels of artery $k$. If the mask is empty, because the artery was not segmented or is absent in this patient, both sums are zero; the lower bound $\epsilon > 0$ on the denominator makes $z_k = 0$ instead of undefined. The repository also implements max pooling and generalised-mean pooling with the same weights.
</Deep>

<Deep title="The masked pooling operation in the winner's code" kind="code">

The repository implements the operation as `RegionMaskedPooling3D`. The mean mode:

```python title="src/models/components/region_mask_pooling.py (excerpt)"
def _masked_pool_single(self, mode, feat, weights):
    feat_expanded = feat.unsqueeze(1)                     # (B, 1, C, D, H, W)
    if mode == "mean":
        denom = weights.flatten(2).sum(-1).unsqueeze(-1).clamp_min(self.eps)
        num = (weights.unsqueeze(2) * feat_expanded).flatten(3).sum(-1)
        return num / denom
```

Tensor shapes, line by line:

- `feat` has shape `(B, C, D, H, W)` (batch, channels, depth, height, width). `unsqueeze(1)` gives `(B, 1, C, D, H, W)`, which broadcasts against $K$ masks.
- `weights` has shape `(B, K, D, H, W)`, one mask per location. `flatten(2)` merges the spatial axes, `.sum(-1)` gives the mask volume per location with shape `(B, K)`, and `unsqueeze(-1)` gives `(B, K, 1)`. `clamp_min(self.eps)` is the lower bound $\epsilon$ in the equation above.
- `weights.unsqueeze(2)` has shape `(B, K, 1, D, H, W)`. The product with `feat_expanded` broadcasts to `(B, K, C, D, H, W)`, and `flatten(3).sum(-1)` sums over space to give `(B, K, C)`, the numerator.
- The quotient is $z_k$ for every location and batch element.

The method is decorated with `@torch.autocast(device_type="cuda", enabled=False)` and casts to float32, because sums over tens of thousands of voxels can overflow half precision.
</Deep>

<Source href="https://github.com/uchiyama33/rsna2025_1st_place/blob/main/src/models/components/region_mask_pooling.py">uchiyama33/rsna2025_1st_place, region_mask_pooling.py</Source>

**Global context.** Each location vector is concatenated with a global feature vector $g$, obtained by global average pooling of the encoder output over the whole ROI.

**Transformer over locations.** The 13 vectors are treated as a sequence of 13 tokens and passed through a transformer encoder, which the author calls a location-aware transformer, so that the representation of each artery depends on the others. Locations are anatomically coupled: an absent ACA segment, for example, changes the appearance of the ACom region. An MLP head outputs one logit per location.

<Foundation id="attention-transformers" />

**Aneurysm-present head.** A separate head pools features over the union of all vessel masks, concatenates $g$, and outputs the logit for any aneurysm.

**Auxiliary localisation target.** The decoder is also trained to reconstruct, at each annotated aneurysm centre, a sphere with a radius of 5 voxels. In the ablation, removing this target lowered the score from 0.902 to 0.876.

### Loss function

Each of the 14 classification outputs is treated as an independent binary problem with the binary cross-entropy

$$
\ell(y, \hat p) = -\,\big[\, y \log \hat p + (1 - y)\log(1 - \hat p) \,\big],
$$

averaged over the 13 location outputs for $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}^{\mathrm{loc}}$ and applied to the AP output for $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}^{\mathrm{AP}}$. The sphere reconstruction $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sphere}}$ combines a class-balanced binary cross-entropy with a Focal-Tversky++ loss (the exact form is in the repository). The total loss is

$$
\mathcal{L} = 0.1\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}^{\mathrm{loc}} + 0.05\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}^{\mathrm{AP}} + 1.0\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sphere}} .
$$

The classification terms have small weights. With all three weights set to 1, the ablation score fell from 0.902 to 0.884; the author attributes this to overfitting of the classification heads.

<Foundation id="binary-cross-entropy" />

<Deep title="Sparsity of the sphere target, and why a dense target helps" kind="math">

A sphere of radius $r = 5$ voxels contains approximately

$$
\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi \cdot 125 \approx 524
$$

voxels. The classifier input has $128 \times 256 \times 256 = 8{,}388{,}608$ voxels, so one aneurysm occupies a fraction of about

$$
\frac{524}{8{,}388{,}608} \approx 6.2 \times 10^{-5}
$$

of the volume. With unweighted voxel-wise cross-entropy, the background term would dominate the loss by a factor of about $1.6 \times 10^4$. The class-balanced cross-entropy and the Tversky-type term correct for this imbalance.

A series-level label provides one scalar target per output for the entire volume, and the gradient it produces with respect to the features at any particular voxel is small and spread over the whole volume. The sphere target provides a target at every voxel. Voxels near an annotated aneurysm receive a direct gradient towards features that distinguish the aneurysm, and distant voxels receive a gradient towards background features. The masked-pooled features read by the classification heads are computed from the same decoder, so they are shaped by this dense supervision. The ablation measures the effect as 0.026 in score.
</Deep>

### Training

- AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$, effective batch size 8 through gradient accumulation, cosine learning-rate schedule with warm-up.
- An exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights is used at inference.
- Augmentation: Gaussian noise and smoothing, intensity shift and scaling, contrast change, sharpening, intensity inversion, flips along all three axes, rotations up to ±10°, scaling and shearing up to ±10%, mild grid distortion, and low-resolution simulation.
- Multilabel-stratified 5-fold cross-validation.

### Inference

The final prediction averages four of the five fold models, each applied to the input and to its left–right mirror image. Let $f_m$ be fold model $m$, $\mathcal{F}$ the left–right flip of the volume, and $\Pi$ the permutation of the 14 outputs that exchanges each left location with its right counterpart and leaves the others fixed. Predictions on the mirrored input must be mapped back with $\Pi$ before averaging:

$$
\hat{p} = \frac{1}{2 \cdot 4} \sum_{m=1}^{4} \Big( f_m(x) + \Pi\, f_m(\mathcal{F}x) \Big).
$$

If segmentation or ROI extraction fails (an unreadable file, an empty vessel mask), the pipeline returns for each label the mean of its out-of-fold predictions on the training set.

The author also wrote an orientation-correction routine. It locates the three coarse vessel groups in the Stage 1 segmentation and compares their relative positions with the expected anatomy, which reveals permuted axes in misoriented scans. It corrected every orientation problem in the training set and did not change the leaderboard score, which suggests that the test set contained no such cases.

### Development of the design

The author's first model was a single end-to-end 3D classifier with auxiliary heads for vessel segmentation and aneurysm localisation. It learned to detect the presence of an aneurysm but not to assign its location. The author then observed that a standard nnU-Net trained on the host's vessel labels generalised well across all four modalities, and built the subsequent designs on the vessel segmentation. An intermediate design gave the network the image and one binary vessel mask as two input channels and ran it once per location; it worked but required 14 forward passes per scan. Masked pooling replaced it with a single pass.

## Second place: tri-axial ROI extraction and a multi-task nnU-Net

The second-place team, BraveCowCow, organise the MICCAI TopCoW and TopBrain challenges on segmentation of the Circle of Willis and the cerebral vessels. Their solution also uses vessel segmentation to guide aneurysm classification, with a different design.

- **ROI extraction in 2D.** Three slices along each axis, at one quarter, one half and three quarters of the volume, give nine 2D images. A 2D nnU-Net segments the vessels in them, and the extent of the segmented vessels defines the crop. This is faster than a 3D pass and succeeded on every training case.
- **One 3D multi-task network.** All volumes are resized to 224 × 224 × 224. An nnU-Net trained from scratch predicts a vessel segmentation with 26 classes (the host's labels merged with TopCoW and TopBrain annotations and manual corrections), an aneurysm segmentation, the 14 labels and the imaging modality. At inference only the encoder and the classification head are run.
- **Aneurysm masks from centre points.** The host provided only centres. The team generated masks iteratively: train a model, predict masks, correct them manually around the given centres, and retrain.
- **Label corrections.** They corrected training labels that confused left with right, and supraclinoid with infraclinoid ICA.
- **Left–right flip with label exchange.** A flipped volume is paired with labels permuted by $\Pi$ as defined above. This doubles the training data without corrupting side-specific labels.
- **Test-time augmentation** with 8 variants, using the same label exchange.

The team also released a 3D Slicer extension and a Docker inference backend, which allow the model to be run on a local scan inside a standard medical image viewer.

## Comparison with other competitions

The 2019 RSNA intracranial haemorrhage competition also involved head scans with an "any" label and subtype labels, and its winners used 2D slice classifiers followed by a sequence model. Haemorrhage is visible on individual slices, and its subtype is determined by its position relative to the brain surface. An aneurysm is a small 3D structure defined by its relation to a specific artery, and the four modalities differ too much in intensity for a fixed intensity window. The vessel segmentation provides an anatomical coordinate system for the 13 labels, and a backbone trained to segment vessels provides features that transfer between modalities.

The winners of the 2022 cervical spine and 2023 abdominal trauma competitions also segmented anatomy (vertebrae, organs) before classification. In those solutions the segmentation was used to crop the input. Here it is also used inside the classifier, as the pooling mask for each location.

### Considerations for deployment

1. **Prevalence.** The competition data were enriched with aneurysms. In routine practice most scans contain none, so the positive predictive value at a given threshold will be lower than in the competition data. The operating point should be chosen on local data.
2. **Failure handling.** The leaderboard fall-back (the mean prediction) should be replaced by an explicit "could not be analysed" result.
3. **Small aneurysms.** Neither write-up reports performance stratified by aneurysm size. Small aneurysms are the most clinically valuable findings and the most likely to be missed.
4. **Laterality.** Both top solutions handled left and right explicitly. Orientation handling should be verified on local scanners before location outputs are used.
