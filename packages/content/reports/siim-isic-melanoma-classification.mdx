## The problem in its field

**Melanoma** is a cancer of the pigment-producing cells of the skin. It is the least common of the main skin cancers but causes most skin-cancer deaths (about 75%, according to the competition description). The American Cancer Society estimated more than 100,000 new cases and about 7,000 deaths in the United States in 2020. Caught early, melanoma is usually cured by removing it with minor surgery; once it has spread, the outlook is much worse.

Dermatologists examine suspicious moles with a **dermatoscope**, a magnifier with polarised or immersion lighting that shows pigment structures below the skin surface. They also compare lesions on the same patient: most people have a consistent "signature" pattern of moles, and a lesion that looks unlike the others (the **ugly duckling sign**) is more suspicious. The competition encouraged approaches that use such patient-level context.

<Deep title="What dermoscopy shows" kind="domain">

Classic dermoscopic criteria for melanoma include:

- **Asymmetry** of colour and structure in one or two axes.
- **Atypical pigment network**: irregular, thickened or broken lines of pigment.
- **Multiple colours**, particularly blue-white veil, grey, and red.
- **Irregular dots, globules and streaks** at the periphery.
- **Regression structures**: white scar-like areas and blue-grey peppering.

Benign lesions that can resemble melanoma include atypical naevi (moles), seborrhoeic keratoses, solar lentigines and lichenoid keratoses. The ISIC diagnosis classes group these, which is why a model can learn from the benign diagnoses as well as from melanomas.
</Deep>

## The data

The 2020 training set has about 33,000 dermoscopic images from about 2,000 patients, of which about 1.8% (fewer than 600 images) are melanoma. Each image comes with metadata: age, sex, and anatomical site. Unlike earlier ISIC challenges, several images per patient are provided, allowing patient-level comparisons. The test set has about 11,000 images. The dataset is described in Rotemberg et al. (2021).

The **ISIC 2018 and 2019 challenge** datasets were allowed as external data. The 2019 set (about 25,000 images) is labelled with nine diagnostic categories and is much richer in melanomas, because it was assembled from specialist collections.

## The metric

<Foundation id="roc-auc" />

<Deep title="How noisy is AUC with few positives?" kind="math">

AUC equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative (the Mann–Whitney statistic):
$$
\widehat{\text{AUC}} = \frac{1}{n_+ n_-}\sum_{i \in +}\sum_{j \in -} \mathbb{1}[s_i > s_j].
$$
Hanley and McNeil (1982) derived an approximation to its variance:
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\text{AUC}}) \approx \frac{A(1-A) + (n_+ - 1)(Q_1 - A^2) + (n_- - 1)(Q_2 - A^2)}{n_+ n_-},
\quad Q_1 = \frac{A}{2 - A},\; Q_2 = \frac{2A^2}{1 + A},
$$
where $A$ is the true AUC. With $A = 0.95$: $Q_1 = 0.9048$, $Q_2 = 0.9256$, so $Q_1 - A^2 \approx 0.0023$ and $Q_2 - A^2 \approx 0.0231$.

Suppose, for illustration, that the public test portion has about 60 melanomas and 3,300 benign lesions (plausible for a 30% public split at the training prevalence). Then
$$
\operatorname{Var} \approx \frac{0.0475 + 59(0.0023) + 3299(0.0231)}{60 \times 3300} \approx \frac{0.0475 + 0.136 + 76.2}{198{,}000} \approx 3.9\times10^{-4},
$$
a standard deviation of about 0.020. Differences of 0.005 between leaderboard entries are therefore far inside the noise. The term with $n_-$ dominates: the scarcity of positives, not the number of negatives, limits precision, because every negative is compared with the same few positives.

This is why the winning team found almost no correlation between single models' cross-validation and public scores, and why the leaderboard shook up.
</Deep>

### Validation that could be trusted

The team used Chris Deotte's resized JPEG datasets and his **triple-stratified, leak-free folds**: patients are grouped so that all images of a patient fall in one fold, and folds are stratified by target and by the number of images per patient. They trained and validated on all three years and tracked two scores:

- **cv_all**: AUC over the validation images from 2018, 2019 and 2020 together. Many more melanomas, so it is stable.
- **cv_2020**: AUC over the 2020 validation images only. It resembles the test distribution but is noisy.

<Foundation id="cross-validation" />

For single models, cross-validation and public leaderboard scores were essentially uncorrelated. Ensembles were stable. The team submitted two ensembles:

| Ensemble | cv_all | cv_2020 | Public | Private |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1: chosen by cv_all | 0.9845 | 0.9600 | 0.9442 | **0.9490** (1st) |
| 2: chosen by cv_2020 | — | 0.9638 | 0.9494 | 0.9481 (would be 3rd) |

The ensemble chosen on the larger, more stable validation set won, even though its public score was lower. The best single model had cv_2020 of 0.9481.

They also noted that public TensorFlow/TPU notebooks had achieved lucky public scores, while PyTorch/GPU pipelines had better cross-validation, an example of how easily the public leaderboard could mislead.

### Nine classes instead of two

Instead of a binary melanoma label, the models predicted the **diagnosis**, using cross-entropy over the nine ISIC 2019 categories. The 2020 diagnoses were mapped onto them: seborrhoeic keratosis, lichenoid keratosis, solar lentigo and "lentigo NOS" to benign keratosis (BKL); naevus to NV; melanoma to MEL; café-au-lait macule and atypical melanocytic proliferation to "unknown". The melanoma score is the softmax probability of MEL. This gained about 0.01 AUC.

<Foundation id="softmax-cross-entropy" />

<Deep title="Why a finer target helps a binary ranking" kind="math">

With a binary target, all non-melanomas form one class, and the network is only told "not melanoma". With diagnosis labels, it must also separate naevi from keratoses from lentigines. The two objectives share the same features; the multi-class one supplies more bits of supervision per image. Most benign images are naevi, the lesions most easily confused with melanoma, and learning the specific features that distinguish naevus from melanoma is exactly what the ranking needs.

Formally, let $z \in \mathbb{R}^9$ be the logits. The multi-class model's melanoma score is
$$
s = \operatorname{softmax}(z)_{\text{MEL}} = \frac{e^{z_{\text{MEL}}}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}} = \sigma\Big(z_{\text{MEL}} - \log\textstyle\sum_{k \ne \text{MEL}} e^{z_k}\Big),
$$
a binary logit that compares melanoma against a soft maximum of all other diagnoses. The binary head is a special case in which the "other" logit has no structure. Since AUC depends only on the ranking of $s$, calibration differences between the two formulations are irrelevant; only the quality of the ordering matters.

This is also a form of **auxiliary supervision**: the extra classes act as auxiliary tasks that shape the representation.
</Deep>

### Models, inputs and augmentation

Backbones: EfficientNet-B3 to B7, SE-ResNeXt-101 and ResNeSt-101, at input sizes from 384 to 896 (cropped from 512, 768 or 1024 JPEGs). Some models had a **metadata branch** with 14 features taken from public notebooks: sex, age, one-hot anatomical site, number of images of the patient, and image size. The metadata branch used Swish activations and was concatenated with the image features before the classifier.

<Foundation id="cnn-backbones" />

Augmentation (albumentations): transpose and flips; brightness and contrast; blur, motion blur, median blur or Gaussian noise; optical, grid or elastic distortion; CLAHE; hue-saturation-value shifts; shift-scale-rotate; and cutout of about 37.5% of the image size. Most were applied with high probability (0.5–0.85). Dermoscopic images have no canonical orientation, and colour varies with the device and lighting, so strong geometric and colour augmentations are appropriate.

<Deep title="The augmentation pipeline" kind="code">

A Nolad reconstruction, in albumentations, of the augmentations listed in the write-up. Probabilities and the main limits follow the write-up; other parameter values are illustrative, so take the exact configuration from the repository.

```python
import albumentations as A

def train_transforms(size):
    return A.Compose([
        A.Transpose(p=0.5), A.VerticalFlip(p=0.5), A.HorizontalFlip(p=0.5),
        A.RandomBrightness(limit=0.2, p=0.75), A.RandomContrast(limit=0.2, p=0.75),
        A.OneOf([A.MotionBlur(blur_limit=5), A.MedianBlur(blur_limit=5),
                 A.GaussianBlur(blur_limit=5), A.GaussNoise(var_limit=(5.0, 30.0))], p=0.7),
        A.OneOf([A.OpticalDistortion(distort_limit=1.0),
                 A.GridDistortion(num_steps=5, distort_limit=1.0),
                 A.ElasticTransform(alpha=3)], p=0.7),
        A.CLAHE(clip_limit=4.0, p=0.7),
        A.HueSaturationValue(hue_shift_limit=10, sat_shift_limit=20, val_shift_limit=10, p=0.5),
        A.ShiftScaleRotate(shift_limit=0.1, scale_limit=0.1, rotate_limit=15, border_mode=0, p=0.85),
        A.Resize(size, size),
        A.Cutout(max_h_size=int(size * 0.375), max_w_size=int(size * 0.375), num_holes=1, p=0.7),
        A.Normalize(),
    ])
```

### Rank averaging

Each model's predictions were converted to **percentile ranks** (`rank(pct=True)`) before averaging. Different models produce probabilities on different scales: a model trained with nine classes and one with a metadata branch may give melanoma probabilities concentrated in different ranges. Averaging raw probabilities would let the most spread-out model dominate. Since AUC depends only on the ordering, averaging ranks is the natural combination.

<Foundation id="ensembling-tta" />

<Deep title="Rank averaging and AUC" kind="math">

For model $m$ with scores $s^{(m)}_i$ over $N$ test images, its percentile rank is
$$
r^{(m)}_i = \frac{1}{N}\,\#\{j : s^{(m)}_j \le s^{(m)}_i\}.
$$
The ensemble score is $\bar r_i = \frac1M \sum_m r^{(m)}_i$.

Each $r^{(m)}$ is a monotone transformation of $s^{(m)}$, so each model's own AUC is unchanged by ranking. Ranks are uniformly spread on $(0, 1]$ for every model, so each contributes equally to the ordering of the average, whatever its original calibration. A model whose probabilities are mostly near 0 with a few near 1 would otherwise decide the order of the extreme cases on its own.

The drawback is that ranks discard confidence: two lesions both ranked in the top 1% by one model count as equally suspicious even if their probabilities are 0.5 and 0.99. For AUC this is harmless; for a clinical threshold, calibrated probabilities are needed afterwards.
</Deep>

Each model was evaluated with 5 folds and 8 TTA views (flips and transposes).

## How this compares with other competitions

- **ISIC 2024** addressed the same disease with a very different kind of image: tiles cropped from 3D total-body photographs, of much lower quality than dermoscopy, with a large set of tabular features. Gradient-boosted trees on image-model outputs and metadata won there, and the "ugly duckling" idea became explicit patient-normalised features.
- **APTOS 2019** also depended on an earlier competition's data and suffered from a public–private mismatch; its winner, by contrast, validated on the public leaderboard carefully.
- **RSNA breast cancer detection** shares the extreme imbalance but used a threshold-dependent metric, which made its leaderboard even noisier than this AUC-scored one.
