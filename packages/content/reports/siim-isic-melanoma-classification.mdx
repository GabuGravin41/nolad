## Clinical background

**Melanoma** is a cancer of the melanocytes, the pigment-producing cells of the skin. It is the least common of the main skin cancers, and the competition description states that it causes about 75% of skin-cancer deaths. The American Cancer Society estimated more than 100,000 new cases and about 7,000 deaths in the United States in 2020. A melanoma found early can usually be removed by minor surgery; once it has spread, the prognosis is much worse.

Dermatologists examine suspicious lesions with a **dermatoscope**, a hand-held magnifier with polarised or immersion lighting that shows pigment structures below the skin surface. They also compare lesions on the same patient. Most of one person's moles resemble each other, and a lesion that differs from the others (the **ugly duckling sign**) is more suspicious. The competition encouraged approaches that use this patient-level context.

<Deep title="Dermoscopic features of melanoma" kind="domain">

Standard dermoscopic criteria for melanoma include:

- **Asymmetry** of colour and structure about one or two axes.
- **Atypical pigment network**: irregular, thickened or broken lines of pigment.
- **Multiple colours**, particularly blue-white veil, grey and red.
- **Irregular dots, globules and streaks** at the periphery.
- **Regression structures**: white scar-like areas and blue-grey peppering.

Benign lesions that can resemble melanoma include atypical naevi (moles), seborrhoeic keratoses, solar lentigines and lichenoid keratoses. The ISIC diagnosis classes separate these lesions, so a model trained on the diagnosis labels receives supervision from the benign classes as well as from melanomas.
</Deep>

## Data

The 2020 training set contains 33,126 dermoscopic images from about 2,056 patients. Of these, 584 images (1.8%) are melanoma. Each image has metadata: age, sex and anatomical site. Unlike earlier ISIC challenges, the data include several images per patient, which allows comparisons between lesions of the same patient. The test set has 10,982 images. The dataset is described by Rotemberg et al. (2021).

The **ISIC 2018 and 2019 challenge** datasets were permitted as external data. The 2019 set contains 25,331 images labelled with nine diagnostic categories. It has a much higher proportion of melanomas than the 2020 set, because it was assembled from specialist collections.

## Evaluation metric

Submissions were scored by the area under the ROC curve (ROC AUC) of the melanoma probability over the test images.

<Foundation id="roc-auc" />

<Deep title="Variance of the AUC when positives are few" kind="math">

Let $n_+$ be the number of melanomas and $n_-$ the number of benign lesions in a test set, and $s_i \in \mathbb{R}$ the score of image $i$. The empirical AUC is the fraction of (positive, negative) pairs that the scores order correctly, which is the Mann–Whitney statistic:

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{AUC}} = \frac{1}{n_+ n_-}\sum_{i \in +}\sum_{j \in -} \mathbb{1}[s_i > s_j].
$$

Let $A \in [0,1]$ be the true AUC. Hanley and McNeil (1982) define two auxiliary probabilities: $Q_1$, the probability that two randomly chosen positives are both scored above one randomly chosen negative, and $Q_2$, the probability that one randomly chosen positive is scored above two randomly chosen negatives. They approximate them by

$$
Q_1 = \frac{A}{2 - A}, \qquad Q_2 = \frac{2A^2}{1 + A},
$$

and derive the variance

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{AUC}}) \approx \frac{V}{n_+ n_-}, \qquad
V = A(1-A) + (n_+ - 1)(Q_1 - A^2) + (n_- - 1)(Q_2 - A^2).
$$

**Worked example.** Take $A = 0.95$, so $A^2 = 0.9025$ and $A(1-A) = 0.0475$. Then

$$
Q_1 = \frac{0.95}{1.05} = 0.90476, \qquad Q_2 = \frac{1.805}{1.95} = 0.92564,
$$

$$
Q_1 - A^2 = 0.00226, \qquad Q_2 - A^2 = 0.02314 .
$$

Assume, for illustration, that the public leaderboard used 30% of the 10,982 test images, about 3,300 images, and that melanomas occur at the training prevalence of 1.8%. This gives $n_+ = 60$ and $n_- = 3{,}300$ (rounded). The three terms of $V$ are

$$
A(1-A) = 0.0475, \qquad 59 \times 0.00226 = 0.133, \qquad 3{,}299 \times 0.02314 = 76.34,
$$

so $V \approx 76.52$. Dividing by $n_+ n_- = 198{,}000$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{AUC}}) \approx \frac{76.52}{198{,}000} \approx 3.9 \times 10^{-4},
$$

a standard deviation of about $\sqrt{3.9 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 0.020$. Two submissions whose public scores differ by 0.005 are therefore well within one standard deviation of each other.

**Dependence on the number of positives.** The third term of $V$ is larger than the other two by a factor of several hundred. Keeping only that term, and using $n_- - 1 \approx n_-$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{AUC}}) \approx \frac{(n_- - 1)(Q_2 - A^2)}{n_+ n_-}
\approx \frac{Q_2 - A^2}{n_+} = \frac{0.02314}{60} = 3.86 \times 10^{-4}.
$$

The variance is approximately inversely proportional to the number of positives and nearly independent of the number of negatives. Doubling the number of benign images would leave it almost unchanged; doubling the number of melanomas would halve it.

The winning team reported that the cross-validation and public-leaderboard scores of single models were almost uncorrelated, which is consistent with this estimate. The final standings on the private leaderboard differed considerably from the public ones.
</Deep>

### Validation

The team used the resized JPEG datasets and the **triple-stratified, leak-free folds** published by Chris Deotte. All images of a patient fall in the same fold, so that no patient appears in both training and validation, and the folds are stratified by target and by the number of images per patient. The models were trained and validated on all three years, and two validation scores were tracked:

- **cv_all**: the AUC over the validation images from 2018, 2019 and 2020 together. It contains many more melanomas than the 2020 data alone, and therefore has a smaller variance.
- **cv_2020**: the AUC over the 2020 validation images only. Its distribution is closer to that of the test set, but it is based on fewer melanomas.

<Foundation id="cross-validation" />

The write-up reports that single-model cross-validation scores and public leaderboard scores were essentially uncorrelated, while ensembles gave more stable scores. The team submitted two ensembles:

| Ensemble | cv_all | cv_2020 | Public | Private |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1: chosen by cv_all | 0.9845 | 0.9600 | 0.9442 | **0.9490** (1st) |
| 2: chosen by cv_2020 | — | 0.9638 | 0.9494 | 0.9481 (would be 3rd) |

Ensemble 1, selected on the larger validation set, had the lower public score and the higher private score, and won. The best single model had a cv_2020 of 0.9481.

The team also observed that public notebooks using TensorFlow on TPUs had obtained high public scores, while PyTorch pipelines on GPUs had better cross-validation scores.

### Nine-class diagnosis target

The models were trained to predict the **diagnosis** with cross-entropy over the nine ISIC 2019 categories, in place of a binary melanoma label. The 2020 diagnoses were mapped onto these categories:

- seborrhoeic keratosis, lichenoid keratosis, solar lentigo and "lentigo NOS" to benign keratosis (BKL);
- naevus to NV;
- melanoma to MEL;
- café-au-lait macule and atypical melanocytic proliferation to "unknown".

The melanoma score is the softmax probability of MEL. The team reports that this target improved the AUC by about 0.01 compared with binary cross-entropy.

<Foundation id="softmax-cross-entropy" />

<Deep title="The softmax melanoma probability as a binary score" kind="math">

Let $z \in \mathbb{R}^9$ be the logits of the nine classes and write $z_{\mathrm{M}}$ for the melanoma logit. Define the log-sum-exp of the other eight logits,

$$
L = \log \sum_{k \ne \mathrm{M}} e^{z_k}, \qquad \text{so that} \qquad \sum_{k \ne \mathrm{M}} e^{z_k} = e^{L}.
$$

The melanoma probability is

$$
s = \operatorname{softmax}(z)_{\mathrm{M}} = \frac{e^{z_{\mathrm{M}}}}{e^{z_{\mathrm{M}}} + e^{L}} .
$$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $e^{z_{\mathrm{M}}}$,

$$
s = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(z_{\mathrm{M}} - L)}} = \sigma(z_{\mathrm{M}} - L),
$$

where $\sigma(t) = 1/(1 + e^{-t})$ is the logistic function. The score is therefore a binary logistic output whose logit compares the melanoma logit with $L$, a smooth maximum of the eight other logits. A binary classifier has a single logit for "melanoma against everything else", and the loss gives it no information about which benign diagnosis an image has. The nine-class loss also requires the network to separate naevi from keratoses and lentigines, which adds supervision from the same images. In this sense the extra classes act as auxiliary tasks.

Because $\sigma$ is strictly increasing, the AUC of $s$ equals the AUC of $z_{\mathrm{M}} - L$. The calibration of $s$ does not affect the metric.
</Deep>

### Models, inputs and augmentation

The backbones were EfficientNet-B3 to B7, SE-ResNeXt-101 and ResNeSt-101, at input sizes from 384 to 896 pixels, cropped from JPEGs resized to 512, 768 or 1024 pixels.

<Foundation id="cnn-backbones" />

Some models had a **metadata branch** with 14 features taken from public notebooks: sex, age, anatomical site (one-hot encoded), the number of images of the patient, and the image size. The branch is a small fully connected network with Swish activations (Ramachandran et al., 2017), and its output is concatenated with the image features before the classification layer.

The augmentations (from the albumentations library) were transposition and flips; brightness and contrast changes; one of motion blur, median blur, Gaussian blur or Gaussian noise; one of optical, grid or elastic distortion; CLAHE (contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalisation); hue, saturation and value shifts; shift, scale and rotation; and cutout of a square about 37.5% of the image side. Most were applied with probabilities between 0.5 and 0.85. Dermoscopic images have no canonical orientation, and their colour varies with the device and the lighting, which is consistent with the choice of strong geometric and colour augmentations.

<Foundation id="data-augmentation" />

<Deep title="The augmentation pipeline" kind="code">

A Nolad illustration: the augmentations listed in the write-up, written with albumentations. The probabilities and the main limits (brightness and contrast 0.2, CLAHE clip limit 4, HSV shifts 10, 20, 10, shift-scale-rotate 0.1, 0.1, 15°, cutout 37.5%) follow the write-up; the other parameter values are illustrative. The exact configuration is in the [winners' repository](https://github.com/haqishen/SIIM-ISIC-Melanoma-Classification-1st-Place-Solution).

```python
import albumentations as A

def train_transforms(size):
    return A.Compose([
        A.Transpose(p=0.5), A.VerticalFlip(p=0.5), A.HorizontalFlip(p=0.5),
        A.RandomBrightness(limit=0.2, p=0.75), A.RandomContrast(limit=0.2, p=0.75),
        A.OneOf([A.MotionBlur(blur_limit=5), A.MedianBlur(blur_limit=5),
                 A.GaussianBlur(blur_limit=5), A.GaussNoise(var_limit=(5.0, 30.0))], p=0.7),
        A.OneOf([A.OpticalDistortion(distort_limit=1.0),
                 A.GridDistortion(num_steps=5, distort_limit=1.0),
                 A.ElasticTransform(alpha=3)], p=0.7),
        A.CLAHE(clip_limit=4.0, p=0.7),
        A.HueSaturationValue(hue_shift_limit=10, sat_shift_limit=20, val_shift_limit=10, p=0.5),
        A.ShiftScaleRotate(shift_limit=0.1, scale_limit=0.1, rotate_limit=15, border_mode=0, p=0.85),
        A.Resize(size, size),
        A.Cutout(max_h_size=int(size * 0.375), max_w_size=int(size * 0.375), num_holes=1, p=0.7),
        A.Normalize(),
    ])
```

### Ensembling by rank averaging

Each model was evaluated over its 5 folds with 8 test-time augmentation (TTA) views, flips and transpositions. The predictions of each model were then converted to **percentile ranks** (`rank(pct=True)` in pandas) before averaging across models. Different models produce probabilities on different scales; a nine-class model and a model with a metadata branch, for example, may concentrate their melanoma probabilities in different ranges. When raw probabilities are averaged, the model whose scores are most spread out has the largest influence on the order of the average. The AUC depends only on the order of the scores, and averaging ranks gives each model equal influence on that order.

<Foundation id="ensembling-tta" />

<Deep title="Rank averaging and AUC" kind="math">

Let model $m \in \{1, \dots, M\}$ assign score $s^{(m)}_i$ to test image $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$. Its percentile rank is

$$
r^{(m)}_i = \frac{1}{N}\,\#\{\, j : s^{(m)}_j \le s^{(m)}_i \,\} \in (0, 1],
$$

and the ensemble score is the mean rank

$$
\bar r_i = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} r^{(m)}_i .
$$

The map $s^{(m)}_i \mapsto r^{(m)}_i$ is non-decreasing, so each model's own AUC is unchanged by the transformation (apart from the handling of ties). Without ties, the ranks of every model take the values $1/N, 2/N, \dots, 1$, so all models have the same distribution of values in the average, whatever their calibration.

**Example** (hypothetical numbers). Model A concentrates its probabilities near 0, and model B spreads them over $[0, 1]$. For two images, the scores and percentile ranks are:

| | A: probability | A: rank | B: probability | B: rank |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Image 1 | 0.001 | 0.40 | 0.60 | 0.55 |
| Image 2 | 0.002 | 0.70 | 0.30 | 0.45 |

The raw averages are $(0.001 + 0.60)/2 = 0.3005$ and $(0.002 + 0.30)/2 = 0.151$, so image 1 is ranked higher and model B alone determines the order. The rank averages are $(0.40 + 0.55)/2 = 0.475$ and $(0.70 + 0.45)/2 = 0.575$, so image 2 is ranked higher: model A separates the two images by 0.30 in rank and model B by only 0.10.

Ranks discard confidence. Two lesions that one model places in its top 1% receive nearly equal ranks even if their probabilities are 0.5 and 0.99. This has no effect on the AUC, but a clinical threshold requires calibrated probabilities, which have to be estimated separately.
</Deep>

## Comparison with other competitions

- **ISIC 2024** concerned the same disease with a different kind of image: crops from 3D total-body photographs, of lower quality than dermoscopic images, accompanied by many tabular features. Gradient-boosted trees on image-model outputs and metadata won that competition, and patient-level comparison was implemented as features that measure how a lesion differs from the same patient's other lesions.
- **APTOS 2019** also used data from an earlier competition. Its winner found that validation on the earlier data did not correlate with the leaderboard, and used the public leaderboard for validation.
- **RSNA breast cancer detection** had a similar class imbalance but a metric that depends on a decision threshold (probabilistic F1), and the winners' scores depended strongly on the threshold they chose.
