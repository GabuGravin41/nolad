## The problem in its field

According to the World Health Organization, breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide: about 2.3 million women were diagnosed in 2020 and about 685,000 died of it. In high-income countries, breast cancer mortality has fallen by around 40% since the 1980s, a decline attributed to screening combined with better treatment. Screening means that women without symptoms receive a **mammogram** every one to three years, and radiologists look for small, early cancers.

Screening is difficult for two reasons. Cancers are rare in a screened population, a few per thousand examinations, so a reader examines many normal studies for each cancer. And false positives are common: women are recalled for more imaging or biopsies that turn out benign, which causes anxiety and cost. Many countries also lack breast radiologists; some use double reading (two independent readers per study), which doubles the workload. An automated reader that is accurate enough could act as a second reader or triage normal studies.

<Deep title="What a screening mammogram contains" kind="domain">

A standard screening examination has **four images**: two views of each breast.

- **CC (craniocaudal)**: the X-ray passes from top to bottom through the compressed breast.
- **MLO (mediolateral oblique)**: the X-ray passes at an angle from the centre of the chest outwards, which includes the upper-outer part of the breast and part of the pectoral muscle.

Radiologists compare the two views of the same breast (a real mass appears in both), compare left with right (the breasts should be roughly symmetric), and compare with earlier examinations. Signs of cancer include masses with irregular or spiculated margins, clusters of fine calcifications, architectural distortion and new asymmetries.

The target features are small. Microcalcifications are a fraction of a millimetre across, and a mammogram detector has pixels of roughly 0.07–0.1 mm, so the original images are several thousand pixels on each side. Downsampling to typical ImageNet resolution erases these features, which is why resolution dominates this competition.

**Breast density** (the proportion of fibroglandular tissue, which appears white) both raises cancer risk and hides tumours, which also appear white. **BI-RADS** is the standard reporting scale for how suspicious a finding is.
</Deep>

## The data

The training set has about 54,000 images from about 12,000 patients, provided as DICOM files. Each image carries the patient, breast side (laterality), view, age, whether an implant is present, machine id, and for training only: the cancer label, biopsy and invasiveness, BI-RADS, density, and a "difficult negative case" flag.

Predictions are made per **breast**: a `prediction_id` such as `10006_L` combines the patient and laterality, and each breast has two or more images. About 2% of breasts in the training data are cancer-positive.

## The metric

The probabilistic F1 score replaces counts of true and false positives with sums of predicted probabilities. For breasts $i$ with labels $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ and predictions $p_i \in [0,1]$:

$$
\text{pTP} = \sum_{i:\,y_i=1} p_i, \qquad \text{pFP} = \sum_{i:\,y_i=0} p_i,
$$

$$
\text{pPrecision} = \frac{\text{pTP}}{\text{pTP} + \text{pFP}}, \qquad
\text{pRecall} = \frac{\text{pTP}}{\#\{i : y_i = 1\}}, \qquad
\text{pF1} = \frac{2\,\text{pPrecision}\cdot\text{pRecall}}{\text{pPrecision} + \text{pRecall}}.
$$

If every $p_i$ is 0 or 1, this is the ordinary F1 score.

<Foundation id="precision-recall-fbeta" />

<Deep title="Why binarising the predictions raises pF1" kind="math">

Take a model whose ranking is fixed and consider what the metric does with its probabilities.

Every negative breast adds its probability to pFP. With 98% negatives, many small probabilities add up: 10,000 negatives with an average prediction of 0.02 contribute pFP = 200, which may be several times the number of positives. Thresholding sets these to 0.

Every positive breast adds only its probability to pTP, while the recall denominator counts it as a full positive. A positive predicted at 0.6 counts as 0.6 of a true positive. Thresholding raises it to 1.

A small worked example. Suppose there are 20 positives and 980 negatives.

- **Soft predictions.** Positives average 0.5, so pTP = 10. Negatives average 0.03, so pFP = 29.4. Then pPrecision = 10/39.4 = 0.254 and pRecall = 10/20 = 0.5, giving pF1 = 2(0.254)(0.5)/(0.754) = 0.337.
- **Binarised at a threshold** that keeps 12 of the positives and 18 of the negatives. Now pTP = 12 and pFP = 18, so precision = 0.4, recall = 0.6 and F1 = 2(0.4)(0.6)/1.0 = 0.48.

The ranking is the same; only the conversion to a decision changed. Because of this, the competition was decided largely by the choice of threshold, which is estimated from a small number of positives (a few hundred) and is therefore noisy. Both top write-ups make this point.

At the optimum, F1 behaves as follows. With counts TP, FP, FN, $F_1 = 2\text{TP}/(2\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN})$. Lowering the threshold to admit one more case increases F1 if it is a positive and decreases it if it is a negative; so the optimal threshold is where the local fraction of positives among newly admitted cases falls to about $F_1^*/2$ (a known result for F1-optimal thresholds, Lipton et al., 2014). With $F_1^* \approx 0.5$, the model should flag cases whose chance of cancer exceeds about 25%, far above the 2% base rate: the metric favours precision.
</Deep>

The winner notes that pF1 favours precision over recall, and that he would not use it to choose an operating point for real screening, where missing a cancer is the more serious error.

### Step 1: crop the breast

Much of a mammogram is black background, and the breast position varies. Cropping to the breast lets the network spend its pixels on tissue. The winner trained a **YOLOX-nano** detector at 416 × 416 on 571 boxes: 472 from a public dataset published by another competitor (Remek Kinas) and 99 he labelled himself. The detector reaches an average precision around 94–96%. If it finds nothing, an Otsu threshold with contour detection provides the crop; if that fails, the whole image is used.

<Foundation id="detection-basics" />

Inference decodes the DICOM images on the GPU to meet the time limit: the images are large (often JPEG 2000 compressed), and CPU decoding alone would take most of the allowed time.

### Step 2: the classifier

The backbone is **ConvNeXt-v1-small**, ImageNet-22k pretrained and fine-tuned on ImageNet-1k at 384 (`convnext_small.fb_in22k_ft_in1k_384` in timm). He also tried EfficientNet-B2/B4 and EfficientNetV2-S; ConvNeXt-small was the best and the most stable. Input size is **2048 × 1024**, about 14 times as many pixels as a 384 × 384 input.

<Foundation id="cnn-backbones" />

Heavy regularisation: dropout 0.5 before the classifier and stochastic depth (drop path) 0.2. Augmentation: a custom random crop without resizing, horizontal and vertical flips, downscaling, tone curve and brightness-contrast changes, shift-scale-rotate, elastic and grid distortion, and coarse dropout.

<Foundation id="data-augmentation" />

**Global max pooling** replaces the usual average pooling before the classifier. A cancer occupies a tiny region of the image; average pooling dilutes its features with those of thousands of normal locations, while max pooling passes on the strongest local response. The winner ranked generalised-mean and max pooling above average pooling and used max pooling in the final models.

<Deep title="Average, max and generalised-mean pooling" kind="math">

A backbone produces a feature map $F \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$. Pooling reduces each channel $c$ to one number.

- Average: $z_c = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{h,w} F_{c,h,w}$.
- Max: $z_c = \max_{h,w} F_{c,h,w}$.
- Generalised mean (GeM), for non-negative features and $q \ge 1$: $z_c = \left(\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{h,w} F_{c,h,w}^{\,q}\right)^{1/q}$.

GeM equals average pooling at $q = 1$ and tends to max pooling as $q \to \infty$, because the largest term dominates the sum of $q$-th powers.

Suppose one location out of $HW = 2048$ (a 64 × 32 feature map from a 2048 × 1024 input at stride 32) has a strong response $a$ for a cancer channel and the rest have a small background value $b$. Average pooling gives $b + (a-b)/2048$, almost indistinguishable from a normal image. Max pooling gives $a$. With a lesion covering several feature-map cells, the ratio improves, but only by a factor of a few. For a small target in a large image, some form of max-like pooling is the natural choice; this is the same reasoning as multiple-instance learning, where a bag is positive if any instance is.
</Deep>

### Step 3: imbalance and the soft positive target

With 2% positives and batches of only 8 images (the memory limit at 2048 × 1024), many batches would contain no positive at all. The winner used a sampler with a positive-to-negative ratio of 1:7, which gives on average one positive per batch, and added a guarantee of at least one positive in each batch. He reports preferring the smallest positive fraction that still achieved this. Heavier oversampling shows the model the same few hundred cancers many more times per epoch, which encourages memorising them.

<Foundation id="soft-labels" />

The positive label was **softened to 0.8** while negatives stayed at 0. His fold-0 comparison:

| Target | PR-AUC | ROC-AUC | best pF1 | best threshold |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Label smoothing 0.1 (both classes) | 0.4755 | 0.9278 | 0.497 | 0.66 |
| Positive target 0.8, negative 0 | 0.5401 | 0.9281 | 0.5714 | 0.49 |

His later ablation reported the soft positive target was not clearly better than ordinary label smoothing across all folds, so the table should be read as one fold, not a general law.

<Deep title="What a soft target does to the optimum" kind="math">

For one example with target $t \in [0,1]$ and prediction $p = \sigma(z)$, the binary cross-entropy is
$$
L(p) = -\big[t \log p + (1-t)\log(1-p)\big].
$$
Its derivative with respect to $p$ is
$$
\frac{dL}{dp} = -\frac{t}{p} + \frac{1-t}{1-p}.
$$
Setting this to zero: $t(1-p) = (1-t)p$, so $t - tp = p - tp$, and $p^* = t$. With respect to the logit, using $dp/dz = p(1-p)$:
$$
\frac{dL}{dz} = \frac{dL}{dp}\cdot p(1-p) = -t(1-p) + (1-t)p = p - t.
$$
With $t = 1$, the gradient $p - 1$ is non-zero for every finite logit, so training keeps pushing positive logits upwards and the model becomes overconfident on the few training positives it has memorised. With $t = 0.8$, the gradient vanishes at $p = 0.8$ ($z = \ln 4 \approx 1.39$), and positives that are already well classified stop pulling on the weights. Negatives keep target 0, so the model is still pushed to be confident about normal breasts, which make up 98% of the data and where confident zeros directly reduce pFP.
</Deep>

### Step 4: external data

The winner added every public mammography dataset he could find:

| Dataset | Patients | Images | Positive images |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| VinDr-Mammo (BI-RADS 5 treated as cancer) | 5,000 | 20,000 | 226 |
| MiniDDSM | 1,952 | 7,808 | 1,480 |

| CDD-CESM (low-energy images only) | 326 | 1,003 | 331 |
| BMCD | 82 | 328 | 22 |
| **Total** | 9,135 | 34,341 | 4,691 (13.7%) |

Each training fold combined three folds of competition data with all external data, about 75,400 images of which about 7.4% were positive, against about 2% in the competition data alone. The external sets are much richer in cancers: they come from diagnostic collections, not screening. His ablation credits external data with about +0.02 F1 both out-of-fold and on the private leaderboard.

### Step 5: choosing checkpoints and the threshold

Four folds gave four models, each with several saved checkpoints. He evaluated 525 combinations of checkpoints on the out-of-fold predictions; the best combination scored pF1 0.5187 and the worst 0.4951. Per breast, the mean over its images gave the best aggregation.

Then the threshold. His table:

| Threshold | OOF pF1 | Public LB | Private LB |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **0.31** (selected) | 0.5049 | 0.61 | **0.55** |
| 0.34 | 0.5187 | 0.58 | 0.53 |
| 0.37 | 0.50 | 0.55 | 0.52 |
| 0.40 | 0.4896 | 0.54 | 0.50 |

The threshold with the best out-of-fold score (0.34) was not the one he submitted. He chose 0.31 because it scored best on the public leaderboard, and it turned out also to be best on the private set. He writes that he was lucky and that many teams had better models that they did not select.

## Second place: pre-train, fine-tune, then combine views

The CancerDetectMan team (sakaku and kapenon of Rist Inc.) also used ConvNeXt-v1-small, via mmclassification, and built models in stages:

1. **Single-view pre-training** at 1280 × 1280 with external data.
2. **Fine-tuning** at 1536 × 1536 on competition data only.
3. **Dual-view and four-view models** that combine the CC and MLO images of a breast, and in the "multi-laterality" variant compare the left and right breast.

A Faster R-CNN trained on about 300 hand-drawn boxes (tight around the breast, excluding the nipple) supplied crops, although the final model used no cropping because the size of the breast itself may carry information.

**Auxiliary targets.** The main loss was Equalization Loss (Tan et al., 2020), designed for long-tailed class distributions, on the cancer label. Auxiliary heads with weight 0.1 predicted BI-RADS, density, the "difficult negative" flag, the view, and invasiveness for positive cases. The dual-view model also received age, implant status and machine id.

<Foundation id="softmax-cross-entropy" />

**Large batch.** AdamW at learning rate $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$, cosine schedule, 24 epochs and an effective batch size of 192 through gradient accumulation, which they describe as important. EMA of weights; test-time augmentation with a diagonal flip.

| Model | Public | Private |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single view, no external data | 0.57 | 0.51 |
| Single view, with external data | 0.58 | 0.53 |
| Dual view | 0.57 | 0.52 |
| Multi-laterality dual view | 0.52 | 0.53 |

They submitted a single high-resolution model rather than an ensemble at lower resolution, and state that it reproduces on one RTX 3090. Their list of things that did not help is long: external data in the fine-tuning stage, rule-based cropping, upsampling positives, EfficientNet, SE-ResNeXt and Swin backbones, mixup, max pooling, the Lion optimiser, pseudo-labels for BI-RADS and density, per-machine models and SWA.

## How this compares with other competitions

- **ISIC 2024 (skin lesion crops)** faced the same extreme imbalance but worked with small images and many tabular features; gradient-boosted trees on image-model outputs won there. Mammograms carry the evidence in fine detail within one large image, so the image model itself had to carry the result.
- **UBC-OCEAN and prostate grading (PANDA)** also work with very large images, but there the images are too big even for 2048-pixel inputs and must be tiled, which turns the problem into multiple-instance learning. Mammograms fit into one GPU pass at 2048 × 1024, and max pooling approximates the MIL "any instance" rule inside a single network.
- **The metric** is the largest difference. Competitions scored by AUC (ISIC, melanoma 2020) reward ranking; pF1 rewards the choice of operating point, which made the leaderboard noisy.
