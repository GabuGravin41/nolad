## Clinical background

According to the World Health Organization, breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide: about 2.3 million women were diagnosed in 2020 and about 685,000 died of it. In high-income countries, breast cancer mortality has fallen by about 40% since the 1980s, which the competition description attributes to screening combined with better treatment. In screening, women without symptoms receive a **mammogram** at regular intervals, typically every one to three years, and radiologists look for small, early cancers.

Cancers are rare in a screened population, of the order of a few per thousand examinations, so a reader examines many normal studies for each cancer. False positives are common: women are recalled for further imaging or biopsies that turn out benign, which causes anxiety and cost. The competition description also cites a shortage of radiologists. Some programmes use double reading, in which two radiologists read each study independently. An automated reader of sufficient accuracy could act as the second reader, or could sort out studies that are clearly normal.

<Deep title="Contents of a screening mammogram" kind="domain">

A standard screening examination has **four images**: two views of each breast.

- **CC (craniocaudal)**: the X-ray passes from top to bottom through the compressed breast.
- **MLO (mediolateral oblique)**: the X-ray passes at an angle from the centre of the chest outwards, which includes the upper-outer part of the breast and part of the pectoral muscle.

Radiologists compare the two views of the same breast (a real mass appears in both), compare left with right (the breasts should be roughly symmetric), and compare with earlier examinations. Signs of cancer include masses with irregular or spiculated margins, clusters of fine calcifications, architectural distortion and new asymmetries.

The target features are small. Microcalcifications are a fraction of a millimetre across, and a mammography detector has pixels of roughly 0.07–0.1 mm, so the original images are several thousand pixels on each side. A calcification 0.3 mm across covers 3–4 pixels at full resolution. At 384 pixels across a 250 mm image, one pixel spans about 0.65 mm, and the same calcification covers less than one pixel.

**Breast density** (the proportion of fibroglandular tissue, which appears white) both raises cancer risk and hides tumours, which also appear white. **BI-RADS** is the standard reporting scale for how suspicious a finding is.
</Deep>

## Data

The training set has 54,706 images from about 11,900 patients, provided as DICOM files. Each image carries the patient, breast side (laterality), view, age, whether an implant is present and the machine id. The training data also give the cancer label, biopsy and invasiveness, BI-RADS, density, and a "difficult negative case" flag.

Predictions are made per **breast**. A `prediction_id` such as `10006_L` combines the patient and laterality, and each breast has two or more images. About 2% of breasts in the training data are cancer-positive. With about 11,900 patients and two breasts each, this is roughly $0.02 \times 23{,}800 \approx 480$ positive breasts.

## Evaluation metric

The probabilistic F1 score replaces the counts of true and false positives with sums of predicted probabilities. Let breasts be indexed by $i$, with label $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ and prediction $p_i \in [0,1]$, and let $P = \#\{i : y_i = 1\}$ be the number of positive breasts. Define

$$
\mathrm{pTP} = \sum_{i:\,y_i=1} p_i, \qquad \mathrm{pFP} = \sum_{i:\,y_i=0} p_i .
$$

Probabilistic precision and recall are

$$
\mathrm{pPrec} = \frac{\mathrm{pTP}}{\mathrm{pTP} + \mathrm{pFP}}, \qquad
\mathrm{pRec} = \frac{\mathrm{pTP}}{P},
$$

and pF1 is their harmonic mean:

$$
\mathrm{pF1} = \frac{2\,\mathrm{pPrec}\cdot\mathrm{pRec}}{\mathrm{pPrec} + \mathrm{pRec}} .
$$

If every $p_i$ is 0 or 1, pTP and pFP are the counts TP and FP, and pF1 is the ordinary F1 score. The recall denominator $P$ counts every positive breast as 1, whatever its prediction.

<Foundation id="precision-recall-fbeta" />

<Deep title="Why binarising the predictions raises pF1" kind="math">

Consider a model whose ranking of the breasts is fixed, and the effect of the metric on its probabilities.

Every negative breast adds its probability to pFP. With 98% negatives, many small probabilities accumulate: 10,000 negatives with a mean prediction of 0.02 contribute $\mathrm{pFP} = 200$, which can be several times the number of positives. Thresholding sets these probabilities to 0.

Every positive breast adds only its probability to pTP, while the recall denominator counts it as a full positive. A positive predicted at 0.6 counts as 0.6 of a true positive. Thresholding raises it to 1.

A worked example with 20 positives and 980 negatives:

- **Soft predictions.** Positives average 0.5, so $\mathrm{pTP} = 10$. Negatives average 0.03, so $\mathrm{pFP} = 29.4$. Then $\mathrm{pPrec} = 10/39.4 = 0.254$ and $\mathrm{pRec} = 10/20 = 0.5$, giving $\mathrm{pF1} = 2(0.254)(0.5)/0.754 = 0.337$.
- **Binarised** at a threshold above which lie 12 of the positives and 18 of the negatives. Now $\mathrm{TP} = 12$ and $\mathrm{FP} = 18$, so precision is 0.4, recall is 0.6 and $\mathrm{F1} = 2(0.4)(0.6)/1.0 = 0.48$.

The winning write-up and the competition discussion report the same effect: binarising the predictions often raised pF1. The threshold is estimated from the out-of-fold predictions on roughly 480 positive breasts, so its estimate is noisy.

**Optimal threshold for F1.** Write the ordinary F1 score with counts TP, FP and FN (false negatives) as

$$
F_1 = \frac{2\,\mathrm{TP}}{D}, \qquad D = 2\,\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{FN}.
$$

Suppose the threshold is lowered just enough to admit one more breast, which has probability $c \in [0,1]$ of being positive. If it is positive, TP increases by 1 and FN decreases by 1, so the numerator becomes $2\,\mathrm{TP} + 2$ and $D$ becomes $D + 1$. If it is negative, FP increases by 1, so the numerator stays $2\,\mathrm{TP}$ and $D$ becomes $D + 1$. The expected new numerator is $2\,\mathrm{TP} + 2c$ in both cases over the same denominator $D + 1$. The change improves F1 in expectation if

$$
\frac{2\,\mathrm{TP} + 2c}{D + 1} > \frac{2\,\mathrm{TP}}{D} .
$$

Multiplying both sides by $D(D+1) > 0$:

$$
2\,\mathrm{TP}\cdot D + 2cD > 2\,\mathrm{TP}\cdot D + 2\,\mathrm{TP} .
$$

Cancelling $2\,\mathrm{TP}\cdot D$ and dividing by $2D$:

$$
c > \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{D} = \frac{F_1}{2} .
$$

A case should therefore be flagged when its probability of cancer exceeds half of the attainable F1 score, a result also derived by Lipton et al. (2014). With $F_1 \approx 0.5$, this threshold is about 0.25, more than ten times the 2% base rate. The metric favours precision.
</Deep>

The winner notes that pF1 favours precision over recall, and that he would not use it to choose an operating point for real screening.

### Stage 1: breast cropping

Much of a mammogram is black background, and the position of the breast varies. Cropping to the breast gives more of the network's input pixels to tissue. The winner trained a **YOLOX-nano** detector at 416 × 416 on 571 images with boxes: 472 from a public dataset published by another competitor (Remek Kinas) and 99 that he labelled himself, split into 521 for training and 50 for validation. The detector reached an average precision of 94.2–96.3% on the validation images. If it finds no breast, an Otsu threshold with contour detection provides the crop; if that also fails, the whole image is used.

<Foundation id="detection-basics" />

The winner's inference notebook decodes the DICOM images on the GPU.

### Stage 2: classifier

The backbone is **ConvNeXt-v1-small**, pretrained on ImageNet-22k and fine-tuned on ImageNet-1k at 384 pixels (`convnext_small.fb_in22k_ft_in1k_384` in timm). He also tried EfficientNet-B2 and B4 and EfficientNetV2-S; ConvNeXt-small gave the best and most stable results. The input size is **2048 × 1024**, which has $2{,}097{,}152 / 147{,}456 \approx 14.2$ times as many pixels as a 384 × 384 input.

<Foundation id="cnn-backbones" />

Regularisation consisted of dropout 0.5 before the classifier and stochastic depth (drop path) 0.2. Augmentation consisted of a custom random crop without resizing (scale 0.5–1, aspect ratio 0.5–0.8, probability 0.4), horizontal and vertical flips, downscaling, tone-curve and brightness–contrast changes, shift-scale-rotate, elastic and grid distortion, and coarse dropout.

<Foundation id="data-augmentation" />

**Global max pooling** replaces the usual average pooling before the classifier. A cancer occupies a small region of the image. Average pooling mixes its features with those of all other locations, while max pooling passes on the strongest local response. The winner's expectation was that generalised-mean pooling would be best, followed by max and then average pooling; the final models used max pooling.

<Deep title="Average, max and generalised-mean pooling" kind="math">

A backbone produces a feature map $F \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ with $C$ channels on an $H \times W$ grid. Pooling reduces each channel $c \in \{1, \dots, C\}$ to one number $z_c$:

$$
z_c^{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{h,w} F_{c,h,w},
\qquad
z_c^{\max} = \max_{h,w} F_{c,h,w} .
$$

For non-negative features and an exponent $q \ge 1$, the generalised mean (GeM) is

$$
z_c^{\mathrm{GeM}} = \Big(\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{h,w} F_{c,h,w}^{\,q}\Big)^{1/q}.
$$

GeM equals average pooling at $q = 1$ and tends to max pooling as $q \to \infty$, because the largest term dominates the sum of $q$-th powers.

At stride 32, a 2048 × 1024 input gives a feature map of $64 \times 32$, so $HW = 2048$. Suppose one location has a strong response $a$ in a channel that responds to cancer, and the other 2,047 locations have a background value $b$. Average pooling gives

$$
z^{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{a + 2047\,b}{2048} = b + \frac{a - b}{2048},
$$

which differs from the value $b$ of a normal image by $(a-b)/2048$. Max pooling gives $a$. If the lesion covers $k$ cells, the difference under average pooling becomes $k(a-b)/2048$, which is still small for a few cells. Treating the image as a bag of locations that is positive if any location is positive is the assumption of multiple-instance learning, and max pooling implements it within one network.
</Deep>

### Class imbalance and the soft positive target

The winner trained with batches of 8 images. With 2% positives, many such batches would contain no positive. If positives occurred at their natural rate, the probability that a batch of 8 contains none would be

$$
(1 - 0.02)^8 = 0.98^8 \approx 0.85 .
$$

The winner used a sampler with a positive-to-negative ratio of 1:7, which gives on average $8 \times \tfrac18 = 1$ positive per batch, and required at least one positive in every batch. He reports preferring the smallest positive fraction that achieved this.

<Foundation id="soft-labels" />

The positive target was **softened to 0.8**, and negatives kept the target 0. His fold-0 comparison:

| Target | PR-AUC | ROC-AUC | best pF1 | best threshold |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Label smoothing 0.1 (both classes) | 0.4755 | 0.9278 | 0.497 | 0.66 |
| Positive target 0.8, negative 0 | 0.5401 | 0.9281 | 0.5714 | 0.49 |

In a later update to the write-up, he reported that across folds the soft positive target was not clearly better than ordinary label smoothing. The table above describes one fold.

<Deep title="Effect of a soft target on the optimum of cross-entropy" kind="math">

For one example with target $t \in [0,1]$, logit $z \in \mathbb{R}$ and prediction $p = \sigma(z) \in (0,1)$, the binary cross-entropy is

$$
L(p) = -\big[\, t \log p + (1-t)\log(1-p) \,\big].
$$

Its derivative with respect to $p$ is

$$
\frac{dL}{dp} = -\frac{t}{p} + \frac{1-t}{1-p}.
$$

Setting this to zero and multiplying by $p(1-p)$:

$$
-t(1-p) + (1-t)p = 0 .
$$

Expanding, the terms $\pm tp$ cancel, which leaves $p - t = 0$, so the minimum is at $p^* = t$.

With respect to the logit, the chain rule with $dp/dz = p(1-p)$ gives

$$
\frac{dL}{dz} = \frac{dL}{dp}\cdot p(1-p) = -t(1-p) + (1-t)p = p - t .
$$

With $t = 1$, the gradient $p - 1$ is non-zero for every finite logit, so training continues to push the logits of positive examples upwards, including those of the few training positives the model already classifies correctly. With $t = 0.8$, the gradient vanishes at $p = 0.8$, which corresponds to the logit

$$
z = \ln\frac{0.8}{0.2} = \ln 4 \approx 1.39 .
$$

Negatives keep the target 0, so training still pushes the predictions for normal breasts towards 0, and these predictions make up pFP.
</Deep>

### Checkpoint and threshold selection

The four folds gave four models, each with several saved checkpoints. He evaluated 525 combinations of checkpoints on the out-of-fold predictions; the best combination scored pF1 0.5187 and the worst 0.4951. For each breast, the mean over its images gave the best aggregation.

The threshold was chosen last:

| Threshold | OOF pF1 | Public LB | Private LB |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **0.31** (selected) | 0.5049 | 0.61 | **0.55** |
| 0.34 | 0.5187 | 0.58 | 0.53 |
| 0.37 | 0.50 | 0.55 | 0.52 |
| 0.40 | 0.4896 | 0.54 | 0.50 |

The threshold with the best out-of-fold score was 0.34. He submitted 0.31, which scored best on the public leaderboard, and it was also the best of the four on the private leaderboard. He writes that he was lucky and that many teams had better models that they did not select.

<Foundation id="cross-validation" />

Across the four thresholds in the table, the private score ranges from 0.50 to 0.55. Moving the threshold by 0.03, from 0.31 to 0.34, lowers the private score by 0.02, and the out-of-fold score, which is computed on roughly 480 positive breasts, ranks the two thresholds in the opposite order.

## Second place: pre-training, fine-tuning and multi-view models

The CancerDetectMan team (sakaku and kapenon of Rist Inc.) also used ConvNeXt-v1-small, implemented in mmclassification, and trained in stages:

1. **Single-view pre-training** at 1280 × 1280 with an external dataset (the collection published by the competitor pourchot).
2. **Fine-tuning** at 1536 × 1536 on competition data only.
3. **Dual-view and four-view models** that combine the CC and MLO images of a breast. The "multi-laterality" variant also compares the left and right breast of the same patient, whose images come from the same machine.

A Faster R-CNN trained on about 300 hand-drawn boxes (tight around the breast, excluding the nipple) supplied crops. The final model used no cropping, because the team considered that the size of the breast may carry information. Augmentation consisted of shift-scale-rotate at any angle, random flips, RandAugment and random erasing.

**Auxiliary targets.** The main loss on the cancer label was the Equalization Loss (Tan et al., 2020), designed for long-tailed class distributions. Auxiliary heads with weight 0.1 predicted BI-RADS, density and the "difficult negative" flag (each with Equalization Loss), and the view and, for positive cases, invasiveness (each with cross-entropy). The dual-view model also received age, implant status and machine id.

<Foundation id="softmax-cross-entropy" />

**Training.** AdamW at learning rate $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$, cosine schedule, 24 epochs and an effective batch size of 192 through gradient accumulation; the team describes the large batch as important. Weights were averaged by EMA, and test-time augmentation used a diagonal flip. Inference used Theo Viel's public notebook for fast DICOM decoding with DALI.

| Model | Public | Private |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single view, no external data | 0.57 | 0.51 |
| Single view, with external data | 0.58 | 0.53 |
| Dual view | 0.57 | 0.52 |
| Multi-laterality dual view | 0.52 | 0.53 |

They submitted a single high-resolution model in preference to an ensemble at lower resolution, and state that it can be reproduced on one RTX 3090. The following did not help them: external data in the fine-tuning stage, concatenated crops, rule-based cropping, upsampling of positives, EfficientNet, SE-ResNeXt and Swin backbones, mixup, max pooling, the Lion optimiser, pseudo-labels for BI-RADS and density, per-machine models, and SWA.

## Comparison with other competitions

- **ISIC 2024 (skin lesion crops)** had a similar class imbalance, but its images are small crops accompanied by many tabular features, and the winner combined gradient-boosted trees with image-model predictions used as features. In mammography the evidence lies in fine detail within one large image, and both top solutions here are image models without a tabular stage.
- **UBC-OCEAN and prostate grading (PANDA)** also involve very large images, which are too large even for a 2048-pixel input and were divided into tiles, making the problem one of multiple-instance learning. A mammogram fits into one GPU pass at 2048 × 1024, and max pooling applies the multiple-instance rule within a single network.
- **The metric.** SIIM-ISIC 2020 was scored by ROC AUC and ISIC 2024 by partial AUC above 80% sensitivity; both depend only on the ranking of the predictions. pF1 depends also on the conversion of probabilities into decisions, and the winner's threshold table shows the size of that effect.
