## Background

More than 70 million Deaf people use a sign language, and more than 1.5 billion people live with some degree of hearing loss (figures from the competition description). Speech recognition on phones is widely available; automatic recognition of sign language is less developed, in part because large public datasets did not exist.

**Fingerspelling** represents words letter by letter with hand shapes, one shape or short movement per letter. Within American Sign Language (ASL) it is used mainly for names, addresses, telephone numbers, web addresses and words without an established sign, which is also the kind of information entered into phones as text. The competition description gives an average fingerspelling rate of about 57 words per minute, against about 36 words per minute for typing on a phone keyboard.

<Deep title="Sources of difficulty in fingerspelling recognition" kind="domain">

- **Speed and coarticulation.** Fluent signers do not hold each letter. Hand shapes blend into one another, and some letters are visible for only one or two frames.
- **Similar hand shapes.** Several letters differ only in the position of the thumb or of one finger (for example A, S, T, M, N and E), and the difference can be hidden by the orientation of the hand relative to the camera.
- **Letters with movement.** J and Z are traced in the air.
- **Variation between signers** in speed, hand size, handedness and style.
- **Recording conditions.** Selfie cameras, varied backgrounds and lighting, and hands partly outside the frame.
</Deep>

## Data

Google released a corpus of more than three million fingerspelled characters produced by over a hundred Deaf signers and recorded with phone selfie cameras. The data were distributed as landmarks, not video. Each frame was processed with **MediaPipe Holistic** (version 0.9.0.1), giving 21 points per hand, 468 face points and 33 body pose points, each with coordinates $x$, $y$ and $z$. Frames in which a hand was not detected have missing values. Each sequence is labelled with the phrase that was spelled: a name, an address, a telephone number or a URL. A supplemental set added about 50,000 sequences covering only about 500 distinct phrases.

<Deep title="Landmark models" kind="domain">

A landmark model is a neural network that outputs the image coordinates of predefined points, such as fingertips and knuckles, lip corners or shoulders. The MediaPipe hand model predicts 21 points per hand: the wrist and four joints on each finger, with coordinates normalised to the image size. Using landmarks instead of pixels removes background, clothing and lighting from the input, reduces each frame to a few hundred numbers, and protects the identity of the signers. When the landmark model fails, for example on a fast or blurred hand, the downstream model receives missing or incorrect points and has no access to the pixels from which the information could be recovered.
</Deep>

## Evaluation metric

The **Levenshtein distance** $\mathrm{Lev}(a, b)$ between two strings is the minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions and substitutions that transform $a$ into $b$. For strings $a = a_1 \dots a_m$ and $b = b_1 \dots b_n$ it is computed by dynamic programming over the table $L(i, j) = \mathrm{Lev}(a_1\dots a_i,\ b_1 \dots b_j)$:

$$
L(i, 0) = i, \qquad L(0, j) = j,
$$

$$
L(i, j) = \min\big(\,L(i-1, j) + 1,\ \ L(i, j-1) + 1,\ \ L(i-1, j-1) + \mathbb{1}[a_i \ne b_j]\,\big),
$$

where the three terms correspond to deleting $a_i$, inserting $b_j$, and matching or substituting. The distance is $L(m, n)$, obtained in $O(mn)$ operations.

For example, $\mathrm{Lev}(\texttt{kitten}, \texttt{sitting}) = 3$: substitute k→s, substitute e→i, insert g.

Let the test set contain phrases $y_1, \dots, y_K$ with predictions $\hat y_1, \dots, \hat y_K$. With

$$
N = \sum_{k=1}^{K} |y_k|, \qquad D = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathrm{Lev}(\hat y_k, y_k),
$$

the total number of reference characters and the total edit distance, the score is

$$
\text{score} = \frac{N - D}{N} = 1 - \frac{D}{N}.
$$

A perfect submission scores 1. An empty prediction for every phrase gives $D = N$ and a score of 0; predictions can score below 0 if they are longer and mostly wrong. Because $N$ and $D$ are summed over the whole test set, long phrases carry more weight than short ones.

<Foundation id="sequence-decoding" />

**Submission format.** Participants submitted a **TensorFlow Lite model**, which Kaggle ran with TFLite runtime 2.14. The model received landmark frames and returned per-character probabilities, decoded by the evaluator with argmax. The file size was limited to 40 MB, and inference over about 35 hours of video had to finish within 5 hours. Every component of a solution, including preprocessing and decoding, therefore had to be expressed in TFLite operations and fit these size and time limits.

### Validation

The training data were split into four folds **by signer**, so that validation always measured performance on people absent from training. Early in the competition the score on fold 0 correlated closely with the public leaderboard, and the authors tracked mainly fold 0.

<Foundation id="cross-validation" />

### Input

Of the 543 MediaPipe landmarks, 130 were used: 21 per hand (42), 6 pose points per arm (12) and 76 face points around the lips, nose and eyes. Each coordinate was normalised to zero mean and unit standard deviation, and missing values were set to zero. Sequences longer than 384 frames were resampled to 384 frames by linear interpolation along time. Shorter sequences were padded to 384 frames for batching during training only; at inference, TFLite received each sequence at its true length.

### Augmentation

The authors describe augmentation as necessary both for generalisation to new signers and for training deep models. They used:

- **Temporal:** resampling along the time axis, shifts in time, and windowed time warping (some segments stretched, others compressed).
- **Spatial:** left–right flip of the landmarks; random affine transformation (scale, shear, shift, rotation).
- **CutMix in time:** a fraction $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ is drawn; two sequences are cut at that fraction of their lengths, the first part of one is joined to the second part of the other, and their phrases are cut and joined at the same fraction. Mixing sequences from the same signer gave the best results.
- **Finger dropout:** 2 to 6 fingers set to zero over 2 or 3 time windows.
- **Face or pose dropout:** all face landmarks, or all pose landmarks, set to zero.
- **Hand dropout:** all hand landmarks set to zero in about 5% of samples.
- **Masking** of windows in time and of groups of landmarks.

Most augmentations were applied to 50% of samples; resampling and the affine transformation to about 80%.

<Deep title="Rationale for finger dropout and same-signer CutMix" kind="detail">

When MediaPipe loses track of fingers, their coordinates are missing in the data. Finger dropout produces the same pattern during training, so the model learns to read letters from the remaining fingers and from context.

In time-wise CutMix, the label of a spliced sequence is the corresponding splice of the two phrases. This is approximately correct because fingerspelling proceeds letter by letter at a roughly constant rate within a phrase: if a sequence of $T$ frames spells a phrase of $n$ characters, the cut at frame $\lambda T$ falls near character $\lambda n$. Restricting the mixing to one signer keeps hand size, speed and camera position consistent across the cut.

In the authors' ablation, CutMix, finger dropout and face or pose dropout each added about 0.005 to the score.
</Deep>

### Feature extraction

A landmark sequence is treated as an image whose width is time (up to 384 frames), whose height is the 130 landmarks, and whose channels are the coordinates $x$, $y$, $z$. A 2D convolution with batch normalisation, followed by a linear layer over the flattened landmark axis, produces one feature vector per frame. There are five such modules. One reads all landmarks and outputs 208 dimensions. Four read one landmark type each (left hand, right hand, face, pose) and output 52 dimensions each; their outputs are concatenated into a second 208-dimensional vector. The encoder receives both vectors for each frame.

<Foundation id="convolution" />

### Encoder

**Conformer** blocks, developed for speech recognition (Gulati et al., 2020), combine multi-head self-attention, which relates all frames, with depthwise convolution, which captures local patterns, and place two half-weighted feed-forward modules around them (the "macaron" arrangement). **Squeezeformer** (Kim et al., 2022) reorganised these blocks and added temporal downsampling in the middle of the network. The winners kept the Squeezeformer block and made three changes:

1. **No temporal downsampling.** All layers operate at the input frame rate; downsampling gave no improvement.
2. **Rotary attention instead of relative positional encoding.** The relative positional encodings used in conformers are slow and add parameters in every layer. The authors used attention with rotary position embeddings (RoPE), as in the Llama language models, which made training about 2 times and TFLite inference about 3 times faster. The rotary embeddings are computed once and passed to every layer, which removed about 20% of the parameters.
3. **Learnable scaling in place of the layer normalisations** of the macaron modules, as proposed in the Squeezeformer paper.

<Foundation id="attention-transformers" />

<Deep title="Rotary position embeddings" kind="math">

Let $q, k \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ be a query and a key in one attention head, with $d$ even. Group the coordinates into $d/2$ pairs and write pair $m$ as a complex number,

$$
\tilde q_m = q_{2m} + i\,q_{2m+1}, \qquad \tilde k_m = k_{2m} + i\,k_{2m+1}, \qquad m = 0, \dots, \tfrac{d}{2} - 1 .
$$

Assign each pair a frequency

$$
\theta_m = b^{-2m/d},
$$

with base $b$ (10,000 by default), so that $\theta_0 = 1$ and the frequencies decrease geometrically. For a query at position $t$ and a key at position $s$, multiply each pair by a phase proportional to its position:

$$
\tilde q_m(t) = \tilde q_m\, e^{i t \theta_m}, \qquad \tilde k_m(s) = \tilde k_m\, e^{i s \theta_m}.
$$

Multiplication by $e^{i\phi}$ rotates a pair by the angle $\phi$ without changing its length. For real vectors $u, v$ written as complex pairs $\tilde u_m, \tilde v_m$, the dot product is $u \cdot v = \mathrm{Re}\sum_m \tilde u_m \overline{\tilde v_m}$. The attention logit of the rotated vectors is therefore

$$
\mathrm{Re}\sum_m \tilde q_m e^{i t \theta_m}\ \overline{\tilde k_m e^{i s \theta_m}}
= \mathrm{Re}\sum_m \tilde q_m \overline{\tilde k_m}\ e^{i t\theta_m} e^{-i s\theta_m}
= \mathrm{Re}\sum_m \tilde q_m \overline{\tilde k_m}\ e^{i (t - s)\theta_m}.
$$

The positions $t$ and $s$ enter only through their difference $t - s$, so the logit depends on relative position. The method adds no parameters, and the rotation angles $t\theta_m$ can be computed once for all positions and reused in every layer.
</Deep>

### Decoder

A two-layer transformer decoder, similar to the `Speech2TextDecoder` of the Hugging Face library, generates the phrase one character at a time while attending to the encoder output. With characters $y_1, \dots, y_n$ and encoder output $h$, it is trained with the cross-entropy of each next character,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dec}} = -\sum_{j=1}^{n} \log p_\theta\big(y_j \mid y_1, \dots, y_{j-1},\ h\big).
$$

The authors found this autoregressive decoder better than **connectionist temporal classification (CTC)**, "even in a setting where computational efficiency matters a lot". Adding CTC as an auxiliary loss lowered the score.

Two further details:

- **Reversed auxiliary decoder.** A causal decoder can predict later characters partly from the characters it has already generated, and depends on the encoder mostly for the beginning of the phrase. A second decoder is trained on the reversed phrase, which requires it to read the end of the phrase from the encoder. Its loss is added during training; it is not used at inference.
- **Masking of decoder inputs.** Randomly masking input characters of the decoder during training (+0.003) increases its reliance on the encoder.

At inference, generation stops when the end token is produced, and the attention keys and values of earlier steps are cached so that each step does not recompute them. Beam search was too slow for the time limit.

### Confidence head and fallback string

Some test sequences were unusable, for example a few frames with no detected hands. A linear layer on the first encoder output predicts a **confidence score**. Its training target was the normalised Levenshtein distance, clipped to $[0, 1]$, that an earlier model achieved on each training sequence in out-of-fold prediction.

When the confidence is below 0.15, or the sequence has fewer than 15 frames, the model outputs the fixed string `2 a-e -aroe`. This string was proposed in a public notebook by Anokas as having a small expected edit distance to the phrases in the data. The post-processing added 0.006 to the score.

<Deep title="Choosing a fixed output for unreadable sequences" kind="math">

For a sequence the model cannot read, the output $s$ that minimises the expected contribution to $D$ is

$$
s^\star = \arg\min_{s}\ \mathbb{E}_{y}\big[\mathrm{Lev}(s, y)\big],
$$

where $y$ ranges over the phrases in the data. Two reference points:

- The empty string gives $\mathrm{Lev}(\varepsilon, y) = |y|$, the length of the phrase.
- A fixed string $s$ of length $\ell \le |y|$ gives at most $|y|$: at most $\ell$ substitutions plus $|y| - \ell$ insertions. Each character of $s$ that can be aligned with an equal character of $y$ in the optimal alignment saves one edit.

A short string composed of characters that are frequent in the phrases (digits, spaces, hyphens, common letters), in the positions where they tend to occur, therefore has an expected distance below the mean phrase length. Summed over the unreadable sequences in the test set, the saving amounted to 0.006.
</Deep>

### Training

- Cosine learning-rate schedule over 400 epochs, peak learning rate 0.0045, 10 warm-up epochs, weight decay 0.08, dropout 0.1, effective batch size 512.
- **Mixed-precision (FP16) training.** The TFLite model runs in FP16, which halves its size. A model trained in FP32 and converted to FP16 afterwards lost about 0.01 between cross-validation and the leaderboard; training in FP16 from the start removed the difference.
- **Masking of padded frames.** Training batches are padded to 384 frames, while TFLite inference receives unpadded sequences. Every layer that combines information across time (the feature extractor and the macaron modules) was masked so that padded frames have no effect, which makes training and inference compute the same function. Keras provides such masking; in PyTorch the authors implemented it for each layer. The masking also allowed inference at variable length, which is faster.

The two final seeds together occupy 39,988 KB, 12 KB below the limit.

<Foundation id="learning-rate-schedules" />

### Supplemental data

The supplemental set contained 50,000 sequences but only about 500 distinct phrases, so a model trained on it can learn to recognise whole phrases instead of characters. The only use that helped (0.838 to 0.839) was to add, in each epoch, one randomly chosen sequence per phrase: about 500 supplemental sequences alongside about 50,000 training sequences.

### Ensembling

The two seeds are combined **at every decoding step**: both decoders compute logits for the next character, the logits are averaged, and the argmax of the average is fed back to both decoders as the next input. Averaging complete output strings is not defined, since the two strings can differ in length.

Four of the listed changes (FP16, rotary attention, masking and decoder caching) do not improve accuracy directly. They reduce computation and memory, and the authors used the savings to add layers.

### Changes that did not help

Using all supplemental data; edit distance as a training loss; CTC, including as an auxiliary loss; label smoothing; adversarial weight perturbation (which produced NaN values in FP16); test-time augmentation; hidden-layer mixup and SpecAugment++; beam search.

## Other top solutions

- **Second place (Hoyeol Sohn)** compared speech-recognition architectures and also selected a Squeezeformer. The same author later placed second in Stanford Ribonanza with a Squeezeformer adapted to RNA sequences.
- **Third place (gezi)** used a 17-layer Squeezeformer that kept temporal downsampling and used RoPE, and released code.
- **Fifth place (Jungwoo Park)** used a standard transformer with data2vec self-supervised pre-training, CutMix, and knowledge distillation from larger models into a model that met the size limit.

<Callout kind="deploy" title="Considerations for deployment">
Fingerspelling is one part of signing. A text-entry application also needs to detect where fingerspelling starts and stops within natural signing, to let users correct errors, and to be evaluated with Deaf users across ages, skin tones, lighting conditions and devices. The model was trained on landmarks from MediaPipe 0.9.0.1; another landmark model version changes the input distribution.
</Callout>
