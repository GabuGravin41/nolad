## Background

Sleep affects mood, attention and behaviour, and disturbed sleep is common in children with mental-health and learning difficulties. Measuring sleep in large studies is difficult. Polysomnography in a sleep laboratory is accurate but expensive and unnatural. Sleep diaries are cheap but unreliable, and cannot separate getting into bed from falling asleep. **Wrist-worn accelerometers** record movement continuously for weeks in a person's normal environment and are standard in large studies. Sleep times have been derived from their signals with hand-crafted rules, whose accuracy varies between individuals.

The Child Mind Institute provided recordings from the **Healthy Brain Network**, a large New York study of child and adolescent mental health, and asked for models that detect **sleep onset** and **wake-up** events.

<Deep title="What the accelerometer measures" kind="domain">

A three-axis accelerometer on the wrist measures acceleration along three directions, including gravity. Two summary signals were provided at 5-second steps:

- **anglez**: the angle of the arm relative to the horizontal plane, derived from the direction of gravity. It changes when the wrist rotates.
- **ENMO** (Euclidean norm minus one): $\max\big(0,\ \sqrt{a_x^2 + a_y^2 + a_z^2} - 1\big)$ in units of $g$, the acceleration beyond gravity. It is near zero at rest and rises with movement.

During sleep both signals are low for long periods, interrupted by occasional changes of posture. A widely used heuristic (van Hees et al.) marks sleep when the arm angle stays nearly constant for several minutes. Such rules have difficulty separating quiet wakefulness (reading, watching a screen) from sleep, and placing the transition at the correct minute.

Devices are sometimes removed. In this dataset, periods without wear show an exact repetition of values with a period of one day, an artefact of how the gaps had been filled.
</Deep>

## Data

Each training series is one participant's recording over many days, at one sample every 5 seconds (17,280 steps per day), with the two signals and a timestamp. Labels give, for each night, the step of sleep onset and of wake-up. Some nights have no labelled events, for example when the device was not worn. The hidden test set had to be processed in 9 hours.

## Evaluation metric

For each event type (onset, wake-up) and each tolerance $\tau$ in $\{1, 3, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, 15, 20, 25, 30\}$ minutes (12 to 360 steps of 5 seconds), predictions are matched to true events. Predictions are processed in order of decreasing confidence; each is matched to the nearest unmatched true event of the same series within $\tau$, if one exists. Matched predictions are true positives and unmatched ones false positives.

With the predictions sorted by confidence, let $\mathrm{TP}(k)$ be the number of true positives among the first $k$, $P(k) = \mathrm{TP}(k)/k$ the precision and $R(k) = \mathrm{TP}(k)/G$ the recall, where $G$ is the number of true events. The average precision at tolerance $\tau$ is

$$
\mathrm{AP}_\tau = \sum_{k} \big(R(k) - R(k-1)\big)\,P(k),
$$

a sum over the ranks at which recall increases, that is, over the true positives. The score is the mean of $\mathrm{AP}_\tau$ over the ten tolerances and the two event types:

$$
\text{score} = \frac{1}{2 \cdot 10} \sum_{e \in \{\text{onset},\,\text{wake-up}\}}\ \sum_{\tau} \mathrm{AP}_{\tau}^{(e)} .
$$

<Foundation id="precision-recall-fbeta" />

<Deep title="Properties of the metric" kind="math">

Three properties of the metric determine the design of the solutions:

1. **Low-confidence predictions cost little.** In the sum above, a false positive ranked below the last true positive does not change any term, because $R(k)$ no longer increases after that rank. A false positive ranked between true positives lowers $P(k)$ for the true positives that follow it. Submitting many candidate times per night with decreasing confidence therefore adds possible matches at little cost.
2. **Several tolerances.** A prediction 4 minutes from the true event is a match at tolerances of 5 minutes and above (8 of the 10) but not at 1 or 3 minutes. A prediction within 1 minute is a match at all ten.
3. **One match per event.** Once a true event has been matched at a given tolerance, a second prediction near it cannot match it and counts as a false positive. Of two confident predictions 2 minutes apart around one event, the second adds a false positive at every tolerance at which the first already matched.

The winning post-processing is designed around these three properties.
</Deep>

## Winning solution

The team of sakami, kami and shimacos built a first-level network, a second-level model and a procedure for selecting submission times. The first-level network alone scored 0.829 on the private leaderboard, which would have placed ninth; with the second level and the selection procedure the private score was 0.852.

### Level 1: CNN–GRU network

The architecture comes from a public notebook by danielphalen:

1. **Input scaling** with a squeeze-and-excitation module over the feature channels (`SEScale`).
2. **Downsampling convolutions**, reducing the number of time steps.
3. **A stack of residual bidirectional GRU layers**, reading the whole day in both directions.
4. **Upsampling convolutions** back to the input resolution.
5. **A minute embedding concatenated at the output layer.**

<Foundation id="recurrent-networks" />

<Deep title="Code: SEScale and the output-layer minute embedding" kind="code">

```python title="from the 1st-place write-up"
class SEScale(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, ch: int, r: int) -> None:
        super().__init__()
        self.fc1 = nn.Linear(ch, r)
        self.fc2 = nn.Linear(r, ch)

    def forward(self, x: torch.FloatTensor) -> torch.FloatTensor:
        h = self.fc1(x)
        h = F.relu(h)
        h = self.fc2(h).sigmoid()
        return h * x
```

`SEScale` computes, for each time step, a weight in $(0, 1)$ for each input channel from all channels together, and multiplies the channels by those weights. For an input vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^{c}$ at one time step, with weight matrices $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times c}$, $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{c \times r}$ and biases $b_1, b_2$, the output is $x \odot \sigma\big(W_2\,\mathrm{ReLU}(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2\big)$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $\odot$ the elementwise product. The model can thereby scale features up or down depending on the values of the other features.

```python title="forward pass (excerpt)"
def forward(self, num_x, cat_x):
    cat_embeddings = [embedding(cat_x[:, :, i]) for i, embedding in enumerate(self.category_embeddings)]
    num_x = self.numerical_linear(num_x)
    x = torch.cat([num_x] + cat_embeddings, dim=2)
    x = self.input_linear(x)
    x = self.conv(x.transpose(-1, -2)).transpose(-1, -2)
    for gru in self.gru_layers:
        x, _ = gru(x)
    x = self.dconv(x.transpose(-1, -2)).transpose(-1, -2)
    minute_embedding = self.minute_embedding(cat_x[:, :, 0])
    x = self.output_linear(torch.cat([x, minute_embedding], dim=2))
    return x
```

- Categorical features (hour, minute, weekday, non-wear flag) pass through embedding tables; numerical features through a linear layer; both are concatenated.
- `self.conv` and `self.dconv` are 1D convolutions over time; `transpose` moves the time axis where `Conv1d` expects it.
- The GRU layers run on the downsampled sequence, which is cheaper.
- The **minute embedding** is concatenated again immediately before the output layer. Competitors had observed that the labelled events were not distributed uniformly over the minutes of the hour. Supplying the minute directly to the last layer allows the model to represent this pattern without passing it through the recurrent layers.
</Deep>

**Features.** Categorical: hour, minute, weekday, non-wear flag. Numerical: anglez / 45; $\log(1 + \text{ENMO})$ clipped at 1 and divided by 0.1; rolling mean, standard deviation and maximum of anglez and ENMO over 12 steps (1 minute); rolling median over 5 minutes of the absolute change in anglez. Month and day of month were removed (this improved CV).

**Data preparation.** Each series is cut into chunks of about one day, with chunk starts offset by 0.35 days so that chunk boundaries fall at different times of day. Each epoch uses half of the chunks. At evaluation, predictions from overlapping chunks are averaged, and 30 minutes are removed from both ends of each chunk, where the network has little context.

### Decaying soft targets

A target equal to 1 at the single step of each event and 0 elsewhere gives very few positive steps. The winners used a **soft target** that is highest at the event and decreases with distance, built from the tolerances of the metric: for tolerance $i$ with window $w_i$ steps (12, 36, ..., 360), each step within $w_i$ of an event gets value $1 - i/10$, and the target is the maximum over tolerances. Steps within 1 minute get 1.0, within 3 minutes 0.9, and so on, down to 0.1 at 30 minutes.

<Deep title="Code: building the decaying target" kind="code">

```python title="from the 1st-place write-up"
tolerance_steps = [12, 36, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, 240, 300, 360]
target_columns = ["event_onset", "event_wakeup"]

train_df = (
   train_df.join(train_events_df.select(["series_id", "step", "event"]), on=["series_id", "step"], how="left")
   .to_dummies(columns=["event"])
   .with_columns(
       pl.max_horizontal(
           pl.col(target_columns)
           .rolling_max(window_size * 2 - 1, min_periods=1, center=True)
           .over("series_id")
           * (1 - i / len(tolerance_steps))
           for i, window_size in enumerate(tolerance_steps)
       )
   )
)
```

- The join puts a 1 in `event_onset` or `event_wakeup` at each labelled step.
- `rolling_max(2w − 1, center=True)` spreads each 1 to all steps within $w - 1$ steps on either side.
- Each spread version is scaled by $(1 - i/10)$, and `max_horizontal` keeps the largest value at each step: a staircase that falls from 1 to 0.1 over 30 minutes.

Then, every epoch:

```python
targets = np.where(targets == 1.0, 1.0, (targets - (1.0 / config.n_epochs)).clip(min=0.0))
```

Every value below 1 is reduced by $1/E$ per epoch, where $E$ is the number of epochs. A step with initial value $v < 1$ therefore reaches 0 after $\lceil vE \rceil$ epochs, and by the last epoch only the steps within 1 minute of an event remain positive.
</Deep>

Early in training the wide target provides many positive steps near each event. As the target narrows, the loss increasingly penalises scores away from the event minute, which sharpens the peaks of the output. The authors report the decaying target as the largest single improvement to the first-level model (CV 0.7510 to 0.7699).

### Level 2: event probability per minute

Ground-truth events were always at second 0 of a minute, and the metric cannot distinguish predictions within 30 seconds of the truth. The level-1 network outputs a score every 5 seconds and is trained to be positive in a window around events. The second level converts this into the probability that an event occurs **exactly at a given minute**.

1. Average the level-1 scores per minute.
2. Find peaks with `scipy.signal.find_peaks` (height 0.001, minimum distance 8).
3. Around each peak, take a window of 8 minutes before and after (overlapping windows merged). These windows contained 98.45% of true events.
4. For each candidate minute, aggregate features from the level-1 predictions and the raw signals, and train LightGBM and CatBoost to predict 1 at the exact event minute and 0 elsewhere. Also train small CNN–RNN, CNN and transformer models on the windows as sequences.

The authors report that the level-2 outputs were well calibrated without further adjustment and captured the dependence on the minute of the hour more fully than the level-1 model.

### Selection of submission times

With calibrated per-minute probabilities $q_m$, the team chose submission times greedily.

1. **Candidate times.** Any second within a minute scores the same, so consider two candidate times per minute, at 15 and 45 seconds, each able to match events in adjacent minutes at the tightest tolerance.
2. **Score a candidate.** For candidate time $t$ and each tolerance $w$, sum the level-2 probabilities of events within $w$ steps of $t$: this is the expected number of events that $t$ could match at tolerance $w$. Add up over the ten tolerances.
3. **Pick the best** candidate in each series and add it to the submission with that score as its confidence.
4. **Discount.** An event within $w$ of the chosen time will already be matched at tolerance $w$ and cannot match another prediction. For the next round, subtract the probability mass within $w$ of the chosen point from the tolerance-$w$ sums of all other candidates.
5. **Repeat** until enough points have been submitted.

Daily normalisation of the level-2 probabilities, computing score changes incrementally, and JIT compilation made this fast enough to run on every series.

<Deep title="Greedy selection with discounting" kind="math">

Let $q_m \in [0,1]$ be the level-2 probability that an event occurs at minute $m$, and let $W_w(t)$ be the set of minutes within tolerance $w$ of a candidate time $t$. Treating tolerances independently, the expected number of true events that $t$ can match at tolerance $w$ is

$$
v_w(t) = \sum_{m \in W_w(t)} q^{(w)}_m ,
$$

where $q^{(w)}_m$ is the probability mass at minute $m$ not yet claimed at tolerance $w$; initially $q^{(w)}_m = q_m$. The value of the candidate is $v(t) = \sum_w v_w(t)$, the expected number of (event, tolerance) matches it creates.

After a time $t^\ast$ is selected, an event within $w$ of $t^\ast$ is expected to be matched by $t^\ast$ at tolerance $w$ and cannot be matched again. The update

$$
q^{(w)}_m \leftarrow 0 \quad \text{for } m \in W_w(t^\ast),
$$

removes that mass for tolerance $w$ (the implementation subtracts it from the running sums), and the values $v(t)$ of all other candidates are recomputed. Selecting the largest remaining $v(t)$ at each step and ranking the selected times in the order of selection puts the times with the largest expected contribution at the top of the ranked list, where each true positive raises the precision of all later ones.

The procedure is a greedy approximation to the combinatorial problem of choosing a set of times and their order. It treats the tolerances independently and is not guaranteed to be optimal.
</Deep>

This post-processing raised the scores from 0.768 to 0.790 public and from 0.829 to 0.852 private.

## Other top solutions

Most top teams combined a 1D U-Net or GRU over the signals with post-processing designed for the metric: second place (k-mat); third place with a GRU, a U-Net and LightGBM; fourth place with a modified U-Net, a transformer and weighted boxes fusion adapted to events; sixth place with a BiLSTM U-Net; seventh place with a WaveNet. Chris Deotte's eleventh-place write-up describes a GPU pipeline comparing GRU, CNN and transformer models. In the winning solution, the second level and the selection procedure added 0.023 to the private score of the first-level model.
