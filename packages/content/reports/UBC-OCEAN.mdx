## Clinical background

Ovarian carcinoma is the most lethal cancer of the female reproductive system. Five common histological subtypes are distinguished. They differ in cell of origin, genetics, response to chemotherapy and prognosis, and the subtype is assigned by a pathologist from stained tissue sections. The competition description notes that underserved communities often have no access to a gynaecological pathologist.

<Deep title="The five subtypes" kind="domain">

| Code | Subtype | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HGSC | High-grade serous carcinoma | The most common subtype, usually diagnosed at an advanced stage; TP53 mutations are nearly universal, and some patients carry BRCA1/2 mutations. |
| CC | Clear-cell carcinoma | Cells with clear cytoplasm; associated with endometriosis; relatively resistant to standard chemotherapy. |
| EC | Endometrioid carcinoma | Resembles endometrial tissue; associated with endometriosis. |
| LGSC | Low-grade serous carcinoma | Slow-growing; occurs in younger patients on average; less responsive to chemotherapy. |
| MC | Mucinous carcinoma | Mucin-producing cells; has to be distinguished from metastases of gastrointestinal tumours. |

The competition also included **rare subtypes**, grouped as "Other", which occur only in the test set.
</Deep>

## Data

The data came from more than 20 centres on four continents, collected by the University of British Columbia, BC Cancer (OVCARE) and the Ovarian Tumor Tissue Analysis (OTTA) consortium. There are two kinds of image:

- **Whole-slide images (WSIs)**, mostly scanned at 20× magnification and often tens of thousands of pixels on a side.
- **Tissue microarrays (TMAs)**, small tissue cores scanned at 40×. The training set contains 25.

Each image has one subtype label. Rough tumour annotations were provided for some training images. All images were released as large **PNG** files, without the multi-resolution pyramid of standard slide formats and without metadata giving the physical size of a pixel. The winning team reports that memory was the limiting resource when reading them.

## Evaluation metric

Let $C = 6$ be the number of classes (five subtypes and "Other"). For class $c$, let $\mathrm{TP}_c$ be the number of test images of class $c$ predicted as $c$, and $\mathrm{FN}_c$ the number of test images of class $c$ predicted as another class. The recall of class $c$ and the balanced accuracy are

$$
R_c = \frac{\mathrm{TP}_c}{\mathrm{TP}_c + \mathrm{FN}_c}, \qquad
\mathrm{BA} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} R_c .
$$

Each class contributes $1/6 \approx 16.7$ percentage points at most, whatever its frequency. A classifier that never predicts "Other" has $R_{\text{Other}} = 0$, so its balanced accuracy is bounded by

$$
\mathrm{BA} \le \frac{1}{6}\,(1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 0) = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833,
$$

even if it classifies every common subtype correctly. No training image is labelled "Other", so this class cannot be learned directly.

## Winning solution

The winning team was from Owkin, a company that develops AI methods for pathology. Its solution uses **Phikon**, a pathology foundation model that Owkin had released publicly, as a frozen feature extractor. The write-up cites Occam's razor for the choice of a simple classification model on top. The winning submission ran in about 6 hours.

### Tissue detection

Tissue is detected on a thumbnail of each image by **Otsu thresholding** in the HSV colour space. Stained tissue has high colour saturation and the glass background has low saturation, so the saturation histogram has two modes, and Otsu's method chooses the threshold between them.

<Deep title="Otsu's threshold" kind="math">

Let a histogram of pixel values in $\{0, \dots, 255\}$ have relative frequencies $q_0, \dots, q_{255}$, with overall mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma_T^2$. A threshold $t$ splits the pixels into class 0 (values $\le t$) and class 1 (values $> t$), with class probabilities and means

$$
\omega_0(t) = \sum_{i \le t} q_i, \qquad \omega_1(t) = 1 - \omega_0(t), \qquad
\mu_0(t) = \frac{1}{\omega_0(t)} \sum_{i \le t} i\,q_i, \qquad \mu_1(t) = \frac{1}{\omega_1(t)} \sum_{i > t} i\,q_i .
$$

The total variance splits into a within-class part $\sigma_W^2 = \omega_0\sigma_0^2 + \omega_1\sigma_1^2$ and a between-class part $\sigma_B^2 = \omega_0(\mu_0 - \mu)^2 + \omega_1(\mu_1 - \mu)^2$, with $\sigma_T^2 = \sigma_W^2 + \sigma_B^2$. Otsu's method chooses the $t$ that minimises $\sigma_W^2$, which, because $\sigma_T^2$ does not depend on $t$, is the $t$ that maximises $\sigma_B^2$.

The between-class part has a simpler form. Write $d = \mu_0 - \mu_1$. Since $\mu = \omega_0\mu_0 + \omega_1\mu_1$ and $\omega_0 + \omega_1 = 1$,

$$
\mu_0 - \mu = \omega_1 d, \qquad \mu_1 - \mu = -\,\omega_0 d .
$$

Substituting into $\sigma_B^2$:

$$
\sigma_B^2 = \omega_0\,\omega_1^2 d^2 + \omega_1\,\omega_0^2 d^2 .
$$

Factoring out $\omega_0\omega_1 d^2$ and using $\omega_0 + \omega_1 = 1$:

$$
\sigma_B^2(t) = \omega_0(t)\,\omega_1(t)\,\big(\mu_0(t) - \mu_1(t)\big)^2 .
$$

This quantity is evaluated for each of the 256 thresholds with cumulative sums, so the search is fast.
</Deep>

### Tiling

**Standardising magnification.** Most WSIs are at 20× and the TMAs at 40×. The team cut TMAs into tiles of 448 pixels and resized them to 224 pixels, so that a TMA tile covers the same physical area as a 224-pixel WSI tile at 20× and cells appear at the same size. At test time, a logistic regression on ImageNet ResNet-18 features of the thumbnail decided whether an image was a TMA.

**Reading the PNG files.** The team wrote C code that uses libpng's `png_read_row` to read a large PNG one row at a time and write tiles to disk. The code was compiled inside the Kaggle notebook. The team estimates its speed as similar to that of `pyvips`.

**Tile budget.** A WSI has on average more than 10,000 tissue tiles of 224 pixels, and processing all of them was not possible within 12 hours. The team kept at most **200 tiles per WSI**, which is less than $200/10{,}000 = 2\%$ of the tissue of an average slide. Using the Ray library, they processed two images at a time on one P100 GPU, each process with half of the GPU and two CPU cores, which brought the run below 7 hours. The team reports memory, and not GPU speed, as the limiting resource.

### Feature extraction with Phikon

**Phikon** is a ViT-Base vision transformer (about 86 million parameters) pretrained with **iBOT**, a self-supervised method, on 40 million tiles from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Its pretraining used no labels. For each 224 × 224 tile it outputs a 768-dimensional embedding, so a slide with $n$ tiles becomes a matrix of shape $n \times 768$.

The team compared Phikon with other pathology feature extractors (CTransPath and Lunit's models) and with DINOv2. Phikon gave the best cross-validation results, and combining several extractors did not help.

<Foundation id="transfer-learning" />

<Deep title="The iBOT pretraining objective" kind="detail">

iBOT trains a student network and a teacher network, where the teacher's weights are an exponential moving average of the student's. It combines two objectives:

1. **Self-distillation on the whole image**, as in DINO. The student and the teacher see different augmented crops of the same image, and the student's output distribution over a set of learned prototypes is trained to match the teacher's.
2. **Masked image modelling.** Some patches of the student's input are masked, and for those patches the student is trained to predict the teacher's output tokens computed on the unmasked image.

The first objective favours global features that are invariant to the augmentations (colour jitter, cropping); the second favours features of local structure. Filiot et al. (2023) describe the scaling of this method to histopathology.
</Deep>

### Slide classification with CHOWDER

The label belongs to the slide and not to any individual tile, so the classifier has to combine up to 200 tile embeddings into one prediction. This setting is called multiple-instance learning (MIL). The team used models from Owkin's public HistoSSLscaling repository. In the write-up, **CHOWDER** (Courtiol et al., 2018) performed on par with more complex MIL models such as TransMIL and DTFD-MIL, especially when ensembled, and its predictions can be traced to individual tiles.

<Foundation id="multiple-instance-learning" />

<Deep title="CHOWDER in equations" kind="math">

Let $h_1, \dots, h_n \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$ be the tile embeddings of one slide, and let the classes be $c = 1, \dots, 5$.

1. **Tile scores.** A small network shared by all tiles (hidden width 192 in the winning configuration) maps each embedding to one score per class, $s_{i,c} \in \mathbb{R}$.
2. **Extreme layer.** For each class $c$, the $n$ scores are sorted in decreasing order, $s^{(1)}_c \ge s^{(2)}_c \ge \dots \ge s^{(n)}_c$, and the $R = 10$ highest and $R = 10$ lowest are kept:
$$
e_c = \big(s^{(1)}_c, \dots, s^{(10)}_c,\ s^{(n-9)}_c, \dots, s^{(n)}_c\big) \in \mathbb{R}^{20} .
$$
3. **Head.** An MLP with hidden width 96, dropout 0.3 and sigmoid activation maps $e_c$ to the logit for class $c$.

The highest scores carry the strongest evidence for a class in any part of the slide, and the lowest scores the strongest evidence against it. Tiles with intermediate scores, for example tiles of stroma or background, do not enter the head. The tiles that produced the extreme scores can be displayed to a pathologist.

Sorting makes the output independent of the order of the tiles, and the layer is defined for any number of tiles $n \ge 2R = 20$. The team did not tune these hyperparameters.
</Deep>

**Training.** Validation used stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated 3 times with different seeds, designed so that each test fold contained at least 60% of the TMAs. Within each split, the four training folds were divided 75/25 into training and validation data. Models were trained for up to 30 epochs with early stopping (patience 4) on validation balanced accuracy, with AdamW at a constant learning rate of $10^{-4}$ and weight decay $10^{-3}$, and with cross-entropy loss. The class imbalance was addressed in two ways: weighted sampling to balance the batches, and class weights in the loss.

### Ensembling

The team reports that CHOWDER is sensitive to its random initialisation. For stability, each model was an ensemble of **50 CHOWDER models that differ only in their initial weights**, trained together as one module, with their predictions averaged.

<Deep title="The ensemble wrapper from the winning submission" kind="code">

```python title="winning-submission notebook (excerpt quoted in the write-up)"
class ModelEnsemble(nn.ModuleList):

    def __init__(self, models: List[nn.Module]) -> None:
        super().__init__(modules=models)

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor, mask: Optional[torch.BoolTensor] = None) -> torch.Tensor:
        """Forward pass."""
        predictions, scores = [], []
        for model in self:
            logits_, scores_ = model(x, mask)
            predictions.append(logits_.unsqueeze(-1))
            scores.append(torch.mean(scores_, dim=1, keepdim=True).unsqueeze(-1))
        predictions = torch.cat(predictions, dim=2)
        scores = torch.cat(scores, dim=2)
        return predictions, scores

chowder_models = [Chowder(**chowder_kwargs) for _ in range(50)]
model = ModelEnsemble(chowder_models)
```

- `nn.ModuleList` registers all 50 models as submodules, so one optimiser over `model.parameters()` updates all of them on the same batches.
- `x` has shape `(batch, n_tiles, 768)`. `mask` marks padded tiles when the slides in a batch have different numbers of tiles.
- Each model returns class logits of shape `(batch, n_classes)` and per-tile scores. `unsqueeze(-1)` adds a model axis, and `torch.cat(..., dim=2)` stacks the outputs of the 50 models to shape `(batch, n_classes, 50)`.
- The per-tile scores are averaged over tiles (`dim=1`) and stacked in the same way.
</Deep>

<Source href="https://www.kaggle.com/code/jbschiratti/winning-submission">jbschiratti/winning-submission (Kaggle)</Source>

The repeated cross-validation gives $3 \times 5 = 15$ trained ensembles, or $15 \times 50 = 750$ CHOWDER models. The winning submission averaged **65 CHOWDER models** from selected repetitions and folds. Each was calibrated with a logistic regression on its internal validation data, and only models that performed well on their internal test fold were kept, a step the team calls model filtering.

### Detecting the "Other" class

No training slide is labelled "Other". The team assigned this class when the five-class prediction was uncertain, as measured by its **entropy**. For predicted probabilities $p = (p_1, \dots, p_5)$ with $p_c \ge 0$ and $\sum_c p_c = 1$,

$$
H(p) = -\sum_{c=1}^{5} p_c \log p_c ,
$$

with natural logarithms and $0 \log 0 = 0$. $H(p) = 0$ when all probability is on one class, and $H(p)$ reaches its maximum $\log 5 \approx 1.609$ when $p_c = 0.2$ for every class. Two intermediate examples:

$$
H(0.9,\ 0.025,\ 0.025,\ 0.025,\ 0.025) \approx 0.095 + 4 \times 0.092 = 0.464,
$$

$$
H(0.6,\ 0.1,\ 0.1,\ 0.1,\ 0.1) \approx 0.306 + 4 \times 0.230 = 1.228 .
$$

Slides with entropy above a threshold were labelled "Other". Without outliers in the training data, the threshold could not be validated locally and was tuned on the public leaderboard. It raised the public score from 0.59 to 0.64.

The team also tried the **variance of the predictions across ensemble members** as the uncertainty measure. That submission scored 0.59 public and 0.67 private and was not among the final selections.

### Results

| Submission | Public | Private |

| Winning submission (selected) | 0.64 | 0.66 |

| Cleaned notebook, after the competition | 0.63 | 0.66 |

Balanced accuracy in cross-validation was between 0.8 and 0.9, at least 20 points above the leaderboard scores. Cross-validation could not include the "Other" class; by the bound derived under "Evaluation metric", a perfect classifier of the five subtypes that never predicts "Other" would score 0.833. The team attributes the rest of the gap to differences between centres in tissue preparation, staining, scanners and magnification.

### Unsuccessful experiments

- Stain normalisation (Vahadane and Reinhard methods).
- More TMA tiles, which led to timeouts.
- Detecting normal slides with a tumour-fraction classifier trained on the provided annotations.
- Hyperparameter tuning with Ray Tune, which raised the cross-validation score and lowered the leaderboard score.

### Continued pretraining (not selected)

The team extracted 6.5 million tiles from the training images and continued iBOT pretraining from Phikon's weights for one epoch, adding four register tokens (extra learnable tokens in the vision transformer's input). One epoch took 2.5 days on two P100 GPUs. A submission using these adapted features scored 0.62 public and 0.67 private, and was not among the final selections.

## Comparison with other competitions

The PANDA prostate cancer grading competition of 2020 was held before pathology foundation models were available. Its winners trained CNNs on tiles end to end and removed training slides with noisy labels. In UBC-OCEAN the winning team trained no image model on competition data: the tile features came from a model pretrained without labels on TCGA, and only the small CHOWDER heads were fitted to the competition labels. The measured gap between cross-validation and the private test set (0.8–0.9 against 0.66) shows that the frozen features did not remove the effect of the new centres in the test set.

### Considerations for deployment

Phikon's weights and the HistoSSLscaling code are released for non-commercial use. A hospital research group can use them under that licence. A company needs a licence from Owkin or a different foundation model, and would then retrain the CHOWDER heads, which take minutes on precomputed embeddings. The "Other" threshold was fitted to the public leaderboard and has to be re-established on local data that contain rare subtypes.
