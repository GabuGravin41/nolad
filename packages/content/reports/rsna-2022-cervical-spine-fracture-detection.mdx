## Clinical background

The competition description cites more than 1.5 million spine fractures a year in the United States and more than 17,000 spinal cord injuries. The **cervical spine**, the part of the spine in the neck, is the most common site of spinal fracture. An unstable cervical fracture that is not recognised can lead to injury of the spinal cord and paralysis. CT has replaced plain radiographs as the standard imaging test after trauma to the neck, because it shows the bone in cross-section at high resolution.

The description states that fractures are harder to detect in elderly patients, whose bones are thinned by osteoporosis and deformed by degenerative disease. A model that assigns a fracture probability to each vertebra could act as a second reader of trauma CT scans.

<Deep title="Anatomy: the seven cervical vertebrae" kind="domain">

The cervical spine has seven vertebrae, numbered **C1 to C7** from the skull downwards.

- **C1 (atlas)** is a ring of bone without a vertebral body. It carries the skull.
- **C2 (axis)** has a peg of bone, the dens or odontoid process, which projects upwards through the ring of C1 and forms the pivot about which the head rotates.
- **C3 to C7** share one structure: a vertebral body at the front, a ring of bone (the neural arch) around the spinal canal behind it, and processes to which muscles attach. The spinous process of C7 is long and can be felt at the base of the neck.

Below C7 lie the twelve thoracic vertebrae, T1 to T12. A CT scan of the neck usually includes the base of the skull and the upper thoracic spine.

A fracture can involve any part of a vertebra. A displaced fracture is visible on many slices; a fracture without displacement may appear as a thin line through the cortical bone on a few slices only.
</Deep>

## Data

The RSNA, with the American Society of Neuroradiology (ASNR) and the American Society of Spine Radiology (ASSR), assembled about 3,000 CT studies from twelve sites in nine countries on six continents. Experts annotated the presence of fractures, their vertebral level and their location. About 2,000 studies (2,019) formed the training set.

Each training study has eight binary labels: `C1` to `C7`, one per vertebra, and `patient_overall`, which is 1 if any vertebra is fractured. Two further kinds of annotation exist for subsets of the training set:

- **3D segmentation masks** for 87 studies, in NIfTI format, which assign every voxel of each vertebra to its level.
- **Bounding boxes** around fractures on individual slices, for a subset of the positive studies.

Each study is a DICOM series of 200 to 300 axial slices. The raw training data occupy about 300 GB. Inference ran in a Kaggle notebook without internet access, within 9 hours.

## Evaluation metric

Let $i$ index the studies and $j \in \{1, \dots, 7, \mathrm{any}\}$ the labels of a study, where $j = 1, \dots, 7$ are the vertebrae C1 to C7 and $\mathrm{any}$ is `patient_overall`. Let $y_{ij} \in \{0, 1\}$ be the true label, $p_{ij} \in (0, 1)$ the submitted probability and $w_{ij} > 0$ a weight. The binary log loss of one prediction is

$$
\ell(y, p) = -\big[\, y \log p + (1 - y)\log(1 - p) \,\big],
$$

and the score is the weighted mean over all predictions:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{\sum_{i}\sum_{j} w_{ij}\,\ell(y_{ij}, p_{ij})}{\sum_i\sum_j w_{ij}} .
$$

The weight depends on the label and on its true value. The organisers did not publish the weights; the values used by competitors, inferred from submissions and discussed in the competition forum, are

| Label | $w$ if $y = 0$ | $w$ if $y = 1$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vertebra C1–C7 | 1 | 2 |
| `patient_overall` | 7 | 14 |

A positive label therefore counts twice as much as a negative one. For a study in which all eight labels have the same value, the patient-level label has the same weight as the seven vertebra labels together, since $7 \times 1 = 7$ and $7 \times 2 = 14$.

<Foundation id="weighted-log-loss" />

<Deep title="The optimal prediction under class-dependent weights" kind="math">

Consider one label. Let $q \in (0,1)$ be the probability that it is positive given the image, $w_1$ the weight of a positive and $w_0$ the weight of a negative. The expected weighted loss of predicting $p \in (0,1)$ is

$$
R(p) = -\,w_1\,q\log p - w_0\,(1-q)\log(1-p).
$$

Differentiating with respect to $p$:

$$
R'(p) = -\frac{w_1 q}{p} + \frac{w_0(1-q)}{1-p}.
$$

Setting $R'(p) = 0$ and multiplying both sides by $p(1-p)$:

$$
w_1 q\,(1-p) = w_0 (1-q)\,p .
$$

Collecting the terms in $p$:

$$
w_1 q = p\,\big[\, w_1 q + w_0(1-q) \,\big].
$$

Dividing by the bracket:

$$
p^* = \frac{w_1 q}{w_1 q + w_0(1-q)} .
$$

The second derivative $R''(p) = w_1 q / p^2 + w_0(1-q)/(1-p)^2$ is positive, so $p^*$ is the minimum.

Both label types in this competition have $w_1 / w_0 = 2$. Substituting $w_1 = 2 w_0$:

$$
p^* = \frac{2q}{2q + 1 - q} = \frac{2q}{1+q} .
$$

A probability $q = 0.1$ should therefore be submitted as $0.2 / 1.1 \approx 0.18$, and $q = 0.5$ as $1 / 1.5 \approx 0.67$.

The same result in terms of log-odds follows from $1 - p^* = w_0(1-q) / \big[w_1 q + w_0(1-q)\big]$:

$$
\log\frac{p^*}{1-p^*} = \log\frac{w_1 q}{w_0 (1-q)} = \log\frac{q}{1-q} + \log\frac{w_1}{w_0} .
$$

With $w_1 / w_0 = 2$, the optimal logit is the calibrated logit plus $\log 2 \approx 0.693$.

In the normalised score the weights also appear in the denominator $\sum_i\sum_j w_{ij}$. It depends on the true labels and not on the predictions, so it does not move the optimum. A model trained with the metric's weights in its loss learns $p^*$ directly; a model trained with unweighted binary cross-entropy learns $q$, to which the conversion above can be applied afterwards.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Relation between the vertebra labels and the patient label" kind="math">

`patient_overall` is 1 exactly when at least one of the seven vertebra labels is 1. Let $p_c \in [0,1]$ be the probability of a fracture of vertebra $c \in \{1, \dots, 7\}$. If fractures at different levels were independent given the image, the probability of no fracture would be the product of the seven probabilities of no fracture, and

$$
P(\mathrm{any}) = 1 - \prod_{c=1}^{7} (1 - p_c).
$$

For example, with $p_c = 0.05$ for every vertebra, $\prod_c (1 - p_c) = 0.95^7 \approx 0.698$, and $P(\mathrm{any}) \approx 0.302$.

Fractures at different levels need not be independent: one injury can involve adjacent vertebrae, and the evidence on one slice can affect the predictions for two neighbouring levels. A model that predicts the patient label from all vertebrae together is not bound to the independence assumption. The winner states that his patient-level model reads all seven vertebrae at once so that it can learn `patient_overall`.
</Deep>

### Stage 1: vertebra segmentation

A 3D U-Net receives the whole study resampled to $128 \times 128 \times 128$ voxels and outputs seven channels, one per vertebra. Two encoders from the timm library were used, ResNet18d and EfficientNetV2-S, each trained in 5 folds. The models were trained on the 87 studies with masks. They then predicted masks for all training studies and, at inference, for every test study.

<Foundation id="unet" />

<Foundation id="dice-and-overlap" />

Because each vertebra has its own output channel, the channel index gives the vertebral level of every segmented voxel. The resolution of $128^3$ serves to locate the vertebrae and define a crop around each; the classifiers in Stage 2 work on crops taken from the original slices.

### Stage 2: per-vertebra classification

Each study yields seven crops, so the roughly 2,000 training studies give about $2{,}019 \times 7 \approx 14{,}000$ vertebra samples, each with one binary label.

From each crop, **15 slices** are taken at equal intervals along the body axis. Each becomes an image with six channels:

- channels 1 to 5: the slice itself and the two slices above and the two below it, a **2.5D** input;
- channel 6: the **predicted mask** of the vertebra on that slice.

A crop around C5 also contains parts of C4 and C6. The author added the mask channel so that the classifier can tell which of the vertebrae in the crop the label refers to.

A 2D CNN computes a feature vector for each of the 15 slices, and an **LSTM** reads the sequence of 15 vectors and outputs the prediction for the vertebra. The author reports that 3D CNNs applied to the crops did not work well.

<Deep title="2D slices with a recurrent layer, compared with a 3D CNN" kind="detail">

The write-up does not analyse why the 3D models performed worse. Three differences between the two designs may contribute:

- ImageNet-pretrained weights are widely available for 2D backbones, which here process each slice at full in-plane resolution.
- A fracture line without displacement lies within a few slices, where a 2D convolution at full in-plane resolution can resolve it.
- A 3D network has more parameters to fit per training sample, and there were about 14,000 samples.

The 2019 RSNA intracranial haemorrhage winners used a comparable design, a 2D CNN per slice followed by a recurrent network, on whole head scans. Here it is applied to the crop of one vertebra.
</Deep>

### Vertebra-level and patient-level models

The author trained two types of classification model.

- **Type 1 (vertebra level).** The input is the 15 slices of one vertebra; the output is the label of that vertebra. Backbones: EfficientNetV2-S at 512 pixels and ConvNeXt-tiny at 384 pixels.
- **Type 2 (patient level).** The input is all $7 \times 15 = 105$ slices of a patient, read by one LSTM. The outputs are the seven vertebra labels and `patient_overall`. Processing 105 slices at once requires much memory, so this type uses small backbones (ConvNeXt nano, pico and tiny; NFNet-L0) and a batch size of 1.

<Deep title="Tensor shapes in a type-2 forward pass" kind="code">

A Nolad illustration of the tensor shapes; the winner's implementation is linked from his discussion post.

```python
import torch, torch.nn as nn, timm

class PatientModel(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, backbone="convnext_nano", in_ch=6, hidden=256):
        super().__init__()
        self.cnn  = timm.create_model(backbone, pretrained=True, in_chans=in_ch, num_classes=0)
        d = self.cnn.num_features
        self.lstm = nn.LSTM(d, hidden, batch_first=True, bidirectional=True)
        self.vert = nn.Linear(2 * hidden, 1)        # per-slice logit, pooled per vertebra
        self.any  = nn.Linear(2 * hidden, 1)        # patient_overall

    def forward(self, x):                           # x: (B, 7, 15, 6, H, W)
        B, V, S = x.shape[:3]
        f = self.cnn(x.flatten(0, 2))               # (B*105, d)
        h, _ = self.lstm(f.view(B, V * S, -1))      # sequence of 105 slices, C1 top to C7 bottom
        v = self.vert(h).view(B, V, S).amax(-1)     # (B, 7) one logit per vertebra
        a = self.any(h.amax(1)).squeeze(-1)         # (B,)  patient-level logit
        return v, a
```
</Deep>

### Ensemble and runtime

The final submission combined the following models.

| Stage | Models |
| --- | --- |
| Segmentation ($128^3$) | ResNet18d-UNet, 5 folds; EfficientNetV2-S-UNet, 5 folds |
| Type 1 classification | EfficientNetV2-S (512), 5 folds; ConvNeXt-tiny (384), 5 folds |
| Type 2 classification | ConvNeXt-nano (512), 5 folds; ConvNeXt-pico (512), 2 folds; ConvNeXt-tiny (384), 2 folds; NFNet-L0 (384), 2 folds |

This is 10 segmentation models and $10 + 11 = 21$ classification models. The submission took 7.5 of the 9 permitted hours. Most training was done on a workstation with two NVIDIA A6000 GPUs.

## Second place: 2.5D segmentation and a CNN with a bidirectional GRU

Team RAWE (Ryan R, Jackeylov3, Zhenghan Chen and Hao Chen) used the same two stages with different components.

**Segmentation in 2.5D.** A 2D U-Net from the segmentation_models_pytorch library, with an EfficientNet-B0 encoder, received three adjacent slices $(i-1, i, i+1)$ as channels and predicted nine classes: background, C1 to C7, and one class for all thoracic vertebrae T1 to T12. With the thoracic spine as a class of its own, T1 is assigned to that class and not to C7. The approach was adapted from a public 2.5D notebook by Awsaf for the UW-Madison GI tract segmentation competition. Training used AdamW with a cosine schedule and warm-up, and augmentation with horizontal flips, shift–scale–rotate, and grid or elastic distortion.

<Foundation id="data-augmentation" />

**Cropping.** A study has 200 to 300 slices, of which about 30 cover one vertebra. The team sampled **24** slices per vertebra at equal intervals (`np.linspace`). Because the data amount to about 300 GB, they stored the crop coordinates and not the cropped voxels.

**Classification.** The input is a stack of 24 slices, a tensor of shape $(B, 24, H, W)$. A CNN (EfficientNetV2-S or ResNeSt-50d) encodes each slice, a **bidirectional GRU** reads the sequence, and an attention layer pools it. The team reports that a custom initialisation of the GRU and spatial dropout improved the score, and they removed two training studies whose labels they judged to be wrong.

They did not try detection models trained on the bounding boxes, or transformer sequence models.

### Considerations for deployment

1. **Segmentation.** Every classification depends on the vertebra masks, and the segmentation model was trained on 87 studies. Its output should be checked on local scans, particularly those with surgical hardware or severe degenerative change.
2. **Patient groups.** The competition description names elderly patients as the group in which fractures are hardest to see. Performance should be measured separately in this group.
3. **Operating point.** The metric scores probabilities; it does not fix a threshold. The threshold for flagging a vertebra has to be chosen on local data for the required sensitivity.
