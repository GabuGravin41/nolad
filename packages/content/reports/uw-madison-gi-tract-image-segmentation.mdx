## Clinical background

The competition description cites about 5 million people diagnosed with cancer of the gastrointestinal tract worldwide in 2019, about half of whom are eligible for radiotherapy. Radiotherapy is typically delivered in sessions of 10–15 minutes, daily, for one to six weeks. The radiation beams are aimed at the tumour and away from the stomach and intestines, which are sensitive to radiation and change position from day to day as they fill, empty and contract.

An **MR-Linac** combines an MRI scanner with a linear accelerator. The tumour and the surrounding organs can be imaged at every session, and the treatment plan adapted to that day's anatomy. The University of Wisconsin–Madison Carbone Cancer Center has used one since 2015. Before each adapted treatment, a radiation oncologist outlines (contours) the stomach and bowel on the day's scan; according to the host, this can extend a 15-minute session to an hour. Automatic segmentation would provide a draft contour for the oncologist to review and correct.

## Data

The data are anonymised MRI scans of patients treated at UW-Madison, with 1 to 5 scans per patient, acquired on different days. Each scan is a 3D volume supplied as individual 2D slice images. Each slice has run-length-encoded masks for three organs: **large bowel**, **small bowel** and **stomach**. Many slices contain none of the three organs (those above or below the abdomen), or only one or two.

## Evaluation metric

The score combines an overlap measure and a boundary distance:

$$
\mathrm{score} = 0.4 \times \overline{\mathrm{Dice}} + 0.6 \times \big(1 - \overline{\mathrm{HD}}_{\mathrm{norm}}\big),
$$

where $\overline{\mathrm{Dice}}$ is the mean Dice coefficient and $\overline{\mathrm{HD}}_{\mathrm{norm}}$ the mean normalised Hausdorff distance. Dice is computed on 2D slices. The Hausdorff distance is computed on the 3D volume built by stacking the slices of a scan, with a spacing of 1 between slices, and with coordinates normalised by the image size so that the distance lies in $[0, 1]$.

<Foundation id="dice-and-overlap" />

**Slices without an organ.** For a slice on which the true mask $Y$ of an organ is empty and the prediction $X$ contains $|X| > 0$ pixels, the Dice coefficient is

$$
\mathrm{Dice} = \frac{2\,|X \cap Y|}{|X| + |Y|} = \frac{2 \times 0}{|X| + 0} = 0,
$$

whatever the size of $X$. Stray pixels on a slice where the organ is absent therefore give that slice the minimum Dice, and they also enter the Hausdorff distance of the volume (see below). The value for a slice on which both masks are empty, where the fraction is $0/0$, is set by the organisers' metric code.

<Deep title="The Hausdorff distance" kind="math">

Let $A$ and $B$ be non-empty finite sets of points in $\mathbb{R}^3$, here the voxels of the predicted and the true mask of one organ in one scan, and let $\|\cdot\|$ be the Euclidean norm. The **directed** Hausdorff distance from $A$ to $B$ is the largest distance from a point of $A$ to its nearest point in $B$:

$$
\vec d(A, B) = \max_{a \in A}\, \min_{b \in B}\, \|a - b\|.
$$

The **Hausdorff distance** is the larger of the two directed distances:

$$
d_H(A, B) = \max\big(\vec d(A, B),\ \vec d(B, A)\big).
$$

On the non-empty compact subsets of a metric space, $d_H$ is a metric: $d_H(A, B) = 0$ if and only if $A = B$, it is symmetric by construction, and it satisfies the triangle inequality. The construction is due to Felix Hausdorff, after whom the Hausdorff separation axiom in topology is also named (and, from that, the Hausdorff Space community).

**Dependence on the worst point.** $d_H$ is a maximum, so it is determined by a single point. Suppose the prediction matches the stomach exactly but adds a false-positive region of a few voxels 100 voxels away from the stomach. Dice changes little, because the region is a few voxels among thousands, while $\vec d(\text{pred}, \text{true}) = 100$ voxels and therefore $d_H = 100$ voxels. In the other direction, a missed part of the organ far from the predicted part, such as a bowel loop several slices away, makes $\vec d(\text{true}, \text{pred})$ large.

**Worked comparison of the two terms.** Consider one organ in one scan. If a stray region raises the normalised Hausdorff distance from 0.05 to 0.35, the Hausdorff term of the score falls by

$$
0.6 \times (0.35 - 0.05) = 0.6 \times 0.30 = 0.18 .
$$

A loss of 0.01 in mean Dice, by comparison, lowers the Dice term by $0.4 \times 0.01 = 0.004$. The numbers are illustrative; they show how much larger the effect of one distant false positive can be than that of a small boundary error.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Computing the Hausdorff distance with a distance transform" kind="code">

A Nolad illustration. Comparing every pair of points costs $O(|A|\,|B|)$ operations. A Euclidean distance transform gives, for every voxel, the distance to the nearest voxel of a set, so each directed distance is one transform followed by a maximum.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import distance_transform_edt

def hausdorff(pred: np.ndarray, true: np.ndarray, spacing=(1.0, 1.0, 1.0)) -> float:
    """pred, true: boolean 3D arrays (z, y, x). Returns the symmetric Hausdorff distance."""
    if not pred.any() and not true.any():
        return 0.0
    if not pred.any() or not true.any():
        return np.inf                      # the competition maps this to the maximum (1 after normalisation)
    dist_to_true = distance_transform_edt(~true, sampling=spacing)   # distance of every voxel to the nearest true voxel
    dist_to_pred = distance_transform_edt(~pred, sampling=spacing)
    return max(dist_to_true[pred].max(), dist_to_pred[true].max())
```

The competition's metric notebook defines the exact normalisation and the handling of empty masks.
</Deep>

## Winning solution

The winning team, nccsm, combined three groups of models: classification models (CLS) that decide whether an organ is present on a slice, 2.5D segmentation models (SEG), and 3D segmentation models.

### Stage 1: slice classification

Four U-Nets with EfficientNet-B4 to B7 encoders, built with mmsegmentation and trained on the whole dataset without folds, predict masks. Their output is used only to decide which slices contain each organ. A slice is declared positive for an organ if **more than 12 pixels** have a predicted probability above 0.6. Otherwise its mask for that organ is set to zero and the segmentation models are not run on it, which also reduces inference time. Some of the classifiers were trained without cases identified in a public discussion as mis-annotated.

The team credits the two-stage design, classification followed by segmentation, to winning solutions of the 2019 SIIM-ACR pneumothorax competition, whose metric also gave a Dice of 0 to any predicted pixel on an image without the target structure.

<Deep title="The minimum-area rule" kind="detail">

The rule has two thresholds, a probability of 0.6 and a count of 12 pixels. A prediction on a negative slice survives only if at least 13 pixels exceed probability 0.6, so small scattered detections are removed. These are the errors that raise the Hausdorff distance by a large amount while hardly changing Dice.

The two kinds of classifier error cost the same on the Dice term. If the organ is present and the slice is rejected, the prediction is empty, and

$$
\mathrm{Dice} = \frac{2 \times 0}{0 + |Y|} = 0 .
$$

If the organ is absent and the slice is accepted with a non-empty prediction, Dice is also 0, as computed above. On the Hausdorff term, a rejected slice can remove part of the organ from the prediction, and an accepted slice can add a distant false-positive region.

With the classifier in front, the five segmentation U-Nets were trained only on annotated slices, where every training image contains at least one organ.
</Deep>

### Stage 2: 2.5D segmentation

Following a public notebook by Awsaf, the input for slice $i$ stacks slices $i-2$, $i$ and $i+2$ as three channels (a **stride of 2**), so the three channels span five slice positions. Images were resized to 640 pixels and randomly cropped to 448 or 512.

<Foundation id="unet" />

- **Five U-Nets** (encoders EfficientNet-B4, two B5, B6 and B7), trained only on annotated slices.
- **Two UPerNets** with ConvNeXt-base and ConvNeXt-small backbones, trained on all slices except the five slices after the last annotated slice of the large and of the small bowel. The team regarded the labels there as ambiguous, because the annotation had stopped.

<Foundation id="cnn-backbones" />

**Losses.** The classification models used binary cross-entropy (BCE). The segmentation models used BCE and Dice loss in the ratio 1:3; across the models, the team used Dice-plus-cross-entropy, cross-entropy and Dice losses.

**Augmentation.** Random flips, rotations, elastic deformation ($\alpha = 120$, $\sigma = 6$, affine parameter 3.6), grid distortion and optical distortion.

**Results.** Horizontal-flip test-time augmentation added 0.001–0.002. Mixed precision (FP16) halved memory use. A single U-Net scored about 0.883 on the public leaderboard, and the fused 2.5D group about 0.889.

<Foundation id="data-augmentation" />

<Deep title="Elastic deformation" kind="math">

Elastic augmentation (Simard et al., 2003) warps an image with a smooth random displacement field, which imitates the deformation of soft tissue. For each pixel $(x, y)$, independent random values $\Delta x(x, y)$ and $\Delta y(x, y)$ are drawn from the uniform distribution $\mathcal{U}(-1, 1)$. Each field is convolved with a normalised Gaussian kernel $G_\sigma$ of standard deviation $\sigma$ pixels and scaled by $\alpha$:

$$
u(x, y) = \alpha\,(G_\sigma * \Delta x)(x, y), \qquad v(x, y) = \alpha\,(G_\sigma * \Delta y)(x, y).
$$

The warped image is $I'(x, y) = I(x + u, y + v)$, with interpolation, and the mask is warped with the same field using nearest-neighbour interpolation.

**Size of the displacements.** A value drawn from $\mathcal{U}(-1, 1)$ has variance $1/3$. Convolving independent noise of variance $s^2$ with a kernel $G$ gives a field of variance $s^2 \sum G^2$, and for a 2D Gaussian with $\sigma$ of several pixels the sum is close to the integral

$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} G_\sigma(\mathbf{r})^2\, d\mathbf{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\sigma^2}.
$$

The standard deviation of $u$ is therefore

$$
\mathrm{sd}(u) = \alpha \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{3}} \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4\pi\sigma^2}} = \frac{\alpha}{\sigma\sqrt{12\pi}} .
$$

With $\alpha = 120$ and $\sigma = 6$,

$$
\mathrm{sd}(u) = \frac{120}{6 \times 6.14} = \frac{120}{36.8} = 3.3 \ \text{pixels}.
$$

The autocorrelation of the smoothed field is a Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt 2 \approx 8.5$ pixels, so the displacements vary smoothly over distances of about ten pixels. A smaller $\sigma$ gives a rough, less realistic field; a much larger $\sigma$ gives a nearly uniform shift of the whole image.
</Deep>

### Stage 3: 3D segmentation

Three **SegResNets** from MONAI, with 12, 20 and 32 initial filters, PReLU activations and batch normalisation, were trained on whole volumes. They were based on a public MONAI pipeline by yiheng, which scored 0.877 on the public leaderboard by itself. A second training stage ran for 20 epochs with a cosine learning-rate decay from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-6}$. SegResNet (Myronenko, 2018) is an encoder–decoder with residual blocks, proposed for brain-tumour segmentation.

<Foundation id="convolution" />

### Fusion

Let $z_{2.5\mathrm{D}}$ and $z_{3\mathrm{D}}$ be the averaged logits of the 2.5D and 3D ensembles at a pixel. The fused logit is

$$
z = 0.6\, z_{2.5\mathrm{D}} + 0.4\, z_{3\mathrm{D}},
$$

which is thresholded at 0.4. Slices that the classifiers rejected keep an empty mask.

The two families receive different context. The 2.5D models see three slices spanning five slice positions around the slice being segmented, and their predictions for neighbouring slices are made independently. The 3D models process the whole volume. The write-ups report the fusion weights but not a separate score for the 3D group within the final ensemble.

## Comparison with other competitions

- **SIIM-ACR pneumothorax (2019).** The winners of that competition also separated the decision that an image is empty from the segmentation, with a minimum pixel count, and the UW-Madison winners credit their design to those solutions. In both metrics a single predicted pixel on an image without the structure gives a Dice of 0.
- **HuBMAP + HPA organ segmentation** is the other segmentation competition in the collection. It uses histology images, and its main difficulty was the transfer between tissue sources in training and test.
- **The 2.5D input** also appears in the haemorrhage, cervical spine and abdominal trauma reports. The second-place team in the cervical spine competition adapted the same public UW-Madison 2.5D notebook for vertebra segmentation.
