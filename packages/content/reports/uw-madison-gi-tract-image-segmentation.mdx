## The problem in its field

The competition description cites about 5 million people diagnosed with cancer of the gastrointestinal tract worldwide in 2019, about half of whom are eligible for radiotherapy, typically delivered in 10–15-minute sessions daily for one to six weeks. Radiation must hit the tumour while sparing the stomach and intestines, which are sensitive to radiation and move from day to day as they fill, empty and contract.

An **MR-Linac** combines an MRI scanner with a linear accelerator, so the tumour and surrounding organs can be imaged at every session and the treatment plan adapted to that day's anatomy. The University of Wisconsin–Madison Carbone Cancer Center has used one since 2015. The bottleneck is contouring: a radiation oncologist must outline the stomach and bowel on the day's scan before treatment, which, according to the host, can stretch a 15-minute session to an hour. Automatic segmentation would shorten this to a quick review.

## The data

Anonymised MRI scans of patients treated at UW-Madison, with 1 to 5 scans per patient on different days. Each scan is a 3D volume provided as individual 2D slices (PNG images, typically 144 slices per scan), and each slice has run-length-encoded masks for three organs: **large bowel**, **small bowel** and **stomach**. Many slices contain none of them (above or below the abdomen) or only one or two.

## The metric

The score combines overlap and boundary distance:
$$
\text{score} = 0.4 \times \overline{\text{Dice}} + 0.6 \times \big(1 - \overline{\text{HD}}_{\text{norm}}\big).
$$
Dice is computed on 2D slices; the Hausdorff distance on the 3D volume built by stacking the slices of a scan (with unit spacing between slices), with coordinates normalised by the image size so the distance lies in $[0, 1]$.

<Foundation id="dice-and-overlap" />

<Deep title="The Hausdorff distance" kind="math">

For two non-empty finite sets of points $A$ and $B$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (here, the voxels of the predicted and true masks), the **directed** Hausdorff distance from $A$ to $B$ is the largest distance from a point of $A$ to its nearest point in $B$:
$$
\vec d(A, B) = \max_{a \in A}\, \min_{b \in B}\, \|a - b\|.
$$
The **Hausdorff distance** is the larger of the two directions:
$$
d_H(A, B) = \max\big(\vec d(A, B),\ \vec d(B, A)\big).
$$
It is a metric on non-empty compact sets: $d_H(A,B) = 0$ if and only if $A = B$, it is symmetric by construction, and it satisfies the triangle inequality. (The same construction defines a metric on the non-empty compact subsets of any metric space. It is due to Felix Hausdorff, as is the separation axiom that defines a Hausdorff topological space, from which the Hausdorff Space community takes its name.)

The key property for this competition: **$d_H$ is determined by the single worst point.** Suppose the prediction matches the stomach perfectly but adds one small false-positive blob 100 voxels away. Dice barely changes, since the blob is a few voxels among thousands. But $\vec d(\text{pred}, \text{true}) = 100$ voxels, so $d_H = 100$. A single stray region anywhere in the volume dominates the score for that organ.

The converse also holds: missing a distant part of the organ (a bowel loop in another slice) gives a large $\vec d(\text{true}, \text{pred})$.

Since the Hausdorff term carries 60% of the weight, controlling false positives on slices where an organ is absent was worth more than refining boundaries.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Computing the Hausdorff distance efficiently" kind="code">

Brute force compares every pair of points, $O(|A|\,|B|)$. A distance transform gives, for every voxel, the distance to the nearest voxel of a set, so each directed distance is one transform plus a maximum.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import distance_transform_edt

def hausdorff(pred: np.ndarray, true: np.ndarray, spacing=(1.0, 1.0, 1.0)) -> float:
    """pred, true: boolean 3D arrays (z, y, x). Returns the symmetric Hausdorff distance."""
    if not pred.any() and not true.any():
        return 0.0
    if not pred.any() or not true.any():
        return np.inf                      # the competition maps this to the maximum (1 after normalisation)
    dist_to_true = distance_transform_edt(~true, sampling=spacing)   # distance of every voxel to the nearest true voxel
    dist_to_pred = distance_transform_edt(~pred, sampling=spacing)
    return max(dist_to_true[pred].max(), dist_to_pred[true].max())
```

This is a Nolad illustration; the competition's metric notebook defines the exact normalisation and the handling of empty masks.
</Deep>

**Empty slices.** As implemented by the organisers, a slice where both the prediction and the truth are empty scores a perfect Dice; any false-positive pixels on an empty slice give that slice a Dice of 0. With many empty slices, predicting "empty" correctly is worth a lot on both parts of the score.

### The classification stage

Four U-Nets with EfficientNet-B4 to B7 encoders (in mmsegmentation), trained on the whole dataset, predict the masks, but their only job is to decide which slices contain each organ. A slice is declared positive for an organ if **more than 12 pixels** have probability above 0.6; otherwise its mask is set to zero, and the segmentation models are not even run on it, which also saves time. Some classifiers were trained without cases known to be mis-annotated (identified in a public discussion).

The team credits this two-stage idea to the winning solutions of the 2019 SIIM-ACR pneumothorax competition, where the same logic (an empty mask is perfect, a false positive is fatal) applied.

<Deep title="Why a separate classifier beats thresholding the segmentation" kind="detail">

A segmentation model trained on all slices must spend capacity on two tasks: finding boundaries on positive slices and suppressing everything on negative slices. The pixel-level loss is dominated by background pixels, so the model learns a conservative boundary but may still produce small spurious blobs on negative slices.

A dedicated decision with a **minimum area** (12 pixels at probability > 0.6) is a morphological filter: it removes isolated small detections, exactly the kind of error that inflates the Hausdorff distance. Segmentation models can then be trained **only on positive slices**, where their loss is focused on boundaries.

The cost of a classifier false negative (a missed organ on a slice) is a Dice of 0 on that slice and possibly a large Hausdorff distance; the cost of a false positive is the same. The area threshold and the probability threshold trade these off and were tuned on validation.
</Deep>

### The 2.5D segmentation stage

Following a public notebook by Awsaf, three slices with a **stride of 2** (slice $i-2$, $i$, $i+2$) are stacked as channels. Images were resized to 640 and randomly cropped to 448 or 512.

<Foundation id="unet" />

- **Five U-Nets** (EfficientNet-B4, two B5, B6, B7) trained only on annotated slices.
- **Two UPerNets** with ConvNeXt-base and ConvNeXt-small backbones, trained on all slices except the five slices after the last annotated slice of the large and small bowel, where annotation had stopped and the labels are ambiguous.

<Foundation id="cnn-backbones" />

Losses: binary cross-entropy for the classification models; for segmentation, BCE and Dice in the ratio 1:3 (combinations of Dice, cross-entropy and Dice-plus-CE across models). Augmentation: flips, rotations, elastic deformation ($\alpha = 120$, $\sigma = 6$), grid distortion and optical distortion. Horizontal-flip TTA added 0.001–0.002; mixed precision halved memory. A single U-Net scored about 0.883 public, the fused 2.5D group about 0.889.

<Foundation id="data-augmentation" />

<Deep title="Elastic deformation" kind="math">

Elastic augmentation (Simard et al., 2003) simulates soft-tissue deformation, which suits bowel and stomach. For each pixel, draw random displacements $\Delta x, \Delta y \sim \mathcal{U}(-1, 1)$ independently, smooth each displacement field with a Gaussian of standard deviation $\sigma$, and scale by $\alpha$:
$$
u(x, y) = \alpha\,(G_\sigma * \Delta x)(x, y), \qquad v(x, y) = \alpha\,(G_\sigma * \Delta y)(x, y).
$$
The warped image is $I'(x, y) = I(x + u, y + v)$ with interpolation; the mask is warped identically (nearest-neighbour interpolation). Small $\sigma$ gives noisy, unrealistic warps; large $\sigma$ gives nearly rigid shifts. With $\sigma = 6$ and $\alpha = 120$, the smoothing reduces the random field's amplitude substantially, so the resulting displacements are a few pixels, varying smoothly over tens of pixels.
</Deep>

### The 3D stage

Three **SegResNets** from MONAI (initial filters 12, 20 and 32; PReLU activations, batch normalisation), based on a public MONAI pipeline by yiheng that scored 0.877 public by itself, were trained on whole volumes, with a second stage of 20 epochs at a cosine-decayed learning rate from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-6}$. SegResNet is an encoder–decoder with residual blocks, proposed for brain-tumour segmentation.

<Foundation id="convolution" />

### Fusion

The final logits are $0.6 \times$ the 2.5D ensemble plus $0.4 \times$ the 3D ensemble, thresholded at 0.4, and zeroed on slices the classifier rejected.

The two families have complementary errors. The 2.5D models see only five slices of context and can be inconsistent from slice to slice; the 3D models see the whole organ and produce coherent shapes, but at lower in-plane resolution. Averaging logits reduces both kinds of error.

## How this compares with other competitions

- **SIIM-ACR pneumothorax (2019)** set the pattern this winner followed: classify for emptiness, then segment, with a minimum-area rule. Both metrics reward empty predictions on empty images.
- **HuBMAP organ segmentation** is the other segmentation competition in the collection, on histology instead of MRI; there the challenge was transfer between tissue sources, not empty images.
- **The 2.5D representation** appears across the collection (haemorrhage, cervical spine, abdominal trauma). The cervical-spine runner-up adapted the same public UW-Madison 2.5D notebook for vertebra segmentation.
