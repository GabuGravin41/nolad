## Clinical background

Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer in men, with more than one million new diagnoses and more than 350,000 deaths each year. It is diagnosed from **needle biopsies**: thin cores of tissue taken from the prostate, stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a microscope by a pathologist.

The pathologist reports both whether cancer is present and how aggressive it is. Prostate cancers range from indolent tumours that may never cause harm to aggressive tumours that spread, and the choice between active surveillance, surgery and radiotherapy depends on the **grade**. Grading is subjective, and the competition organisers describe large variation between pathologists, especially between neighbouring grades.

<Deep title="Gleason patterns, Gleason score and ISUP grade" kind="domain">

**Gleason patterns** describe the architecture of cancerous glands:

- **Pattern 3**: separate, well-formed glands of varying size. Low-grade.
- **Pattern 4**: fused, poorly formed or cribriform (sieve-like) glands.
- **Pattern 5**: no gland formation; sheets, cords or single cells, or central necrosis.

Patterns 1 and 2 are no longer used in biopsies.

The **Gleason score** is the sum of the most common pattern and the second most common pattern (in biopsies, the highest pattern present), for example $3+4 = 7$. The order matters: $3+4$ (mostly pattern 3) has a better prognosis than $4+3$ (mostly pattern 4).

The **ISUP grade group** (International Society of Urological Pathology, 2014) maps Gleason scores onto five prognostic groups:

| ISUP grade | Gleason score |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | $3+3 = 6$ |
| 2 | $3+4 = 7$ |
| 3 | $4+3 = 7$ |
| 4 | $4+4$, $3+5$, $5+3 = 8$ |
| 5 | $4+5$, $5+4$, $5+5 = 9$–$10$ |

In the competition, grade 0 means no cancer, which gives six **ordered** classes.

## Data

The dataset contains about 11,000 whole-slide images of prostate biopsies from two centres, Radboud University Medical Center (Nijmegen) and Karolinska Institutet (Stockholm). The organisers state that it is about eight times the size of the CAMELYON17 pathology dataset. Each slide has a Gleason score and an ISUP grade.

The **training labels are noisy**, and the noise differs by centre. At Radboud, labels came from trained students, whose agreement with experts the organisers reported as about 0.853 on the kappa scale; at Karolinska, a single experienced pathologist graded the slides. The test slides were graded by expert uropathologists, three per slide. Some biopsies appear more than once in the training data as near-duplicate images.

Slides are stored as multi-resolution TIFF files with three levels. At full resolution a slide is tens of thousands of pixels on each side, most of it white background.

## Evaluation metric

<Foundation id="quadratic-weighted-kappa" />

Quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) compares the confusion matrix of predictions and labels with the matrix expected by chance, and penalises a disagreement between grades $i$ and $j$ in proportion to $(i-j)^2$. Predicting grade 4 for a grade-5 slide has weight $1^2 = 1$; predicting grade 1 for a grade-5 slide has weight $4^2 = 16$. In Iafoss's experiments, regression losses gave higher QWK than cross-entropy over the six classes (see the eleventh-place section).

The public and private test sets each held about 500 slides. Iafoss noted that this is small for QWK and that the leaderboard scores were correspondingly noisy.

## Tile pooling

Many top solutions, including the winner's, built on a public notebook by Iafoss. A whole-slide image is too large to pass through a CNN at once, and the grade is a property of the whole slide.

<Foundation id="models-and-loss" />

**Tile selection.** The slide, read at an intermediate resolution level, is cut into a grid of square tiles, and the $N$ tiles with the most tissue are kept. On an H&E slide the background is white, so the tiles with the most tissue are those with the smallest sum of pixel intensities. For $N = 64$ tiles of 256 × 256 pixels, the input to the network is

$$
64 \times 256 \times 256 = 4{,}194{,}304 \ \text{pixels},
$$

against the hundreds of millions of pixels of a full-resolution slide.

**Tile pooling.** Each tile passes through the same CNN. Let $F^{(n)} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times h \times w}$ be the feature map of tile $n = 1, \dots, N$, with $C$ channels on an $h \times w$ grid. Iafoss's notebook concatenates the $N$ feature maps into one map and pools it, so each channel $c$ is pooled over all tiles and positions together:

$$
z_c = \operatorname{pool}\Big(\big\{\, F^{(n)}_{c,a,b} \ :\ n = 1, \dots, N;\ a = 1, \dots, h;\ b = 1, \dots, w \,\big\}\Big).
$$

Average and max pooling depend only on the set of values, so $z$ does not change if the tiles are reordered. In the public notebook, the head concatenates average and max pooling and applies a small multilayer perceptron (MLP) to obtain the prediction.

<Foundation id="multiple-instance-learning" />

In the terms of multiple-instance learning, the slide is the bag and the tiles are its instances. The whole bag is processed in one forward pass, so the network is trained end to end from slide labels. This requires that all selected tiles fit in GPU memory at once, which holds for 64 tiles of this size.

## Winning solution

Team PND (arutema47, poteman and fam_taro) selected two submissions. The winning one was an equal-weight average of an EfficientNet-B0 trained by arutema47 and an EfficientNet-B1 trained by fam_taro: public 0.904, private **0.940**. The other selection, a ResNeXt-50 by poteman, scored higher on the public leaderboard (0.910) and lower on the private leaderboard (0.922).

### Duplicate-aware cross-validation split

If near-duplicate slides are placed in different folds, a model sees the same biopsy during training and validation, and its out-of-fold predictions on those slides are too accurate. The team found duplicates with **image hashing** (a perceptual hash of a low-resolution thumbnail), grouped slides whose hash similarity exceeded 0.90, and stratified the folds by grade with each group kept in one fold. fam_taro took the image-hash grouping from a public notebook by Appian, and describes the duplicate-aware split as essential for the denoising step below.

<Foundation id="cross-validation" />

### Label denoising

The procedure, proposed by arutema47:

1. Train $k$-fold models (an EfficientNet-B1 similar to a public notebook by Qishen Ha) with **regression** on the ISUP grade.
2. Predict each training slide with the model that did not train on it (out-of-fold prediction).
3. **Remove** slides with $|\text{label} - \text{prediction}| > 1.6$.
4. Retrain on the remaining slides.

An EfficientNet-B0 based on Qishen Ha's public notebook, trained with 5-fold cross-validation after this denoising, reached a cross-validation QWK of 0.94, public 0.90 and private 0.934, against 0.940 for the winning ensemble.

fam_taro replaced the single threshold with thresholds per grade and per centre, larger for grades 0 and 5 at the ends of the scale:

| Centre | Thresholds for grades 0–5 |
| --- | --- |
| Radboud | 1.3, 0.8, 0.8, 0.8, 0.8, 1.3 |
| Karolinska | 1.5, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.5 |

The Radboud thresholds are smaller, so more Radboud slides are removed at a given residual.

<Deep title="Effect of label noise on a regression model, and what the residual filter removes" kind="math">

Let $x$ be a slide, $y \in \{0, \dots, 5\}$ its true grade and $\tilde y = y + \varepsilon$ its training label, where $\varepsilon$ is the annotator's error. For a squared-error loss, the function that minimises the expected loss over the data distribution is the conditional mean of the label:

$$
f^*(x) = \mathbb{E}[\tilde y \mid x] .
$$

Substituting $\tilde y = y + \varepsilon$ and using linearity of expectation:

$$
f^*(x) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid x] + \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x] .
$$

If the errors have mean zero at every $x$, the second term vanishes and noise does not shift the optimum. A network trained on about 11,000 slides does not reach this optimum: it can fit individual training labels, including wrong ones, and these fitted errors affect its predictions on similar slides.

An out-of-fold prediction $\hat y_i$ comes from a model that did not see slide $i$. A large residual $|\tilde y_i - \hat y_i|$ means that the label or the prediction is wrong. Removing the slides with large residuals therefore removes both label errors and slides that are difficult for the model. If most removed slides carry label errors, the training labels become more accurate. If many are difficult slides with correct labels, such as rare patterns or artefacts, the retrained model sees fewer examples of them. The measured gains in this competition (above, and in the eleventh-place section) show that removal improved the score against the expert-graded test labels.

The duplicate-aware split affects the filter directly. If a duplicate of slide $i$ were in the training folds, the model would have fitted the label of the duplicate, which is the same possibly wrong label; the out-of-fold prediction would reproduce it, the residual would be small, and the error would not be removed.
</Deep>

The team also tried **confident learning** (the CleanLab library), which identifies label errors from predicted class probabilities; it gave worse results than the residual threshold.

<Deep title="Out-of-fold denoising in code" kind="code">

A Nolad illustration of the procedure described in the write-up.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedGroupKFold

# df: one row per slide with columns isup (0-5), provider, dup_group (from image hashing)
folds = StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
oof = np.zeros(len(df))
for tr, va in folds.split(df, df.isup, groups=df.dup_group):
    model = train_regressor(df.iloc[tr])          # tile-pooling CNN, MSE on ISUP grade
    oof[va] = predict(model, df.iloc[va])         # never saw these slides or their duplicates

thr = {"radboud":    [1.3, 0.8, 0.8, 0.8, 0.8, 1.3],
       "karolinska": [1.5, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.5]}
limit = np.array([thr[p][g] for p, g in zip(df.provider, df.isup)])
keep  = np.abs(oof - df.isup.values) <= limit
clean = df[keep]                                  # retrain every fold on this subset
```
</Deep>

### Models

- **fam_taro**: EfficientNet-B1 with generalised-mean (GeM) pooling, 64 tiles of 192 × 192. The targets were the ISUP grade and the first Gleason pattern (as a 10-dimensional binned encoding). Stratified 5-fold split on Gleason score with hash grouping; benign slides relabelled as "0+0".
- **arutema47**: EfficientNet-B0.
- **poteman**: ResNeXt-50 (32×4d) with Iafoss's tile method, 64 tiles of 256 × 256, stratified folds with hash grouping, and a head with three regression outputs and one softmax output.

A regression output is converted to an integer grade with thresholds between consecutive grades. The thresholds can be set at the half-integers or tuned on out-of-fold predictions to maximise QWK.

The following did not help fam_taro: confident learning, CycleGAN stain transfer between centres, adaptive batch normalisation, and CutMix or mixup applied before denoising.

## Eleventh place: a differentiable kappa loss

Iafoss, whose public notebook many teams used, finished eleventh alone with a gold medal. His public notebook, with 36 tiles of 256 × 256 and a kappa loss, scored about 0.90 public and 0.91 private. In his final solution he used 128 tiles of 128 × 128 from an intermediate resolution level, with tile cutout and random tile selection as augmentation.

**Denoising.** Removing about 1,400 Radboud and 300 Karolinska slides, which left 8,700, improved his score by 0.005 on the public and 0.01 on the private leaderboard. Relabelling the removed slides with model predictions did not help. He also tried noise-robust training methods (co-teaching, JoCoR, negative learning, progressive label distillation).

**Kappa loss.** In his experiments the losses ranked as follows: kappa loss, then sorted binning, then binning, then mean squared error (MSE), then cross-entropy.

<Deep title="From quadratic weighted kappa to a differentiable loss" kind="math">

Let $y_1, \dots, y_n$ be the labels and $\hat y_1, \dots, \hat y_n$ the predictions, treated as real numbers. With the observed and expected matrices of the Foundation block above, the weighted sums for integer grades are

$$
\sum_{i,j} (i-j)^2\,O_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (y_k - \hat y_k)^2,
\qquad
\sum_{i,j} (i-j)^2\,E_{ij} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n} (y_k - \hat y_l)^2 .
$$

The first sum counts each slide once at its own pair of grades. The second follows from $E_{ij} = r_i c_j / n$: pairing every label with every prediction gives $r_i c_j$ pairs with grades $(i, j)$. The normalisation $(N-1)^2$ of the weights cancels in the ratio, so

$$
\kappa = 1 - \frac{O}{D},
\qquad
O = \sum_{k} (y_k - \hat y_k)^2,
\qquad
D = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k}\sum_{l} (y_k - \hat y_l)^2 .
$$

The right-hand side is defined for real-valued predictions, which gives the continuous version of QWK.

**Expanding $D$.** Expanding the square and summing over $k$ and $l$:

$$
\sum_{k}\sum_{l} (y_k - \hat y_l)^2 = n\sum_k y_k^2 + n\sum_l \hat y_l^2 - 2\Big(\sum_k y_k\Big)\Big(\sum_l \hat y_l\Big).
$$

With the means $\bar y = \tfrac1n \sum_k y_k$ and $\bar{\hat y} = \tfrac1n \sum_k \hat y_k$, dividing by $n$ gives

$$
D = \sum_k y_k^2 + \sum_k \hat y_k^2 - 2n\,\bar y\,\bar{\hat y} .
$$

**Expanding $O$:**

$$
O = \sum_k y_k^2 + \sum_k \hat y_k^2 - 2\sum_k y_k \hat y_k .
$$

**The difference.** The squared terms cancel in $D - O$:

$$
D - O = 2\sum_k y_k\hat y_k - 2n\,\bar y\,\bar{\hat y} .
$$

**Centring.** Let $s_y^2$ and $s_{\hat y}^2$ be the (biased) sample variances and $s_{y\hat y}$ the sample covariance. From the identities $\sum_k y_k\hat y_k - n\bar y\bar{\hat y} = n\,s_{y\hat y}$ and $\sum_k y_k^2 = n\,s_y^2 + n\,\bar y^2$ (and likewise for $\hat y$):

$$
D - O = 2n\,s_{y\hat y},
\qquad
D = n\,s_y^2 + n\,s_{\hat y}^2 + n\,(\bar y - \bar{\hat y})^2 .
$$

**Result.** Since $\kappa = (D - O)/D$, cancelling $n$:

$$
\kappa = \frac{2\,s_{y\hat y}}{s_y^2 + s_{\hat y}^2 + (\bar y - \bar{\hat y})^2} .
$$

This is **Lin's concordance correlation coefficient** (1989). It is a smooth function of the predictions and can be computed on each mini-batch; the loss is $1 - \kappa$. Iafoss centred the predictions and labels within the batch, as the covariance and variances require.

**Difference from MSE.** Consider predictions with the correct mean, $\bar{\hat y} = \bar y$, and scale their deviations by a factor $\lambda > 0$, so that $\hat y_k = \bar y + \lambda u_k$ with $\bar u = 0$. Write $s_{yu}$ and $s_u^2$ for the covariance and variance of $u$. Then

$$
\kappa(\lambda) = \frac{2\lambda\,s_{yu}}{s_y^2 + \lambda^2 s_u^2} .
$$

Differentiating with respect to $\lambda$, the numerator of the derivative is proportional to

$$
(s_y^2 + \lambda^2 s_u^2) - 2\lambda^2 s_u^2 = s_y^2 - \lambda^2 s_u^2 ,
$$

which is zero at $\lambda^2 s_u^2 = s_y^2$. Kappa is maximised when the predictions have the same variance as the labels. The mean squared error is

$$
\tfrac1n\,O(\lambda) = s_y^2 - 2\lambda\,s_{yu} + \lambda^2 s_u^2 ,
$$

which is minimised at $\lambda = s_{yu}/s_u^2$. The variance of the predictions is then

$$
\lambda^2 s_u^2 = \frac{s_{yu}^2}{s_u^2} = \rho^2\,s_y^2 ,
$$

where $\rho = s_{yu}/(s_y s_u)$ is the correlation between labels and predictions. Since $\rho^2 \le 1$, MSE training shrinks the predictions towards the mean grade by a variance factor $\rho^2$, while the kappa loss does not. For example, with $\rho = 0.9$, the MSE-optimal predictions have $0.81$ of the label variance.
</Deep>

**Number of epochs.** He limited training to 32–48 epochs, so that the model would not learn the grading tendencies of the training annotators, which differ from those of the expert test panel. His final submission was a majority vote of 8 ResNeXt-50 models (0.917 public, 0.930 private).

## Comparison with other competitions

- **UBC-OCEAN** (ovarian cancer subtyping) also uses whole-slide images, together with tissue microarrays at a different magnification, and the target is a subtype. Its winner embedded tiles with a pathology foundation model (Phikon) and trained only a small multiple-instance head (CHOWDER), without training the tile encoder end to end.
- **APTOS 2019** (diabetic retinopathy) is the other ordinal-grading competition in the collection and was also scored with QWK. The winner there also regressed the grade, with a smooth L1 loss, and converted the output to classes with thresholds.
- **Label noise.** In PANDA the training and test labels were produced by different kinds of annotator, and the top solutions removed training slides by the size of their out-of-fold residual.
