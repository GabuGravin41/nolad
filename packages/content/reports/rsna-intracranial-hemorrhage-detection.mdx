## Clinical background

An **intracranial haemorrhage** is bleeding inside the skull. It can follow trauma, rupture of an aneurysm, a stroke, high blood pressure, a vascular malformation or anticoagulant treatment. The competition description states that haemorrhages cause about 10% of strokes in the United States. Blood inside the rigid skull raises the pressure and compresses brain tissue, and some types require an operation within hours. Patients with sudden severe headache, reduced consciousness or head injury therefore receive an urgent **non-contrast head CT**, from which the radiologist determines whether there is blood, where it lies and of which type it is.

<Deep title="The five subtypes and their appearance on CT" kind="domain">

Fresh blood is denser than brain tissue (roughly 50–90 Hounsfield units against 20–45) and appears bright on CT. The subtype is determined by location and shape:

| Subtype | Where the blood is | Typical appearance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Epidural** | between the skull and the outer membrane (dura) | lens-shaped, bounded by skull sutures; often from an arterial tear after a fracture |
| **Subdural** | between the dura and the arachnoid membrane | crescent along the brain surface, can cross sutures; often venous, common in the elderly |
| **Subarachnoid** | in the fluid spaces around the brain | fills the grooves (sulci) and cisterns; typical after aneurysm rupture |
| **Intraparenchymal** | inside the brain tissue | a rounded bright region; hypertension, stroke, trauma |
| **Intraventricular** | inside the ventricles | fluid levels or casts in the ventricles |

A patient can have several types at once. Thin subdural collections lying against the skull are hard to see in a standard brain window because they merge with the bright bone; a wider "subdural window" separates them.
</Deep>

## Data

The RSNA, with the American Society of Neuroradiology and MD.ai, assembled de-identified head CT studies from four institutions: Stanford University, Thomas Jefferson University, Unity Health Toronto and the Federal University of São Paulo. More than 60 volunteer neuroradiologists labelled over 25,000 examinations. The dataset paper (Flanders et al., 2020) describes the construction.

Each **slice** carries six binary labels: epidural, intraparenchymal, intraventricular, subarachnoid, subdural, and **any** (at least one of the five). Slices are distributed as individual DICOM files. A scan is reconstructed by grouping the files by series and ordering them by their position along the head–foot axis, stored in the DICOM field `ImagePositionPatient[2]`.

The competition had two stages. Stage 1 ended on 4 November 2019, by which date teams had to upload their models. The stage-2 test set was released on 5 November and predicted with the uploaded models until 13 November.

## Evaluation metric

Let $n = 1, \dots, N$ index the test slices and $k \in \{\mathrm{epi}, \mathrm{ipa}, \mathrm{ivt}, \mathrm{sah}, \mathrm{sdh}, \mathrm{any}\}$ the six labels. Let $y_{nk} \in \{0,1\}$ be the true label and $p_{nk} \in (0,1)$ the submitted probability. Before logarithms are taken, each probability is clipped:

$$
\tilde p_{nk} = \min\!\big(\max(p_{nk},\ 10^{-15}),\ 1 - 10^{-15}\big).
$$

The binary log loss of one entry is

$$
\ell_{nk} = -\big[\, y_{nk}\log \tilde p_{nk} + (1 - y_{nk})\log(1 - \tilde p_{nk}) \,\big].
$$

With label weights $w_k > 0$, the score is the weighted mean of these terms over slices and labels:

$$
L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \ell_n,
\qquad
\ell_n = \frac{\sum_k w_k\,\ell_{nk}}{\sum_k w_k} .
$$

The competition overview states that the "any" label is weighted more heavily than the five subtypes and does not give the value. With the subtypes at $w_k = 1$ and, for illustration, $w_{\mathrm{any}} = 2$, the denominator is $\sum_k w_k = 7$, so the "any" label contributes $2/7 \approx 29\%$ of each slice's loss and each subtype $1/7 \approx 14\%$. Lower scores are better.

<Foundation id="weighted-log-loss" />

<Deep title="Why log loss rewards calibrated probabilities" kind="math">

Consider a group of slices that a model cannot distinguish, of which a fraction $q \in (0,1)$ carries a given label. If the model predicts the same probability $p \in (0,1)$ for all of them, the expected log loss per slice for that label is

$$
R(p) = -\,q\log p - (1-q)\log(1-p).
$$

Differentiating with respect to $p$:

$$
R'(p) = -\frac{q}{p} + \frac{1-q}{1-p}.
$$

Setting $R'(p) = 0$ and multiplying by $p(1-p)$:

$$
-q(1-p) + (1-q)p = 0 .
$$

Expanding, the terms $\pm qp$ cancel:

$$
p - q = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = q .
$$

The second derivative $R''(p) = q/p^2 + (1-q)/(1-p)^2$ is positive, so $p = q$ is the minimum. The metric is therefore minimised by predicting the true frequency. Because the label weight $w_k$ depends only on the label and not on the true class, it scales $R$ by a constant and does not move this minimum.

Worked numbers for a negative slice ($y = 0$): a prediction of $p = 0.1$ costs $-\ln 0.9 \approx 0.105$; $p = 0.5$ costs $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$; $p = 0.99$ costs $-\ln 0.01 \approx 4.61$. At the clipping bound, a prediction of exactly 1 costs $-\ln(10^{-15}) = 15\ln 10 \approx 34.5$. A single confident error of this kind outweighs the loss on hundreds of correctly predicted slices.
</Deep>

Most slice labels are 0, and neighbouring slices usually share their labels. The third-place author observed that, within a series sorted by `ImagePositionPatient[2]`, the labels form long runs of consecutive 0s and consecutive 1s: a subdural haematoma, for example, spans many consecutive slices. A radiologist uses the same continuity when scrolling through a scan, accepting a faint density that continues on the adjacent slices and discounting one that appears on a single slice. The first three teams all gave their models access to neighbouring slices, and the first- and second-place teams both titled their write-ups "sequential model".

### Stage 1: 2D CNNs on windowed slices

A CT window maps a range of Hounsfield units, given by its centre and width, linearly onto the displayed intensity range and clips values outside it. The team used three input settings, each with three channels so that networks pretrained on ImageNet colour images could be used without modification:

1. **One slice, three windows**: brain (centre 40, width 80), subdural (80, 200) and bone (600, 2800).
2. **Three adjacent slices, one window.**
3. **Three adjacent slices with three windows** (a combination of 1 and 2).

<Plot series={[{fn: "window", params: {c: 40, w: 80}, label: "brain (40, 80)"}, {fn: "window", params: {c: 80, w: 200}, label: "subdural (80, 200)"}, {fn: "window", params: {c: 600, w: 2800}, label: "bone (600, 2800)"}]} x={[-100, 700]} y={[0, 1]} xLabel="Hounsfield units" yLabel="channel intensity" caption="The three windows used as channels. Blood at 50–90 HU lies in the upper part of the brain window, in the middle of the subdural window, and is barely distinguishable from brain tissue in the bone window." />

<Deep title="The window function, with worked values" kind="math">

For a window with centre $c$ and width $w$ (both in HU), a voxel with value $h$ (in HU) is mapped to

$$
u(h) = \min\!\Big(\max\!\Big(\frac{h - (c - w/2)}{w},\ 0\Big),\ 1\Big) \in [0,1].
$$

The window covers the interval $[c - w/2,\ c + w/2]$. For the three windows used:

| Window | $c$ | $w$ | Interval (HU) | $u(20)$ | $u(70)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brain | 40 | 80 | $[0, 80]$ | 0.25 | 0.875 |
| Subdural | 80 | 200 | $[-20, 180]$ | 0.20 | 0.45 |
| Bone | 600 | 2800 | $[-800, 2000]$ | 0.293 | 0.311 |

Brain tissue at 20 HU and blood at 70 HU differ by $0.625$ in the brain window, by $0.25$ in the subdural window and by $50/2800 \approx 0.018$ in the bone window. In the brain window, blood above 80 HU and the skull both map to 1, so a thin collection against the skull has the same intensity as the bone. In the subdural window, blood at 50–90 HU maps to 0.35–0.55 while bone still maps to 1, so the two are separated. The bone window keeps the skull, at several hundred to over 1,000 HU, below saturation.
</Deep>

Setting 2 is a **2.5D** input: neighbouring slices in the channels give a 2D network information about the adjacent depth without the memory cost of a 3D network.

The backbones were SE-ResNeXt-101 and DenseNet-169 at 256 × 256 pixels, and DenseNet-121 at 512 × 512. Augmentation consisted of shift-scale-rotate, random resized crop and horizontal flip. The optimiser was Adam with a cyclic learning rate between $5\times10^{-4}$ and $10^{-5}$. Training images were sampled across series: each epoch drew a number of images equal to four times the number of series, so that long scans did not dominate the training set.

### Stage 2: sequence models

After the CNNs were trained, their outputs were computed for every slice of every scan, and two sequence models were trained on them.

**Sequence model 1** takes the **embeddings** (the penultimate-layer feature vectors) of all CNNs for each slice, passes them through a multilayer perceptron (MLP), and then through recurrent layers along the slice axis. The write-up describes these as LSTM layers; the author's comments describe GRU blocks.

**Sequence model 2** takes the **logits** of all CNNs (six per model per slice), the output of sequence model 1, and the spacing between consecutive slices, and passes them through 1D convolutions and recurrent layers. According to the author, the two sequence models were trained end to end together. During training, sub-sequences were sampled at random from each scan.

<Foundation id="recurrent-networks" />

<Deep title="Input dimensions of the sequence models" kind="math">

Let a scan have $T$ slices indexed by $t = 1, \dots, T$, and let the ensemble contain $M$ CNNs. CNN $m$ produces an embedding $e^{(m)}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_m}$ and a logit vector $s^{(m)}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{6}$ for slice $t$.

Sequence model 1 receives, at each $t$, the concatenated embeddings

$$
x^{(1)}_t = \big[\, e^{(1)}_t,\ \dots,\ e^{(M)}_t \,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 + \dots + d_M},
$$

and outputs six logits $o_t \in \mathbb{R}^6$ per slice.

Sequence model 2 receives the concatenated logits, the output of sequence model 1 and the slice spacing $\Delta z_t$ (defined below):

$$
x^{(2)}_t = \big[\, s^{(1)}_t,\ \dots,\ s^{(M)}_t,\ o_t,\ \Delta z_t \,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{6M + 6 + 1}.
$$

With $M = 4$ CNNs, as in the ensemble that scored 0.060 below, $x^{(2)}_t$ has $6 \cdot 4 + 6 + 1 = 31$ components. The recurrent layers run along $t$, so the output at slice $t$ depends on the inputs at all slices of the scan.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Progression of the stage-1 score, as reported by the author" kind="detail">

Replying to the fourth-place team, SeuTao described how the score improved on the stage-1 public leaderboard:

| Step | Stage-1 log loss |
| --- | --- |
| Ensemble of four 2D CNNs, simple post-processing | 0.060 |
| 1D CNN stacking over slices (early sequence model 2) | 0.057 |
| + GRU and slice-position input | 0.056 |
| + sequence model 1 and further changes | 0.054 |

The largest single step (0.003) came from the 1D convolution over the CNN outputs along the scan, before any recurrent layer was added. The three steps together reduced the loss by 0.006, or 10% of 0.060.
</Deep>

### Slice spacing as an input

Let $z_t$ be the head–foot position of slice $t$ (`ImagePositionPatient[2]`, in millimetres), with slices sorted so that $z_1 < z_2 < \dots < z_T$. The sequence models receive the first-order difference

$$
\Delta z_t = z_t - z_{t-1}, \qquad t = 2, \dots, T,
$$

which equals the slice spacing. For contiguous slices the spacing equals the slice thickness. The author's stated reason for the input is that slices reconstructed with different thicknesses have different noise levels, so the same pixel pattern carries different information on a 2.5 mm slice and on a 5 mm slice. The input improved the loss by about 0.0005.

### Result

The final submission scored 0.04383 on the private leaderboard. The authors later published the method with a clinical evaluation in *NeuroImage: Clinical* (Wang et al., 2021). In the competition discussion, a commenter pointed out that an earlier paper, RADnet (Grewal et al., 2018), had also combined a slice-level CNN with a recurrent model over slices.

## Second place: one backbone, embeddings and an LSTM

Darragh Hanley and Dmitry Larko (team NoBrainer) used a single image backbone and one type of sequence model.

- **Image classifier.** ResNeXt-101 with Facebook's weakly supervised pretrained weights, at 480 pixels. Of five folds, three were trained, for 5 epochs each. SE-ResNeXt and EfficientNet-B0 gave worse results.
- **Preprocessing.** The windowing code from Appian's public repository ([appian42/kaggle-rsna-intracranial-hemorrhage](https://github.com/appian42/kaggle-rsna-intracranial-hemorrhage), eleventh place), cropping of black space, and a minimum filter (`scipy.ndimage.minimum_filter`) to remove thin lines from headrests and scanner parts. Models were also trained without the minimum filter, and averaging both versions gave a slightly better score.
- **Embeddings.** The output of the global-average-pooling layer before the logits was extracted after each epoch, averaged over test-time augmentations.
- **LSTM.** Sequences were formed per patient, study and series and sorted by position; most had 24–60 slices. A separate LSTM was trained for 12 epochs on the embeddings from each epoch of each fold. The architecture was taken from the winners of the first stage of the Jigsaw toxic-comment competition. Larger hidden layers helped, as did adding the embeddings to the LSTM output. Shorter sequences were padded with zero embeddings, and the padded positions were excluded from the loss.
- **Ensemble.** The 15 LSTMs (3 folds × 5 epochs of embeddings) were averaged.

Each slice's input to the LSTM was its embedding concatenated with its differences from the embeddings of the neighbouring slices. With $e_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ the embedding of slice $t$,

$$
x_t = \big[\, e_t,\ \ e_t - e_{t-1},\ \ e_t - e_{t+1} \,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{3d}.
$$

The difference terms are zero where the image does not change between slices and large where a structure appears or disappears.

Mixup on images or on embeddings, sequence augmentation (partial or reversed sequences) and 1D convolutions over the sequence did not improve their score. The winning team did use 1D convolutions successfully. With more time, the team would have trained end to end and trained on the stage-2 data.

## Third place: neighbouring slices as channels, and stacking along the scan

takuoko competed alone and became a Kaggle Grandmaster with this result. The solution concentrated on the 2.5D input:

- **Windows.** A subdural window and Appian's three windows were compared; the subdural window was slightly better.
- **Neighbouring slices as channels.** Slices were stacked in several combinations: $(s_{t-1}, s_t, s_{t+1})$, $(s_t, s_{t+1}, s_{t+2})$, $(s_{t-2}, s_{t-1}, s_t)$, $(s_{t-2}, s_t, s_{t+2})$, and variants whose outer channels were the means of the 3 or 5 slices before and after, or of all slices on either side.
- **Multi-task training.** From $(s_{t-1}, s_t, s_{t+1})$ the model predicted the labels of all three slices.
- **Stacking with neighbours.** The same concatenation of neighbouring slices was applied to the predictions of the first-level models in the second-level ensemble. This improved the ensemble from 0.057 to 0.056 on the stage-1 public set.

The best single model scored 0.060–0.062 on the stage-1 public set. The author also added the external CQ500 dataset, cropped black space, retrained on stage-2 data, used generalised-mean pooling, and predicted only the five subtypes, setting "any" to their maximum. EfficientNet and CBAM ResNet did not work. The selected submission scored 0.045 on the private leaderboard; a submission using the stacking model alone, which was not selected, would have scored 0.043.

## Comparison with other competitions

A haemorrhage is visible on individual slices, so a 2D network can be trained on the individual slices of the dataset, of which there are many more than scans, starting from ImageNet-pretrained weights. The sequence models add the information that a single slice lacks, the continuity of a finding across slices, at the cost of a small recurrent model per scan.

Later RSNA CT competitions reused this structure. The 2020 pulmonary embolism winner credits the 2019 haemorrhage winners and ran a bidirectional GRU over slice embeddings. The winners of the 2022 cervical spine and 2023 abdominal trauma competitions applied 2D CNNs with recurrent layers to crops located by a segmentation model. The 2025 aneurysm winner, whose targets were small 3D structures imaged in four modalities, used 3D models guided by a vessel segmentation instead.
