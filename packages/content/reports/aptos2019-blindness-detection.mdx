## The problem in its field

**Diabetic retinopathy** is damage to the small blood vessels of the retina caused by long-standing high blood sugar. It is a leading cause of blindness in working-age adults. Vision loss is largely preventable if the disease is found early and treated with laser or injections, which is why people with diabetes are advised to have their retinas examined regularly.

Examination requires an ophthalmologist or a trained grader to look at **fundus photographs** of the back of the eye. Aravind Eye Hospital in India runs outreach in which technicians travel to rural areas, photograph patients' retinas, and send the images to doctors for remote review. There are far more patients than graders. The hospital hosted this competition through the Asia Pacific Tele-Ophthalmology Society (APTOS) to obtain a model that could screen and grade the images automatically.

<Deep title="The five grades of diabetic retinopathy" kind="domain">

The competition used the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy scale:

| Grade | Name | Typical findings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | No DR | normal retina |
| 1 | Mild non-proliferative | microaneurysms only (tiny red dots, bulges in capillary walls) |
| 2 | Moderate non-proliferative | more than microaneurysms: haemorrhages, hard exudates (yellow lipid deposits), cotton-wool spots |
| 3 | Severe non-proliferative | many haemorrhages in all four quadrants, venous beading, or intraretinal microvascular abnormalities |
| 4 | Proliferative | new abnormal vessels (neovascularisation) or vitreous haemorrhage |

Grade 2 and above is usually "referable": the patient should see an ophthalmologist. Grade 4 needs urgent treatment. The smallest diagnostic features, microaneurysms, are only a few pixels wide in a downsampled photograph, which is why resolution matters.
</Deep>

## The data

The training set contains 3,662 photographs graded 0–4 by clinicians. The test photographs were hidden and processed by the submitted notebook. Images came from several clinics and cameras over time, and quality varies: some are out of focus, poorly lit or cropped differently.

The 2019 data are small for deep learning. A much larger labelled set was available: the **2015 Kaggle Diabetic Retinopathy Detection** competition (EyePACS, United States), with about 35,000 training images and 53,000 test images on the same five-grade scale. Two smaller public datasets, **IDRiD** (India) and **Messidor** (France), were also permitted.

After the competition closed, several teams near the top were removed for breaking the rules, and the leaderboard was revised.

## Regression instead of classification

Every top solution treated the grade as a **number** and trained a regressor, then converted the output to a class with thresholds. Three reasons:

1. **The metric is a squared-distance agreement.** QWK is closely related to squared error between the grade and the prediction (see the derivation in the PANDA report, where QWK on continuous values reduces to Lin's concordance correlation). A regression loss directly optimises this distance; cross-entropy treats "predict 0 for a 4" and "predict 3 for a 4" as equally wrong.
2. **The classes are ordered.** Regression encodes the order automatically.
3. **Thresholds can be tuned.** The mapping from a continuous score to a grade can be adjusted after training, which exploits the metric's structure.

<Foundation id="soft-labels" />

The winner used the **smooth L1 (Huber) loss**.

<Deep title="Smooth L1: squared near zero, absolute far away" kind="math">

For residual $r = \hat y - y$ and threshold $\beta$ (1 by default in PyTorch):
$$
\ell_\beta(r) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{r^2}{2\beta}, & |r| < \beta,\\[2mm]
|r| - \dfrac{\beta}{2}, & |r| \ge \beta.
\end{cases}
$$
At $|r| = \beta$, both branches equal $\beta/2$, so the loss is continuous. Its derivative is
$$
\ell_\beta'(r) =
\begin{cases}
r/\beta, & |r| < \beta,\\
\operatorname{sign}(r), & |r| \ge \beta,
\end{cases}
$$
which is also continuous at $|r| = \beta$ (both sides equal $\pm 1$).

Near the target, the loss is quadratic, like MSE: the gradient shrinks as the prediction approaches the label, which gives stable convergence. Far from the target, the gradient has constant magnitude, like L1, so a mislabelled image (a grade-4 photograph labelled 0) cannot dominate training with a gradient proportional to the error. With noisy labels from different graders and datasets, this robustness is useful.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Choosing rounding thresholds" kind="math">

The regressor outputs $\hat y \in \mathbb{R}$. Grades are assigned by thresholds $t_1 < t_2 < t_3 < t_4$:
$$
\text{grade}(\hat y) = \#\{k : \hat y \ge t_k\}.
$$
The default is $t = (0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5)$, i.e. rounding. Because QWK depends on the whole confusion matrix, the thresholds that maximise it are generally not the midpoints. Common practice in 2019 was to optimise $t$ on validation predictions with a coordinate search or Nelder–Mead.

The winner changed only the first threshold, from 0.5 to 0.7, which raised his private score from 0.935 to 0.936. Raising $t_1$ means an image needs a higher score to be called "mild" rather than "no DR", which reduces false positives in the large grade-0 class.

With a small validation set, threshold tuning overfits easily; changing one threshold by a small amount is the cautious choice.
</Deep>

### Validation: when nothing correlates

The obvious scheme, training on 2015 data and validating on the 2019 training set, did not correlate with the public leaderboard: the two datasets differ in cameras, population and grading. He therefore combined 2015 and 2019 data for training and used the **public leaderboard** as the validation set. To limit overfitting to it, he evaluated pairs of seeds together and compared epochs only in steps of 5.

This is a deliberate exception to the usual advice never to validate on the public leaderboard. It worked here because the public test set came from the same distribution as the private one, and he restricted how many decisions he made with it.

<Foundation id="cross-validation" />

### Models and training

No preprocessing beyond resizing: no circular cropping and no colour normalisation of the kind popularised by Ben Graham in the 2015 competition. The augmentation did the work instead: contrast (±0.2), brightness (±20), hue (±10), saturation (±20), blur and sharpening, rotation up to 180°, scale (±0.2), shear (±0.2), shift (±0.2) and mirroring. A fundus photograph has no canonical orientation, so full rotation is valid; the colour changes simulate different cameras and exposure.

<Foundation id="data-augmentation" />

**GeM pooling** replaced average pooling and worked better. Lesions such as microaneurysms and haemorrhages are small and scattered; GeM pooling, which interpolates between average and max, gives more weight to strong local activations.

Two models of each architecture, trained with different seeds, were averaged. Public and private scores differ by about 0.1 for every model, so the gap is a property of the two test splits rather than of any one model; the ranking of the models is not the same on both, which is why an ensemble was the safer choice.

### Stage 2: soft pseudo-labels and relabelled external data

Three further sources were added for 10 more epochs of training:

- **The public test set**, labelled with the stage-1 ensemble's continuous predictions (**soft pseudo-labels**).
- **IDRiD**, whose provided grades were averaged with the stage-1 predictions.
- **Messidor**, which uses a different four-level scale. For each Messidor grade, the mean stage-1 prediction over its images was computed, and each image's pseudo-label was bounded to stay near that mean. The write-up's example: in a group with mean 2.2, an image predicted at 1.1 received 1.7; as we read it, predictions were clipped to within 0.5 of their group's mean.

<Foundation id="pseudo-labelling" />

<Deep title="Why average a label with a prediction" kind="math">

Suppose an external dataset's grade $g$ differs systematically from the target scale, and the stage-1 model's prediction $\hat y$ is a noisy estimate on the target scale. A convex combination
$$
\tilde y = \lambda g + (1 - \lambda)\hat y
$$
has lower variance than either alone if the errors are not perfectly correlated. For independent errors with variances $\sigma_g^2$ and $\sigma_{\hat y}^2$, the variance of the combination is $\lambda^2\sigma_g^2 + (1-\lambda)^2\sigma_{\hat y}^2$; setting its derivative $2\lambda\sigma_g^2 - 2(1-\lambda)\sigma_{\hat y}^2$ to zero gives $\lambda^* = \sigma_{\hat y}^2 / (\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_{\hat y}^2)$, which is $1/2$ when both are equally reliable. Averaging with $\lambda = 1/2$ is the natural choice when neither source is known to be better.

For Messidor, the grade is on a different scale, so it is used only to constrain the prediction to the right region: bounding keeps the model's fine-grained ranking within a grade while preventing it from drifting into another grade's range.
</Deep>

The stage-2 ensemble scored 0.850 public and 0.935 private; with the first threshold moved to 0.7, the final private score was 0.936.

## Second place after the cleanup: EfficientNet and pseudo-labelling

Artur Ispiriants and Mykhailo Matviiv (team ods.ai) originally finished fourth and moved to second after the leaderboard was cleaned.

- **Pre-training on the 2015 data** at full size (this alone scored 0.73–0.75 public), then fine-tuning on 2019 data.
- **EfficientNet-B3 (300 px), B4 (460), B5 (456)**: they report EfficientNet as the only family that worked for them; SE-ResNeXt and DenseNet were worse.
- Cropping of black background; Ben Graham's colour preprocessing did not help.
- Heavy augmentation with albumentations: blur, flips, brightness-contrast, shift-scale-rotate, elastic and grid distortion, transposition, HSV shifts, CLAHE and coarse dropout.
- Pre-training for about 80 epochs (B3) or 15 (B5), then about 50 or 15 epochs on 2019 data; averaging of the best epochs; flip TTA.
- **Two rounds of pseudo-labelling** on the 2019 test set and the 2015 test set, which they describe as a large gain.

<Foundation id="transfer-learning" />

The two solutions differ in architecture and preprocessing but agree on the essentials: use the 2015 data, regress the grade, augment heavily, and add pseudo-labels.

## How this compares with other competitions

- **PANDA prostate grading** is the other ordinal competition in the collection with QWK. There the difficulty was label noise and gigapixel images; here it is domain shift and a small target dataset. Both winners regressed the grade.
- **SIIM-ISIC melanoma (2020)** also relied heavily on an earlier competition's data (ISIC 2017–2019) to supplement a small and shifted target set.
- **Diabetic retinopathy** is the application in which autonomous AI screening was first approved by the US FDA (IDx-DR, 2018, supported by the pivotal trial of Abràmoff et al.). The Kaggle competitions of 2015 and 2019 were part of the path to that point.
