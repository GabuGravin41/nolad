## Clinical background

**Diabetic retinopathy** is damage to the small blood vessels of the retina caused by long-standing high blood glucose. The competition description names it a leading cause of blindness among adults of working age. Loss of vision can largely be prevented if the disease is detected early and treated with laser or with injections into the eye, and people with diabetes are therefore advised to have their retinas examined at regular intervals.

The examination is a review of **fundus photographs**, images of the back of the eye, by an ophthalmologist or a trained grader. Aravind Eye Hospital in India runs an outreach programme in which technicians travel to rural areas, photograph patients' retinas, and send the images to doctors for remote review. The hospital hosted this competition, through the 4th symposium of the Asia Pacific Tele-Ophthalmology Society (APTOS), to obtain a model that detects the disease and grades its severity automatically.

<Deep title="The five grades of diabetic retinopathy" kind="domain">

The labels follow the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy scale.

| Grade | Name | Typical findings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | No DR | normal retina |
| 1 | Mild non-proliferative | microaneurysms only (small red dots, bulges in capillary walls) |
| 2 | Moderate non-proliferative | more than microaneurysms: haemorrhages, hard exudates (yellow lipid deposits), cotton-wool spots |
| 3 | Severe non-proliferative | many haemorrhages in all four quadrants, venous beading, or intraretinal microvascular abnormalities |
| 4 | Proliferative | new abnormal vessels (neovascularisation) or vitreous haemorrhage |

Grade 2 and above is usually considered referable: the patient should be seen by an ophthalmologist. Grade 4 requires urgent treatment. Microaneurysms, the earliest lesions, cover only a few pixels in a downsampled photograph, so the input resolution limits which lesions a model can resolve.
</Deep>

## Data

The training set contains 3,662 photographs graded 0 to 4 by clinicians. The test photographs were hidden and were processed by the submitted notebook, without internet access.

A larger labelled dataset was allowed as external data: that of the **2015 Kaggle Diabetic Retinopathy Detection** competition (EyePACS, United States), with about 35,000 training and 53,000 test images graded on the same five-level scale. Two smaller public datasets, **IDRiD** (India) and **Messidor** (France), were also permitted.

After the competition closed, several teams near the top of the leaderboard were removed for breaking the rules, and the final ranking was revised.

## Evaluation metric

<Foundation id="quadratic-weighted-kappa" />

With $N = 5$ grades, the disagreement weight is $w_{ij} = (i-j)^2 / 16$. A prediction one grade away from the reference has weight $1/16 = 0.0625$, two grades away $4/16 = 0.25$, three grades away $9/16 \approx 0.56$, and four grades away $1$.

<Plot series={[{fn: "qwk-weight", params: {n: 5}, label: "w = (i − j)² / 16"}]} x={[0, 4]} y={[0, 1]} xLabel="|predicted − true| grade" yLabel="penalty weight" caption="The quadratic weight as a function of the distance between the predicted and the true grade. A disagreement of two grades has four times the weight of a disagreement of one." />

<Deep title="Quadratic weighted kappa in terms of means, variances and covariance" kind="math">

Let $n$ be the number of images, $y_1, \dots, y_n \in \{0, \dots, 4\}$ the reference grades and $\hat y_1, \dots, \hat y_n \in \{0, \dots, 4\}$ the predicted grades. Write $\mu_y, \mu_{\hat y}$ for their means, $\sigma_y^2, \sigma_{\hat y}^2$ for their variances and $\sigma_{y\hat y}$ for their covariance, all computed with the factor $1/n$.

**Step 1: remove the constant.** The factor $1/(N-1)^2$ appears in every weight of both sums and cancels:

$$
\kappa = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,j} (i-j)^2\, O_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j} (i-j)^2\, E_{ij}} .
$$

**Step 2: the numerator.** $O_{ij}$ counts the images with reference grade $i$ and predicted grade $j$, so summing $(i-j)^2 O_{ij}$ over all cells is the same as summing $(y_t - \hat y_t)^2$ over all images $t$. Dividing by $n$ gives the mean squared error:

$$
\mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j} (i-j)^2\, O_{ij} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} (y_t - \hat y_t)^2 .
$$

**Step 3: the denominator.** $E_{ij} = n\,P_y(i)\,P_{\hat y}(j)$, where $P_y$ and $P_{\hat y}$ are the two histograms normalised to sum to 1. Dividing by $n$ gives the expected squared difference between two independent random variables $Y$ and $\hat Y'$ with these distributions:

$$
D = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j} (i-j)^2\, E_{ij} = \mathbb{E}\big[(Y - \hat Y')^2\big].
$$

For independent variables, the variance of the difference is the sum of the variances, so

$$
D = \operatorname{Var}(Y - \hat Y') + \big(\mathbb{E}[Y - \hat Y']\big)^2 = \sigma_y^2 + \sigma_{\hat y}^2 + (\mu_y - \mu_{\hat y})^2 .
$$

**Step 4: expand the MSE** in the same moments:

$$
\mathrm{MSE} = \sigma_y^2 + \sigma_{\hat y}^2 - 2\sigma_{y\hat y} + (\mu_y - \mu_{\hat y})^2 = D - 2\sigma_{y\hat y}.
$$

**Step 5: combine.** Dividing numerator and denominator of Step 1 by $n$ gives $\kappa = 1 - \mathrm{MSE}/D$, and with Step 4

$$
\kappa = \frac{D - \mathrm{MSE}}{D} = \frac{2\,\sigma_{y\hat y}}{\sigma_y^2 + \sigma_{\hat y}^2 + (\mu_y - \mu_{\hat y})^2} .
$$

This is Lin's concordance correlation coefficient between the reference and the predicted grades. Quadratic weighted kappa is therefore one minus the squared error, measured relative to the squared error expected by chance. For fixed histograms of predicted grades, $D$ is fixed and maximising $\kappa$ is equivalent to minimising the squared error. The histogram of predictions also enters $D$, so the two objectives are not identical in general.
</Deep>

## Regression on the grade

The winner treated the grade as a real number, trained the networks as regressors, and converted the continuous output to a grade with thresholds. Three properties of the task relate to this choice.

1. **The metric penalises squared distance.** From the derivation above, $\kappa = 1 - \mathrm{MSE}/D$. A regression loss penalises the distance between the prediction and the grade. The cross-entropy loss of a classifier depends only on the probability assigned to the correct grade, so it does not distinguish between a prediction of 0 and a prediction of 3 for an image of grade 4.
2. **The grades are ordered.** A single real-valued output represents the order of the grades.
3. **Thresholds can be adjusted after training.** The mapping from the continuous output to a grade can be tuned without retraining.

<Foundation id="soft-labels" />

The winner used the **smooth L1 loss**, also called the Huber loss.

<Deep title="Smooth L1 loss" kind="math">

Let $\hat y \in \mathbb{R}$ be the prediction, $y$ the target, $r = \hat y - y$ the residual and $\beta > 0$ a threshold (1 by default in PyTorch). The loss is

$$
\ell_\beta(r) =
\begin{cases}
r^2 / (2\beta), & |r| < \beta,\\[1mm]
|r| - \beta / 2, & |r| \ge \beta.
\end{cases}
$$

At $|r| = \beta$ the first branch gives $\beta^2 / (2\beta) = \beta/2$ and the second gives $\beta - \beta/2 = \beta/2$, so the loss is continuous. Its derivative is

$$
\ell_\beta'(r) =
\begin{cases}
r / \beta, & |r| < \beta,\\
\operatorname{sign}(r), & |r| \ge \beta,
\end{cases}
$$

and at $r = \pm\beta$ both branches give $\pm 1$, so the derivative is continuous as well.

For $|r| < \beta$ the loss is quadratic, as in mean squared error, and the gradient decreases as the prediction approaches the target. For $|r| \ge \beta$ the gradient has magnitude 1 whatever the size of the error. A grade-4 photograph labelled 0 therefore contributes a gradient of magnitude 1 to the regressor, where mean squared error would contribute one proportional to the error of 4. This limits the influence of wrongly labelled images, which are more likely when the training data come from several datasets and graders.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Rounding thresholds" kind="math">

The regressor outputs $\hat y \in \mathbb{R}$. A grade is assigned with thresholds $t_1 < t_2 < t_3 < t_4$ by counting the thresholds that $\hat y$ reaches:

$$
\mathrm{grade}(\hat y) = \#\{\, k \in \{1,2,3,4\} : \hat y \ge t_k \,\}.
$$

The choice $t = (0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5)$ is ordinary rounding. Because $\kappa$ depends on the whole confusion matrix, the thresholds that maximise it are in general not the midpoints between grades. They can be optimised on validation predictions, for example by coordinate search or with the Nelder–Mead method, at the risk of fitting the noise of a small validation set.

The winner changed only the first threshold, from 0.5 to 0.7, which raised his private score from 0.935 to 0.936. Images with $0.5 \le \hat y < 0.7$, graded 1 (mild) before the change, are graded 0 after it.
</Deep>

### Validation

The author first trained on the 2015 data and validated on the 2019 training set. Scores on this validation set did not correlate with the public leaderboard. The write-up does not identify the cause; the two datasets come from different countries and programmes. He then combined the 2015 and 2019 data for training and used the **public leaderboard** as the validation set. To reduce overfitting to it, he evaluated models in pairs of random seeds and compared training epochs only in steps of 5.

<Foundation id="cross-validation" />

### Models and training

The only preprocessing was resizing. The author did not crop the circular field of the fundus or normalise its colour, as Ben Graham, winner of the 2015 competition, had done. The augmentation consisted of changes to contrast (±0.2), brightness (±20), hue (±10) and saturation (±20), blurring and sharpening, rotation by up to 180°, scaling (±0.2), shearing (±0.2), shifting (±0.2) and mirroring. A fundus photograph has no fixed orientation, so rotations by any angle produce valid images.

<Foundation id="data-augmentation" />

The author replaced global average pooling with **generalised-mean (GeM) pooling** (Radenović et al., 2018) and reports that it performed better.

<Deep title="Generalised-mean pooling" kind="math">

Let $x_{c,v} \ge 0$ be the activation of channel $c$ at spatial position $v$ in the last feature map, after the ReLU, and let $\Omega$ be the set of positions. GeM pooling with exponent $p \ge 1$ gives, for each channel,

$$
\mathrm{GeM}_p(x_c) = \Big( \frac{1}{|\Omega|} \sum_{v \in \Omega} x_{c,v}^{\,p} \Big)^{1/p} .
$$

For $p = 1$ this is the average. As $p \to \infty$ it tends to the maximum $\max_v x_{c,v}$, because the largest term dominates the sum. Intermediate values give strong local activations more weight than the average does. Radenović et al. learn $p$ with the network; the write-up does not state the value used.
</Deep>

The ensemble scored higher than every single model on both.

### Stage 2: soft pseudo-labels and adjusted external labels

Three further sources were added, and training continued for 10 more epochs.

- **The public test set**, labelled with the continuous predictions of the stage-1 ensemble (**soft pseudo-labels**).
- **IDRiD**, with the provided grades averaged with the stage-1 predictions.
- **Messidor**, which uses a four-level scale. For each Messidor grade, the mean stage-1 prediction over its images was computed, and the pseudo-label of each image was bounded to stay near that mean. In the write-up's example, in a group with mean 2.2, an image predicted at 1.1 received the label 1.7. The example is consistent with clipping each prediction to within 0.5 of its group's mean ($2.2 - 0.5 = 1.7$); this is Nolad's reading and not a statement of the write-up.

<Foundation id="pseudo-labelling" />

<Deep title="Averaging a label with a prediction" kind="math">

Let $g$ be the grade given in an external dataset and $\hat y$ the stage-1 prediction, and suppose that both are unbiased estimates of the true grade $y$ on the competition's scale, with independent errors of variances $\sigma_g^2$ and $\sigma_{\hat y}^2$. Consider the combination

$$
\tilde y = \lambda g + (1 - \lambda)\hat y, \qquad \lambda \in [0,1].
$$

Its error is $\lambda (g - y) + (1-\lambda)(\hat y - y)$, and for independent errors its variance is

$$
V(\lambda) = \lambda^2\sigma_g^2 + (1-\lambda)^2\sigma_{\hat y}^2 .
$$

Setting the derivative to zero,

$$
V'(\lambda) = 2\lambda\sigma_g^2 - 2(1-\lambda)\sigma_{\hat y}^2 = 0,
$$

and collecting the terms in $\lambda$ gives $\lambda(\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_{\hat y}^2) = \sigma_{\hat y}^2$, so

$$
\lambda^* = \frac{\sigma_{\hat y}^2}{\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_{\hat y}^2} .
$$

When the two variances are equal, $\lambda^* = 1/2$, which is the plain average used for IDRiD, and $V(1/2) = \sigma^2/2$, half the variance of either source. If the errors are correlated, the reduction is smaller.

For Messidor the given grade is on a different scale, so it cannot be averaged with a prediction on the competition's scale. It is used instead to bound the prediction: the model's ordering of images within a Messidor grade is kept, while the label is kept near the group's mean.
</Deep>

The stage-2 ensemble scored 0.850 on the public and 0.935 on the private test set. With the first threshold moved to 0.7, the final private score was 0.936.

## Second place: EfficientNet and pseudo-labelling

Artur Ispiriants and Mykhailo Matviiv (team ods.ai) finished fourth on the private leaderboard and were placed second after it was revised.

- **Pre-training on the 2015 data** at full size, which alone scored 0.73–0.75 on the public leaderboard, followed by fine-tuning on the 2019 data.
- **EfficientNet-B3 (300 pixels), B4 (460) and B5 (456).** They report that EfficientNet was the only architecture family that worked for them; SE-ResNeXt and DenseNet gave worse results.
- Cropping of the black background around the fundus. Ben Graham's colour preprocessing did not help.
- Augmentation with the albumentations library: blur, flips, brightness and contrast, shift–scale–rotate, elastic and grid distortion, transposition, HSV shifts, CLAHE and coarse dropout.
- About 80 (B3) or 15 (B5) epochs of pre-training, followed by about 50 or 15 epochs on the 2019 data; averaging of the best epochs; test-time augmentation with flips.
- **Two rounds of pseudo-labelling** on the 2019 test set and the 2015 test set, which they describe as a large gain.

<Foundation id="transfer-learning" />

Both solutions used the 2015 data, strong augmentation and pseudo-labels on the test data. They differ in architecture and in preprocessing: the winner resized the images without cropping, and the second-place team cropped the background.

## Comparison with other competitions

- **PANDA prostate cancer grading (2020)** is the other competition in the collection scored by quadratic weighted kappa. Its input was gigapixel pathology slides with noisy labels, and its solutions also used regression heads.
- **SIIM-ISIC melanoma classification (2020)** also relied on data from earlier challenges (ISIC 2017–2019) to supplement a small target dataset.
- **Diabetic retinopathy** is the application in which the US FDA first authorised an autonomous AI diagnostic system, IDx-DR, in 2018, supported by the pivotal trial of Abràmoff et al. (2018).

### Considerations for deployment

1. **Domain shift.** Validation on the 2019 data did not predict leaderboard scores for a model trained on the 2015 data. Performance at a new site, with its own cameras and population, has to be measured there.
2. **Label scales.** The external datasets were graded by different graders, and their labels were adjusted towards the model's own predictions. A deployed model should be validated against grades from the programme that will use it.
3. **Scope.** The competition asked only for the grade of diabetic retinopathy. Image-quality assessment and detection of other eye disease are required in screening and were not part of the task.
