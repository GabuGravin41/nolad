## Scientific background

Many cellular processes are carried out by **protein complexes**, assemblies of several protein chains: ribosomes synthesise proteins, and ferritin stores iron, for example. The number, position and arrangement of these complexes inside a cell are informative about how the cell functions. **Cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET)** images them in their native environment in three dimensions, at a resolution at which individual complexes are visible.

<Deep title="How cryo-electron tomography works" kind="domain">

1. **Vitrification.** The sample (purified proteins, or a thin section of a cell) is frozen fast enough that water solidifies as an amorphous glass without forming ice crystals. Structures are preserved close to their native state, and no stain is used.
2. **Tilt series.** In the electron microscope the sample is tilted in steps, typically from about −60° to +60°, and imaged at each angle. The total electron dose is kept low to limit radiation damage, so each image has a low signal-to-noise ratio.
3. **Reconstruction.** The projections are combined into a 3D volume, the **tomogram**, by a reconstruction procedure related to that of CT. Because the tilt range stops short of ±90°, a wedge of directions in Fourier space is never sampled (the "missing wedge"), and structures appear elongated along the beam axis.
4. **Particle picking.** Each copy of a complex of interest is located in the tomogram. The copies are then extracted and averaged (subtomogram averaging) to obtain a structure at higher resolution than any single copy.

Particle picking has traditionally been done by template matching (cross-correlation of the tomogram with a known structure) or by manual annotation. Both are slow at low signal-to-noise ratio, and automatic picking is the subject of this competition.
</Deep>

The competition asked participants to locate the centres of five kinds of complex in tomograms of a prepared sample. The organisers classed three as easy and two as hard, and a sixth complex, β-amylase, was present in the data but not scored:

| Particle | Organisers' class | Weight in the score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Apo-ferritin | easy | 1 |
| Ribosome | easy | 1 |
| Virus-like particle | easy | 1 |
| Thyroglobulin | hard | 2 |
| β-galactosidase | hard | 2 |
| β-amylase | not scored | 0 |

## Data

The training data were tomograms from seven experiments, each of about 630 × 630 × 184 voxels with a voxel size of about 10 Å, together with the centre coordinates of every annotated particle. Each tomogram contains many particles, so the training set has many positive examples, but they come from only seven volumes.

The hidden test set had about 500 tomograms. Submissions ran in a Kaggle notebook without internet access, on two T4 GPUs, within 12 hours. The time available per tomogram was therefore

$$
\frac{12 \times 60\ \text{min}}{500} = 1.44\ \text{min} \approx 1.5\ \text{min}.
$$

## Evaluation metric

For each particle type $c$, a predicted point is a true positive if it lies within $\tfrac12 r_c$ of a ground-truth centre of type $c$ that has not already been matched, where $r_c$ is the radius of particle type $c$. Unmatched predictions are false positives and unmatched ground-truth centres are false negatives. The counts $\mathrm{TP}_c$, $\mathrm{FP}_c$ and $\mathrm{FN}_c$ are summed over all test tomograms (micro-averaging) before the score is computed.

<Foundation id="precision-recall-fbeta" />

With precision $P_c = \mathrm{TP}_c/(\mathrm{TP}_c + \mathrm{FP}_c)$ and recall $R_c = \mathrm{TP}_c/(\mathrm{TP}_c + \mathrm{FN}_c)$, the per-type score is

$$
F_{\beta,c} = \frac{(1+\beta^2)\,P_c\,R_c}{\beta^2 P_c + R_c}, \qquad \beta = 4 .
$$

Substituting the definitions of $P_c$ and $R_c$ and multiplying numerator and denominator by $(\mathrm{TP}_c + \mathrm{FP}_c)(\mathrm{TP}_c + \mathrm{FN}_c)/\mathrm{TP}_c$ gives the same score in counts:

$$
F_{\beta,c} = \frac{(1+\beta^2)\,\mathrm{TP}_c}{(1+\beta^2)\,\mathrm{TP}_c + \beta^2\,\mathrm{FN}_c + \mathrm{FP}_c} .
$$

In the denominator a false negative has coefficient $\beta^2 = 16$ and a false positive coefficient 1. The final score is the weighted mean over the five scored types, with weights $w_c$ from the table above:

$$
\text{Score} = \frac{\sum_c w_c\,F_{4,c}}{\sum_c w_c}
= \frac{F_{\text{apo}} + F_{\text{ribo}} + F_{\text{VLP}} + 2F_{\text{thyro}} + 2F_{\beta\text{-gal}}}{7} .
$$

The two hard types together carry $4/7 \approx 57\%$ of the score.

**Worked example.** Consider a type with 100 true particles and two submissions. Submission A finds 90 of them and adds 50 false detections; submission B finds 80 and adds 10. With $1+\beta^2 = 17$:

$$
F_A = \frac{17 \cdot 90}{17 \cdot 90 + 16 \cdot 10 + 50} = \frac{1530}{1740} \approx 0.879,
\qquad
F_B = \frac{17 \cdot 80}{17 \cdot 80 + 16 \cdot 20 + 10} = \frac{1360}{1690} \approx 0.805 .
$$

Submission A scores higher although its precision is $90/140 \approx 0.64$ and that of B is $80/90 \approx 0.89$.

<Deep title="The threshold at which an extra prediction raises the score" kind="math">

Write the count form as $F = N/D$ with $N = (1+\beta^2)\,\mathrm{TP}$ and $D = (1+\beta^2)\,\mathrm{TP} + \beta^2\,\mathrm{FN} + \mathrm{FP}$ for one particle type. Consider adding one candidate point that is a true positive with probability $q$.

If it is correct, $\mathrm{TP}$ rises by 1 and $\mathrm{FN}$ falls by 1, so $N$ rises by $1+\beta^2$ and $D$ changes by $(1+\beta^2) - \beta^2 = 1$.

If it is wrong, $\mathrm{FP}$ rises by 1, so $N$ is unchanged and $D$ rises by 1.

The denominator becomes $D+1$ in both cases, so the expected score after adding the point is

$$
\mathbb{E}[F'] = \frac{N + q\,(1+\beta^2)}{D + 1} .
$$

Adding the point raises the expected score when $\mathbb{E}[F'] > N/D$. Multiplying both sides by $D(D+1)$:

$$
D\,N + q\,(1+\beta^2)\,D > N\,D + N .
$$

Cancelling $DN$ and dividing by $(1+\beta^2)\,D$:

$$
q > \frac{F}{1+\beta^2} .
$$

For $\beta = 4$ and a current score of $F = 0.8$, any candidate with $q > 0.8/17 \approx 0.047$ is worth submitting. At $\beta = 1$ the same bound would be $0.8/2 = 0.4$. The bound accounts for the low per-class thresholds of competitive submissions, and for their unsuitability for applications that need high precision (see "Considerations for deployment").
</Deep>

## Winning solution

Christof Henkel and Eugene Khvedchenya each reached the top five independently, with different approaches. They then merged teams and combined their models, which placed first. Both used MONAI, a PyTorch library for medical imaging; both trained their models from scratch, and neither used external or simulated data.

### Segmentation model

This part is Henkel's.

**Validation.** Seven folds were used, one per training tomogram. After each epoch a per-class threshold was chosen by grid search on the validation tomogram. After all folds had been trained, the thresholds were recalibrated on out-of-fold predictions: for each fold, the threshold was fitted on the predictions for the other six folds, the resulting $F_4$ curves were averaged, and the threshold with the highest average was taken.

**Preprocessing.** Each tomogram was standardised to zero mean and unit variance before being cut into patches.

**Augmentation.** Random crops, flips along each axis and rotations from MONAI were combined with Henkel's own implementation of MixUp, which blends pairs of training patches and their targets. Henkel reports MixUp as the main measure against overfitting on the seven training volumes.

<Foundation id="data-augmentation" />

**Development of the model.** Henkel first trained a U-Net whose target was a small 3D Gaussian at each particle centre, the usual heatmap formulation for point detection. He then tried MONAI's example object detector, which performed well without modification, and examined where its advantage came from. He reports two findings:

1. The output of the penultimate decoder stage, at half the input resolution, gave more accurate centres than the output of the last stage.
2. Regressing bounding boxes added almost nothing, because all particles of one type have the same size.

A pixel-wise loss on the penultimate feature map is therefore sufficient, and the final model is a U-Net with its last decoder stage removed, which Henkel calls a "partly U-Net". He also found that the Gaussian target can be dropped if the background class receives a low weight: the target is then a single voxel at each particle centre.

<Foundation id="unet" />

**Loss function.** The output has 7 classes: background, the five scored particle types and β-amylase. Let $v$ index the voxels of the output block, $y_v \in \{0, \dots, 6\}$ the class of voxel $v$ (0 for background), and $p_k(v) \in [0,1]$ the predicted probability of class $k$ at $v$. With class weights $w_k$, the weighted cross-entropy is

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{\sum_v w_{y_v}\,\log p_{y_v}(v)}{\sum_v w_{y_v}}, \qquad w_0 = 1, \qquad w_1 = \dots = w_6 = 256 .
$$

<Deep title="Class balance with single-voxel targets" kind="math">

The input patch is $96^3$ voxels and the loss is computed on the $48^3$ output of the penultimate decoder stage, which has

$$
48^3 = 110{,}592
$$

voxels. Suppose, for illustration, that a block contains $n = 20$ particle centres. Without weights the positive voxels make up

$$
\frac{20}{110{,}592} \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-4}
$$

of the terms in the loss, and a model that predicts background everywhere attains a low loss. With weight 256 on each positive voxel, their share of the total weight in the denominator becomes

$$
\frac{256 \cdot 20}{256 \cdot 20 + (110{,}592 - 20)} = \frac{5{,}120}{115{,}692} \approx 0.044,
$$

an increase by a factor of about 240. The loss then rewards a sharp probability peak at each centre. A Gaussian target would additionally require the network to reproduce the profile of the Gaussian around each centre, while the metric depends only on the position of the peak.
</Deep>

**β-amylase as a separate class.** β-amylase is not scored, but Henkel kept it as its own class and reports that this helped the model distinguish β-galactosidase. Without the class, β-amylase particles would be labelled as background.

**Architecture and training.** The network is MONAI's `FlexibleUNet` with ResNet-34 and EfficientNet-B3 encoders. Inputs are $96^3$-voxel patches, and the loss is computed on the $48^3$ penultimate output. Training used a cosine learning-rate schedule with a peak of $10^{-3}$, mixed precision and an effective batch size of 32 patches. An ensemble of six checkpoints of this model alone ran in under 2 hours and, according to the write-up, would have placed seventh on the leaderboard.

Henkel lists the following as not helping: external or simulated data, other augmentations, and Tversky and Dice losses. He concludes that relatively small models are sufficient and that the design of the loss has the largest effect.

### Point-detection model

This part is Khvedchenya's.

**Baseline.** His baseline was a 3D U-Net with a SegResNet backbone trained on Gaussian heatmaps, with the penalty-reduced focal loss of CenterNet (Zhou et al., 2019) and non-maximum suppression. It scored about 0.740 on the leaderboard. He then replaced it with an anchor-free point detector.

<Foundation id="detection-basics" />

**Architecture.** SegResNet and DynUNet backbones from MONAI are followed by a custom head. At every cell of a stride-2 output grid, the head predicts class probabilities and a 3D offset from the cell to the particle centre. Because there are no boxes, the intersection over union (IoU) used to match box predictions to targets is replaced by a similarity between points, the object keypoint similarity:

$$
s(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{g}) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{g} \rVert^2}{2 r^2}\right),
$$

where $\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is a predicted centre, $\mathbf{g} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ a ground-truth centre and $r > 0$ the radius of the particle type. The similarity is 1 for coincident points. At the scoring tolerance $\lVert \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{g}\rVert = r/2$ it is $\exp(-\tfrac18) \approx 0.88$, and at a distance of one radius it is $\exp(-\tfrac12) \approx 0.61$.

**Loss function.** The loss is modelled on that of PP-YOLO, with changes:

- Ground-truth particles are assigned to predictions by keypoint similarity, keeping the top $k$ predictions for each target.
- The class map is trained with the **varifocal loss**, a focal-type loss in which the target for a positive prediction is its localisation quality (here the keypoint similarity) instead of 1.
- The offsets are trained with a loss based on the keypoint similarity.

<Foundation id="focal-loss" />

**Training.** One fold trained in about 2 hours on four RTX 3090 GPUs and scored 0.752 on the leaderboard. Five folds were used. Each epoch drew a fixed number of random crops per tomogram and a fixed number of crops centred near particles. Augmentations were rotations about the z-axis by up to ±180° and about the x- and y-axes by up to ±10°, scaling by ±5%, and flips along all axes. Erasing particles, copying particles between tomograms and particle-level mixup gave no gain.

**Output stride.** Predicting at stride 2 was about 50% faster than predicting at full resolution, which scored only about 0.002 higher. Stride 4 worked but scored lower. A model with two heads, at strides 2 and 4, gave no gain; during training the large particles were assigned mostly to the stride-4 head and the small ones to the stride-2 head.

### Combining the two models

Both models output, for each class, a map of the probability that a particle centre lies at each voxel. They were trained with different architectures and losses, and their values follow different distributions, so a direct average would be dominated by the model with the larger values. The team mapped one model's values onto the other's distribution by rank, separately for each class, and then averaged the maps before peak detection.

Let $A$ and $B$ be the two maps for one class, each with $n$ voxels. Let $a_{(1)} \le a_{(2)} \le \dots \le a_{(n)}$ be the values of $A$ in increasing order, and let $\operatorname{rank}_B(v) \in \{1, \dots, n\}$ be the position of voxel $v$ when the values of $B$ are sorted in increasing order. The matched map is

$$
\tilde B(v) = a_{(\operatorname{rank}_B(v))} .
$$

$\tilde B$ has exactly the same multiset of values as $A$, and it orders the voxels as $B$ does: if $B(u) < B(v)$ then $\tilde B(u) \le \tilde B(v)$. The blended map is $\tfrac12\big(A + \tilde B\big)$.

<Deep title="Quantile matching in NumPy" kind="code">

A Nolad illustration of the operation described in the write-up; it is not the team's code.

```python title="quantile_match.py (A Nolad illustration)"
import numpy as np

def quantile_match(b: np.ndarray, a: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Return b with its values replaced by a's values of the same rank."""
    order_b = np.argsort(b, axis=None)            # voxel indices of b, lowest to highest
    sorted_a = np.sort(a, axis=None)              # a's values, lowest to highest
    out = np.empty(b.size, dtype=a.dtype)
    out[order_b] = sorted_a                       # k-th smallest voxel of b gets a's k-th smallest value
    return out.reshape(b.shape)

blended = 0.5 * seg_map + 0.5 * quantile_match(det_map, seg_map)
```

Both maps must have the same number of voxels; if the two models predict at different strides, one map is resampled first. Because the mapping is monotonic, it does not change which of two voxels $B$ ranks higher; it replaces $B$'s confidence values with $A$'s.
</Deep>

### Inference within the time limit

Both models were exported to ONNX and compiled with NVIDIA TensorRT in FP16. The team reports a speed-up of about 200% over eager PyTorch. Predictions ran on both T4 GPUs in parallel, and the time saved allowed a larger ensemble within the 12-hour limit.

## Comparison with other competitions

The task of locating the centres of many small objects of known size in a large, noisy 3D volume also occurs in fluorescence and electron microscopy and in medical imaging, where the RSNA intracranial aneurysm competition supplied aneurysm centres without masks. The findings of the winning team that do not depend on cryo-ET are the following. When all objects of a class have the same size, the models predicted centres without shapes or boxes. Single-voxel targets with strong positive weighting replaced Gaussian heatmaps. Two models with different output distributions were combined after quantile matching.

### Considerations for deployment

The competition metric with β = 4 leads to low thresholds (see the derivation under "Evaluation metric"). In subtomogram averaging, false positives are averaged together with true particles and degrade the resulting structure, so a laboratory would raise the per-class thresholds until precision is acceptable. The per-class thresholds can be refitted on out-of-fold predictions for a smaller β or for a target precision, in the same way as the team fitted them for β = 4.
