## Clinical background

A **pulmonary embolism (PE)** is a blockage of a pulmonary artery, usually by a blood clot that has travelled from the deep veins of the legs. It obstructs blood flow through the lungs, and a large obstruction strains the right ventricle of the heart, which has to pump against it. The competition description cites 60,000 to 100,000 deaths a year in the United States from PE.

The standard diagnostic test is **CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA)**: a CT scan of the chest acquired while iodinated contrast fills the pulmonary arteries. The contrast-filled arteries appear bright, and a clot appears as a darker **filling defect** inside a bright vessel. A study contains hundreds of slices, and the radiologist follows the arterial tree from the main pulmonary artery out to the small subsegmental branches. The description names the time radiologists spend on these studies and over-diagnosis as problems that an automated reader could help with.

<Deep title="What a PE report states" kind="domain">

A radiology report of a CTPA states more than the presence of an embolus.

- **Location.** A *central* embolus lies in the main or a lobar pulmonary artery; more peripheral emboli are segmental or subsegmental. The competition also labels whether emboli are present in the **left** or **right** lung, or both.
- **Chronicity.** An **acute** clot is recent and usually lies in the centre of the vessel lumen. A **chronic** clot has become organised tissue attached to the vessel wall, and appears as a web or an eccentric thickening of the wall. Some patients have **acute and chronic** emboli together. Treatment and prognosis differ between the two.
- **Right-heart strain.** A large obstruction dilates the right ventricle (RV). The ratio of the RV diameter to the left-ventricular (LV) diameter on axial CT is a standard marker; **RV/LV ≥ 1** indicates strain and a higher risk, and can lead to more aggressive treatment such as thrombolysis.
- **Indeterminate.** When contrast timing or motion makes the study unreadable, it is reported as indeterminate.
</Deep>

## Data

The RSNA and the Society of Thoracic Radiology assembled the dataset, which is described by Colak et al. (2021). The training set contains about 7,300 studies (7,279) and about 1.8 million slices. The number of slices per study ranges from about 100 to more than 1,000.

Each **slice** has one label: an embolus is visible on this image. Each **study** has nine exam labels, listed below with their weights in the metric.

| Label | Weight $w_j$ |
| --- | --- |
| negative_exam_for_pe | 0.0736 |
| indeterminate | 0.0920 |
| chronic_pe | 0.1043 |
| acute_and_chronic_pe | 0.1043 |
| central_pe | 0.1877 |
| leftsided_pe | 0.0626 |
| rightsided_pe | 0.0626 |
| rv_lv_ratio_gte_1 | 0.2347 |
| rv_lv_ratio_lt_1 | 0.0782 |

The nine weights sum to 1.0000. The hidden test set was processed by a submitted notebook without internet access, within 9 hours.

## Evaluation metric

Let $i$ index the studies. Study $i$ has exam labels $y_{ij} \in \{0,1\}$ with predictions $p_{ij} \in (0,1)$ for $j = 1, \dots, 9$, and $n_i$ slices with image labels $y_{ik} \in \{0,1\}$ and predictions $p_{ik} \in (0,1)$ for $k = 1, \dots, n_i$. The binary log loss is $\ell(y, p) = -\big[\,y\log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\,\big]$. Define

- $w_j$, the weight of exam label $j$ from the table above;
- $w_{\mathrm{img}} = 0.0736$, the base weight of an image label (0.07361963 in the metric code);
- $q_i \in [0,1]$, the fraction of slices of study $i$ that are positive.

The exam and image parts of study $i$ are

$$
L_{\mathrm{exam}, i} = \sum_{j=1}^{9} w_j\,\ell(y_{ij}, p_{ij}),
\qquad
L_{\mathrm{img}, i} = \sum_{k=1}^{n_i} w_{\mathrm{img}}\, q_i\, \ell(y_{ik}, p_{ik}).
$$

The score is the sum of both parts over all studies divided by the sum of all weights used:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{\sum_i \big( L_{\mathrm{exam}, i} + L_{\mathrm{img}, i} \big)}{\sum_i \big( W_{\mathrm{exam}} + W_{\mathrm{img}, i} \big)},
\qquad
W_{\mathrm{exam}} = \sum_{j=1}^{9} w_j = 1,
\qquad
W_{\mathrm{img}, i} = \sum_{k=1}^{n_i} w_{\mathrm{img}}\, q_i .
$$

Every slice of study $i$ has the same weight, so the sum over slices is a product. Writing $n_i^{+} = q_i n_i$ for the number of positive slices:

$$
W_{\mathrm{img}, i} = w_{\mathrm{img}}\, q_i\, n_i = w_{\mathrm{img}}\, n_i^{+} .
$$

The total image weight of a study is therefore proportional to its number of positive slices. In a negative study ($n_i^{+} = 0$) the image labels do not count at all. In a positive study of 400 slices with 40 positive slices, the image labels carry a total weight of $0.0736 \times 40 \approx 2.9$, against a total of 1 for the nine exam labels.

### Consistency rules

Submissions had to satisfy logical relations between the labels of a study. Among them:

- If `negative_exam_for_pe` or `indeterminate` is predicted above 0.5, no image may have a PE probability above 0.5, and none of the labels that describe a positive study (chronicity, location, RV/LV) may exceed 0.5.
- If any image has a PE probability above 0.5, exactly one of `rv_lv_ratio_gte_1` and `rv_lv_ratio_lt_1` must exceed 0.5, at least one of `leftsided_pe`, `rightsided_pe` and `central_pe` must exceed 0.5, and at most one of `chronic_pe` and `acute_and_chronic_pe` may exceed 0.5.

Inconsistent submissions could be disqualified. Models that predict each label separately do not guarantee these relations, and both top solutions included a post-processing step that enforces them.

### Stage 1: lung localisation

The lungs do not fill the CT image: the field of view also contains the chest wall, in some studies the arms, and air outside the body. The author annotated bounding boxes of the lungs on four images from each of a set of studies and trained an EfficientNet-B0 to **regress** the four box coordinates. He reports that cropping to the lungs and enlarging the input from 512 to 640 pixels improved the slice model.

### Stage 2: slice model

**Input.** Three adjacent slices in a PE window, as the three channels of one image. The author found this better than one slice shown in three different windows.

**Labels.** Only the image-level PE label. Adding exam labels at this stage did not improve the results.

**Training.** Binary cross-entropy, a linearly decaying learning rate, and **one epoch** over the training slices, which the author found to be the best number. Augmentation consisted of random contrast (±0.2), shift–scale–rotate (shift and scale ±0.2, rotation ±20°), and cutout with two holes.

| Backbone | Validation loss | Slice ROC AUC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SE-ResNeXt-50 | 0.080 | 0.962 |
| SE-ResNeXt-101 | 0.079 | 0.964 |

Inception-ResNet-v2 and EfficientNets also gave good results; DenseNet and ResNeXt without squeeze-and-excitation gave worse ones. The input size after cropping was 576 pixels, the largest that allowed inference within the 9-hour limit.

**Validation.** A single hold-out set of 1,000 studies, with about 6,200 studies for training. The final models were retrained on all training data.

### Stage 3: study model

The CNN gives a 2048-dimensional embedding for each slice. The number of slices varies from about 100 to over 1,000, so the author **resized the sequence along the slice axis** to a fixed length $M$ by linear interpolation, applying `cv2.resize` to the $n \times 2048$ matrix of embeddings, and resized the image labels in the same way. He searched $M$ in steps of 32. $M = 128$ was best on validation, and the final models used $M = 192$.

Each element of the sequence is concatenated with its differences to the previous and the next element, which gives $3 \times 2048$ features per position. A single **bidirectional GRU** layer reads the sequence. Its per-position outputs predict the image labels. For the exam labels, the outputs are pooled by an **attention-weighted average** and by **max pooling**, and the two pooled vectors are concatenated.

<Deep title="Resizing a sequence of embeddings" kind="math">

Let a study have $n$ slices with embeddings $e_0, \dots, e_{n-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, and let $M$ be the target length. Linear resizing in OpenCV aligns the centres of the first and last cells of the two grids. With the scale factor $s = n / M$, output position $m \in \{0, \dots, M-1\}$ is placed at the source coordinate

$$
t_m = \big(m + \tfrac12\big)\, s - \tfrac12 .
$$

Let $a = \lfloor t_m \rfloor$ be the slice below $t_m$ and $\alpha = t_m - a \in [0, 1)$ the fractional part. The output embedding interpolates between slices $a$ and $a+1$:

$$
\tilde e_m = (1 - \alpha)\, e_a + \alpha\, e_{a+1},
$$

with the indices clamped to $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$ at the ends.

For example, with $n = 400$ slices and $M = 128$, the scale factor is $s = 400/128 = 3.125$. Position $m = 10$ lies at $t_{10} = 10.5 \times 3.125 - 0.5 = 32.3125$, so $a = 32$, $\alpha = 0.3125$ and $\tilde e_{10} = 0.6875\, e_{32} + 0.3125\, e_{33}$. When $n > M$, as here, the output samples the sequence at intervals of $s$ slices; when $n < M$, it interpolates between slices.

All studies then have the same length, which allows batching without padding. Resizing ignores slice spacing: two studies of the same chest with different slice thickness give sequences of equal length, in which each position covers the same fraction of the scanned range. The author also considered zero-padding, which keeps the original slices but lengthens short studies with padding.

Image predictions at the $M$ positions are mapped back to the $n$ original slices by the inverse resize.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Attention pooling and max pooling for exam labels" kind="math">

Let $h_1, \dots, h_M \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$ be the GRU outputs, and let $u \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$ be a learned scoring vector (in general a small network). **Attention pooling** computes a weight for each position and a weighted mean:

$$
\alpha_m = \frac{\exp(u^\top h_m)}{\sum_{m'=1}^{M}\exp(u^\top h_{m'})},
\qquad
z_{\mathrm{att}} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \alpha_m h_m .
$$

**Max pooling** takes the maximum of each channel $c \in \{1, \dots, D\}$ over positions:

$$
z_{\max, c} = \max_{m} h_{m,c} .
$$

The exam head receives the concatenation $[z_{\mathrm{att}};\, z_{\max}] \in \mathbb{R}^{2D}$.

The two pools respond to different patterns. The maximum is determined by the single most activated position, so a strong response on a few slices passes through it. The attention weights can concentrate on a subset of positions chosen by the network, such as the slices through the heart that are relevant to the RV/LV ratio. The concatenation makes both available to the exam head; the write-up does not report an ablation of either pool.
</Deep>

<Deep title="The study model in PyTorch" kind="code">

A Nolad illustration following the description in the write-up; the author's code is in the repository.

```python
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class StudyGRU(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d=2048, hidden=512, n_exam=9):
        super().__init__()
        self.gru   = nn.GRU(3 * d, hidden, batch_first=True, bidirectional=True)
        self.img   = nn.Linear(2 * hidden, 1)          # per-position PE logit
        self.score = nn.Linear(2 * hidden, 1)          # attention scores
        self.exam  = nn.Linear(4 * hidden, n_exam)     # from [attention pool ; max pool]

    def forward(self, e):                              # e: (B, M, d) resized embeddings
        prev = torch.cat([e[:, :1], e[:, :-1]], 1)
        nxt  = torch.cat([e[:, 1:], e[:, -1:]], 1)
        x = torch.cat([e, e - prev, e - nxt], -1)      # (B, M, 3d)
        h, _ = self.gru(x)                             # (B, M, 2*hidden)
        a = F.softmax(self.score(h), dim=1)            # (B, M, 1)
        z = torch.cat([(a * h).sum(1), h.amax(1)], -1)
        return self.img(h).squeeze(-1), self.exam(z)   # (B, M), (B, 9)
```
</Deep>

The author's local validation loss was about 0.18. He reports that the private leaderboard showed no shake-up relative to the public one, and attributes this to the quality of the data.

### Post-processing: consistent labels

A prediction that violates the rules can be made consistent in two ways: as a **negative** configuration (no image above 0.5; negative or indeterminate exam) or as a **positive** one (at least one image above 0.5, one RV/LV label, at least one location label, at most one chronicity label). For an inconsistent study, the author computed the nearest consistent configuration of each kind and its loss, and submitted the one with the lower loss. In this computation, the image weight factor $q_i$, which depends on the unknown true labels, was replaced by the constant 0.005.

<Deep title="Comparing two repairs by expected loss" kind="math">

A Nolad model of the choice, for a simplified case. Consider two predictions of one study, the exam label "negative" with probability $p_n$ and the largest image probability $p_{\max}$, both above 0.5, which violates the rules. Let $\epsilon > 0$ be small. The two repairs are:

- **negative repair:** keep $p_n$ and lower every image probability above 0.5 to $0.5 - \epsilon$;
- **positive repair:** lower $p_n$ to $0.5 - \epsilon$ and keep the images.

Take the current prediction $p$ of a label as the best estimate of the probability that the label is 1. The expected log loss of submitting $p'$ is then $-p\log p' - (1-p)\log(1-p')$. The increase caused by moving from $p$ to $p'$ is

$$
\Delta(p \to p') = \big[-p\log p' - (1-p)\log(1-p')\big] - \big[-p\log p - (1-p)\log(1-p)\big].
$$

Collecting the terms in $p$ and in $1-p$:

$$
\Delta(p \to p') = p\log\frac{p}{p'} + (1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-p'} = \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{Bern}(p)\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(p')\big) \ge 0 .
$$

The repair with the smaller weighted sum of these increases is chosen. Two worked values, with $p' = 0.49$:

$$
\Delta(0.9 \to 0.49) = 0.9\log\tfrac{0.9}{0.49} + 0.1\log\tfrac{0.1}{0.51} \approx 0.547 - 0.163 = 0.384 \ \text{nats},
$$

$$
\Delta(0.55 \to 0.49) = 0.55\log\tfrac{0.55}{0.49} + 0.45\log\tfrac{0.45}{0.51} \approx 0.0635 - 0.0563 = 0.007 \ \text{nats}.
$$

Under this model the repair changes the less confident predictions, weighted by the metric weight of each label.
</Deep>

## Second place: separate models for slices, sequences and the heart

Ian Pan, a radiologist, competed as team high-d-dimer; a D-dimer blood test is used in the work-up of suspected PE. His solution uses separate models for separate questions. He trained on four GPUs with 24 GB of memory each (Quadro RTX 6000), with distributed data parallelism and mixed precision, and notes in the write-up that most competitors did not have this compute.

1. **Slice model.** ResNeSt-50 on three consecutive slices in a PE window (level 100, width 700), resized to 512 and randomly cropped to 448, predicting seven PE-related labels per slice. The exam labels were assigned to the positive slices of a study, which makes them noisy slice targets. GeM pooling gives a 512-dimensional embedding. RandAugment, batch size 128, 5 epochs of 2,500 steps, RAdam with a cosine schedule. The slice AUC was 0.95–0.96.
2. **Sequence models.** Transformer encoders with 4 layers, from the DistilBERT implementation, over the slice embeddings. One was trained on positive studies only to refine the slice predictions, which improved cross-validation by 0.01–0.02. A second predicted the exam labels, with a validation loss of 0.15–0.17.
3. **End-to-end refinement.** A time-distributed CNN trained end to end on the 30% of slices with the highest PE scores, initialised from the models of steps 1 and 2, with batch normalisation frozen.
4. **Heart localisation.** He labelled the first and last slices containing the heart on 1,000 studies, which he reports took about a day, and trained a pruned EfficientNet-B1 to identify heart slices (AUC 0.998).
5. **RV/LV model.** A 3D video network, ir-CSN-101 pretrained on 65 million Instagram videos (Tran et al., 2019), applied to the heart slices only, in a mediastinal window (level 50, width 350), and trained on positive studies. The AUC for RV/LV ≥ 1 was about 0.85.

His solution also includes a heuristic that makes the labels consistent.

The RV/LV ratio depends on the heart, which occupies a minority of the slices of a CTPA. Steps 4 and 5 restrict the RV/LV model to those slices, at a resolution chosen for them.

## Comparison with other competitions

- **The 2019 RSNA intracranial haemorrhage competition** was won with a 2D CNN per slice followed by a sequence model, the design the PE winner credits. The PE winner added a localiser that crops the region of interest, and pooling of the sequence for exam-level labels.
- **Hierarchical labels** also occur in the 2022 cervical spine competition (seven vertebrae and the patient) and the 2023 abdominal trauma competition (healthy, low or high grade per organ, and any injury). Of the three, only the PE competition made label consistency a condition of a valid submission.
- **Organ-specific models**, such as Ian Pan's heart localiser and RV/LV model, correspond to the crop-then-classify designs of the winners of the 2022 and 2023 competitions.

### Considerations for deployment

1. **Secondary labels.** Chronicity and the RV/LV ratio were harder to predict than the presence of an embolus (RV/LV AUC about 0.85 in the second-place solution). They should be validated separately before they are shown to clinicians.
2. **Indeterminate studies.** Poorly opacified studies are labelled indeterminate in the data. Their frequency depends on the local contrast protocol.
3. **Consistency.** The post-processing enforces the logical relations required by the competition. A clinical system needs the same guarantee, so that a study reported as negative carries no positive slices.
