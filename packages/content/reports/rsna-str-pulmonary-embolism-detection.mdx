## The problem in its field

A **pulmonary embolism (PE)** is a blockage of a pulmonary artery, usually by a blood clot that has travelled from the deep veins of the legs. It impairs blood flow through the lungs and can strain the right side of the heart, which must pump against the obstruction. The competition description cites 60,000 to 100,000 deaths a year in the United States.

The diagnostic test is **CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA)**: a CT scan of the chest acquired while iodinated contrast fills the pulmonary arteries. Contrast-filled arteries appear bright; a clot appears as a darker **filling defect** inside a bright vessel. A study has hundreds of slices, and the radiologist must follow the arterial tree from the main pulmonary artery out to small subsegmental branches. CTPA is ordered very often, most studies are negative, and reading them takes time.

<Deep title="What the radiologist reports" kind="domain">

A PE report states more than presence:

- **Location.** *Central* emboli sit in the main or lobar pulmonary arteries; others are segmental or subsegmental. The competition also labels whether emboli are in the **left** or **right** lung (or both).
- **Chronicity.** An **acute** clot is fresh and fills the vessel centrally; a **chronic** clot is organised scar, adherent to the vessel wall, forming webs or eccentric thickening. Some patients have **acute and chronic** emboli. Treatment and prognosis differ.
- **Right-heart strain.** When the obstruction is large, the right ventricle (RV) dilates. The ratio of RV to left ventricle (LV) diameter on axial CT is a standard marker; **RV/LV ≥ 1** indicates strain and higher risk, and may prompt more aggressive treatment such as thrombolysis.
- **Indeterminate.** If contrast timing or motion makes the study unreadable, it is reported as indeterminate.
</Deep>

## The data

The RSNA and the Society of Thoracic Radiology assembled the largest public annotated PE dataset at the time, described in Colak et al. (2021). The training set holds about 7,000 studies and about 1.8 million slices. Studies range from roughly 100 to more than 1,000 slices.

Each **slice** has one label: PE visible on this image. Each **study** has nine exam-level labels:

| Label | Weight |
| --- | --- |
| negative_exam_for_pe | 0.0736 |
| indeterminate | 0.0920 |
| chronic_pe | 0.1043 |
| acute_and_chronic_pe | 0.1043 |
| central_pe | 0.1877 |
| leftsided_pe | 0.0626 |
| rightsided_pe | 0.0626 |
| rv_lv_ratio_gte_1 | 0.2347 |
| rv_lv_ratio_lt_1 | 0.0782 |

## The metric

The loss combines exam-level and image-level terms. For study $i$ with exam labels $y_{ij}$ and predictions $p_{ij}$:
$$
L_{\text{exam}, i} = \sum_{j=1}^{9} w_j\,\ell(y_{ij}, p_{ij}),
$$
and for its $n_i$ slices with image labels $y_{ik}$:
$$
L_{\text{image}, i} = \sum_{k=1}^{n_i} w\, q_i\, \ell(y_{ik}, p_{ik}),
$$
where $w = 0.0736$ and $q_i$ is the fraction of slices in study $i$ that are positive. The total is the sum of both parts over all studies, divided by the sum of all weights used. Because image weights scale with $q_i$, image labels in negative studies ($q_i = 0$) do not count at all, and each positive study's slices count in proportion to how much of the study contains PE.

<Foundation id="weighted-log-loss" />

**Consistency rules.** Submissions had to satisfy logical relations between labels, for example:

- If `negative_exam_for_pe` or `indeterminate` is predicted above 0.5, no image may have PE probability above 0.5, and none of the positive-exam labels (chronicity, location, RV/LV) may exceed 0.5.
- If any image has PE above 0.5, exactly one of `rv_lv_ratio_gte_1` and `rv_lv_ratio_lt_1` must exceed 0.5, at least one of `leftsided_pe`, `rightsided_pe`, `central_pe` must exceed 0.5, and at most one of `chronic_pe`, `acute_and_chronic_pe` may.

Inconsistent submissions could be disqualified. A model that predicts each label independently does not guarantee this, so every top solution needed a post-processing step.

### Step 1: crop the lungs

The lungs do not fill the CT image; the field of view includes the chest wall, the arms in some studies, and air outside the body. He annotated bounding boxes on four images from each of a set of studies and trained an EfficientNet-B0 to **regress** the box coordinates. Cropping to the lungs and then resizing to a larger input (640 instead of 512) improved the slice model, because the pixels saved from the background go to the vessels.

### Step 2: the slice model

**Input.** Three adjacent slices in a PE window, as three channels. He found this better than one slice in three different windows: here the extra depth context helps more than extra contrast settings, because a clot is followed along a vessel across slices.

**Labels.** Only the image-level PE label. Adding exam-level labels at this stage did not help.

**Training.** Plain binary cross-entropy, linear learning-rate decay, and **one epoch**: with about 1.5 million highly correlated slices, a second pass over the data overfit. Augmentation: random contrast, shift-scale-rotate and two cutout holes.

| Backbone | Validation loss | AUC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SE-ResNeXt-50 | 0.080 | 0.962 |
| SE-ResNeXt-101 | 0.079 | 0.964 |

Inception-ResNet-v2 and EfficientNets were also good; DenseNet and plain ResNeXt were worse. The input size after cropping was 576, the largest that fitted the 9-hour inference limit.

<Foundation id="cnn-backbones" />

**Validation.** A single hold-out set of 1,000 studies, with about 6,200 studies for training; the final models were retrained on all data.

### Step 3: the study model

Each slice's 2048-dimensional embedding from the CNN becomes one element of a sequence. Studies have very different numbers of slices, from about 100 to over 1,000, so the sequence was **resized along the slice axis** to a fixed length $M$ with linear interpolation (`cv2.resize` applied to the $n \times 2048$ matrix), and the image labels were resized the same way. He searched $M$ in steps of 32; 128 was best on validation and the final models used 192.

As in the 2019 haemorrhage runner-up's model, each element is concatenated with its differences to the previous and next element, giving $3 \times 2048$ features per position. A single **bidirectional GRU** layer reads the sequence. Its per-position outputs predict the image labels; for the exam labels, the outputs are pooled by concatenating an **attention-weighted average** and a **max pool**.

<Foundation id="recurrent-networks" />

<Deep title="Resizing a sequence of embeddings" kind="math">

Let the study have $n$ slices with embeddings $e_1, \dots, e_n \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$. Linear resizing to $M$ positions places output position $m \in \{0, \dots, M-1\}$ at source coordinate
$$
t_m = \Big(m + \tfrac12\Big)\frac{n}{M} - \tfrac12
$$
(the pixel-centre convention used by OpenCV), and interpolates between the neighbouring slices $a = \lfloor t_m \rfloor$ and $a+1$:
$$
\tilde e_m = (1 - \alpha)\,e_a + \alpha\,e_{a+1}, \qquad \alpha = t_m - a,
$$
with indices clamped at the ends. When $n > M$, each output is a blend of nearby slices, a form of smoothing and down-sampling; when $n < M$, slices are interpolated.

This gives every study the same length, which simplifies batching and removes the dependence of the GRU on the number of slices. The price is that slice spacing is ignored: a thin-slice study and a thick-slice study covering the same anatomy end up the same length, which is roughly the right behaviour, since both span the chest. Zero-padding, the alternative he also tried, keeps the original slices but makes the GRU process long runs of padding for short studies.

Image predictions at the $M$ positions are mapped back to the $n$ original slices by the inverse resize.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Attention pooling and max pooling for exam labels" kind="math">

The GRU outputs $h_1, \dots, h_M \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$. **Attention pooling** learns a scoring vector $u$ (in general a small network):
$$
\alpha_m = \frac{\exp(u^\top h_m)}{\sum_{m'}\exp(u^\top h_{m'})}, \qquad
z_{\text{att}} = \sum_m \alpha_m h_m.
$$
**Max pooling** takes $z_{\max,c} = \max_m h_{m,c}$ for each channel. The exam head sees $[z_{\text{att}}; z_{\max}]$.

The two pools answer different questions. "Is there any PE?" is an existence question, best served by max pooling: a single strongly positive position should decide. "Is RV/LV ≥ 1?" depends on specific slices (those through the heart) and is served by attention, which can learn to weight them. "Central PE" depends on the slices through the main pulmonary arteries. A concatenation lets the head use either.
</Deep>

<Deep title="The study model in PyTorch" kind="code">

A Nolad illustration following the description in the write-up; see the repository for the author's code.

```python
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class StudyGRU(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d=2048, hidden=512, n_exam=9):
        super().__init__()
        self.gru   = nn.GRU(3 * d, hidden, batch_first=True, bidirectional=True)
        self.img   = nn.Linear(2 * hidden, 1)          # per-position PE logit
        self.score = nn.Linear(2 * hidden, 1)          # attention scores
        self.exam  = nn.Linear(4 * hidden, n_exam)     # from [attention pool ; max pool]

    def forward(self, e):                              # e: (B, M, d) resized embeddings
        prev = torch.cat([e[:, :1], e[:, :-1]], 1)
        nxt  = torch.cat([e[:, 1:], e[:, -1:]], 1)
        x = torch.cat([e, e - prev, e - nxt], -1)      # (B, M, 3d)
        h, _ = self.gru(x)                             # (B, M, 2*hidden)
        a = F.softmax(self.score(h), dim=1)            # (B, M, 1)
        z = torch.cat([(a * h).sum(1), h.amax(1)], -1)
        return self.img(h).squeeze(-1), self.exam(z)   # (B, M), (B, 9)
```
</Deep>

His local validation loss was about 0.18. He noted that the leaderboard did not shake up at the end, which he attributed to the high quality of the data.

### Step 4: making the labels consistent

If the predictions violate the rules, there are two families of consistent configurations: a **negative** one (no image above 0.5, negative or indeterminate exam) and a **positive** one (some PE images, one RV/LV label, at least one location, at most one chronicity label). For each, the smallest change to the predictions that satisfies the rules is computed (clipping the offending probabilities to just below or above 0.5), and the expected loss of each repaired version is estimated. The version with the lower loss is submitted. For this estimate, the image weight $q_i$, which depends on the unknown true labels, was replaced by a fixed 0.005.

<Deep title="Choosing between two repairs by expected loss" kind="math">

Consider only a simplified pair: the exam label "negative" with prediction $p_n$ and the maximum image prediction $p_{\max}$, both above 0.5 (inconsistent). The two repairs are:

- **Negative repair:** keep $p_n$, lower every image probability above 0.5 to $0.5 - \epsilon$.
- **Positive repair:** lower $p_n$ to $0.5 - \epsilon$, keep the images.

The expected cost of a change from $p$ to $p'$ on a label whose true probability is believed to be $p$ is the increase in expected log loss:
$$
\Delta(p \to p') = -p\log p' - (1-p)\log(1-p') - \big[-p\log p - (1-p)\log(1-p)\big] = \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{Bern}(p)\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(p')\big) \ge 0.
$$
The repair with the smaller weighted sum of these increases is chosen. Moving $p = 0.9$ to $0.49$ costs $\mathrm{KL} \approx 0.9\log(0.9/0.49) + 0.1\log(0.1/0.51) \approx 0.547 - 0.163 = 0.384$ nats; moving $p = 0.55$ to $0.49$ costs about $0.007$. The rule therefore changes the less confident side, weighted by how much each label counts in the metric.
</Deep>

## Second place: a radiologist's decomposition

Ian Pan, a radiologist, competed as team high-d-dimer (a D-dimer blood test is used to screen for PE). His solution follows the clinical reading process more literally, with separate models for separate questions. He trained on four 24 GB GPUs and noted in the write-up that this compute was a privilege most competitors did not have.

1. **Slice model.** ResNeSt-50 on three consecutive slices in a PE window (level 100, width 700) at 448 after random cropping from 512, predicting seven PE-related labels per slice (exam labels assigned to positive slices, which is noisy). GeM pooling to a 512-dimensional embedding; slice AUC 0.95–0.96.
2. **Sequence models.** Transformer encoders (4 layers, from the DistilBERT implementation) over the slice embeddings: one trained only on positive studies to refine slice labels, which improved cross-validation by 0.01–0.02, and one for exam labels (validation loss 0.15–0.17).
3. **End-to-end refinement.** A time-distributed CNN trained end to end on the 30% of slices with the highest PE scores, initialised from steps 1–2, with batch normalisation frozen.
4. **Heart localisation.** He hand-labelled the top and bottom heart slices on 1,000 studies (about a day's work) and trained a pruned EfficientNet-B1 to find heart slices (AUC 0.998).
5. **RV/LV model.** A 3D video network (ir-CSN-101, pretrained on 65 million Instagram videos) on the heart slices only, in a mediastinal window (level 50, width 350), trained on positive studies. AUC for RV/LV ≥ 1 was about 0.85.

<Foundation id="attention-transformers" />

Step 5 illustrates a point that recurs in medical imaging: a label that depends on one organ (here the heart) is predicted better by a model that sees only that organ at adequate resolution than by a global model that must find it among hundreds of lung slices.

## How this compares with other competitions

- **The 2019 haemorrhage competition** introduced slice CNN + sequence model. The PE winner applied it with two additions: a localiser to crop the region of interest, and pooling designed for exam-level labels.
- **Hierarchical and consistent labels** reappear in the 2023 abdominal trauma competition (healthy/low/high per organ plus "any injury") and the 2022 cervical spine competition (vertebrae plus patient). Only PE enforced consistency as a submission rule.
- **Organ-specific models** such as Ian Pan's heart model anticipate the crop-then-classify designs that won the 2022 and 2023 competitions.
