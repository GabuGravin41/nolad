## Clinical background

Patients in intensive care, for example after cardiac arrest, stroke or brain injury, or with severe infection, can have seizures that produce no visible convulsions. Such seizures are detected with continuous **electroencephalography (EEG)**, in which electrodes on the scalp record the electrical activity of the brain for hours or days. Untreated seizures, and some seizure-like patterns, are associated with brain injury, so the recordings need prompt review.

The review is done by neurologists trained in EEG. The competition description names it as a bottleneck: it is slow and expensive, readers tire, and different readers classify the same segment differently. The patterns of interest are defined in the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society's critical care EEG terminology (ACNS, 2021 version), and experts disagree on many segments. The host group at Massachusetts General Hospital (Brandon Westover's laboratory) had developed an expert-level classifier, SPaRCNet, published in *Neurology* in 2023 (Jing, Ge et al.). The competition used the same data.

<Deep title="The six classes and the ictal–interictal–injury continuum" kind="domain">

- **Seizure (SZ):** rhythmic activity that evolves in frequency, location or shape over time, or a periodic pattern faster than 2.5 Hz.
- **Lateralised periodic discharges (LPD):** sharp waveforms repeating at regular intervals over one hemisphere; common after strokes and focal lesions.
- **Generalised periodic discharges (GPD):** similar discharges over both hemispheres; common in metabolic and anoxic brain injury.
- **Lateralised rhythmic delta activity (LRDA):** rhythmic slow waves (below 4 Hz) over one hemisphere.
- **Generalised rhythmic delta activity (GRDA):** rhythmic slow waves over both hemispheres.
- **Other:** none of the above.

The four periodic and rhythmic patterns lie on the **ictal–interictal–injury continuum (IIIC)**. They are not seizures, but they are associated with brain injury, and some carry a risk of seizure. The definitions separate them by frequency, spatial extent and evolution over time, and a segment can satisfy two definitions approximately.
</Deep>

<Deep title="EEG montages and bipolar channels" kind="domain">

The 19 scalp electrodes of the international 10–20 system are named by region (Fp frontopolar, F frontal, C central, T temporal, P parietal, O occipital) and side (odd numbers left, even numbers right, z midline).

A **bipolar montage** displays the voltage difference between adjacent electrodes. The standard **longitudinal bipolar ("double banana") montage** forms four chains from front to back:

- Left temporal: Fp1–F7, F7–T3, T3–T5, T5–O1
- Right temporal: Fp2–F8, F8–T4, T4–T6, T6–O2
- Left parasagittal: Fp1–F3, F3–C3, C3–P3, P3–O1
- Right parasagittal: Fp2–F4, F4–C4, C4–P4, P4–O2

Two midline channels (Fz–Cz, Cz–Pz) complete the montage, giving 18 signals. Let $V_a(t)$ be the potential recorded at electrode $a$. The channel F7–T3 is

$$
V_{\mathrm{F7}}(t) - V_{\mathrm{T3}}(t).
$$

A component common to both electrodes, such as activity from a distant source or noise on the reference electrode, cancels in the difference, and activity local to one of the two electrodes remains. A discharge with its maximum at T3 lowers $V_{\mathrm{F7}} - V_{\mathrm{T3}}$ and raises $V_{\mathrm{T3}} - V_{\mathrm{T5}}$, so it appears with opposite sign in the two adjacent channels (a **phase reversal**). Neurologists read EEG in this montage, and most of the solutions described here computed the same differences.

The chain structure defines augmentations that preserve anatomy: exchanging the left and right chains mirrors the brain, and exchanging the temporal with the parasagittal chains keeps each chain intact.
</Deep>

## Data

Each training row is a 50-second EEG window (19 channels plus ECG, sampled at 200 Hz) whose central 10 seconds were labelled, together with a 10-minute spectrogram provided by Kaggle. Many rows come from the same recording (`eeg_id`) and overlap in time. There are about 107,000 rows from about 17,000 recordings. At 200 Hz, a 50-second window has $50 \times 200 = 10{,}000$ samples per channel.

Each window was labelled by several reviewers, and the target is the distribution of their votes over the six classes. The host paper describes three kinds of segment: idealised patterns with high agreement, "proto-patterns" in which about half the votes go to Other, and edge cases whose votes are split between two named patterns.

The test labels differ in origin from the training labels. Chris Deotte pointed out, from the host paper, that the training labels came from 119 annotators of varying experience and the test set was labelled by 20 experts, and that class frequencies differ between the two (seizure at 18.8% in one and 1.5% in the other). Training rows with at least 10 votes resemble the expert-labelled test distribution most closely.

## Evaluation metric

<Foundation id="kl-divergence" />

For one test sample, let $q = (q_1, \dots, q_6)$ be the vote distribution, with $q_k \ge 0$ and $\sum_k q_k = 1$, and let $p = (p_1, \dots, p_6)$ be the predicted distribution, with $p_k > 0$ and $\sum_k p_k = 1$. The index $k$ runs over the six classes, and terms with $q_k = 0$ contribute zero. The score for the sample is

$$
\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \sum_{k=1}^{6} q_k \log\frac{q_k}{p_k}.
$$

Writing the logarithm of the quotient as a difference of logarithms,

$$
\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \sum_{k=1}^{6} q_k \log q_k - \sum_{k=1}^{6} q_k \log p_k .
$$

With the entropy $H(q) = -\sum_k q_k \log q_k$ and the cross-entropy $H(q, p) = -\sum_k q_k \log p_k$, this is

$$
\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = H(q, p) - H(q).
$$

The entropy $H(q)$ does not depend on $p$, so minimising KL divergence over $p$ is the same as minimising cross-entropy with soft targets $q$. The minimum, zero, is attained at $p = q$. The competition score is the mean of $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$ over test samples, with natural logarithms.

<Foundation id="softmax-cross-entropy" />

<Deep title="Cost of a confident error, and the effect of averaging models" kind="math">

For a class with $q_k > 0$, the term $q_k \log(q_k / p_k)$ grows without bound as $p_k \to 0$. Suppose two of ten reviewers voted LPD, so $q_{\mathrm{LPD}} = 0.2$, and the model predicts $p_{\mathrm{LPD}} = 0.001$. That class alone contributes

$$
0.2 \log\frac{0.2}{0.001} = 0.2 \log 200 = 0.2 \times 5.298 = 1.060
$$

nats, about 3.7 times the private-leaderboard score of 0.290 reported for the eighth-place single model (a mean over all test samples). A prediction of $p_{\mathrm{LPD}} = 0.1$ would contribute $0.2 \log 2 = 0.139$.

**Convexity.** For fixed $q$, the first sum in $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$ is a constant and the second is $-\sum_k q_k \log p_k$. Each $-\log p_k$ is a convex function of $p_k$, and a sum of convex functions with non-negative weights $q_k$ is convex, so $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$ is convex in $p$. For $M$ models with predictions $p^{(1)}, \dots, p^{(M)}$ and their mean $\bar p = \tfrac{1}{M}\sum_m p^{(m)}$, Jensen's inequality then gives

$$
\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,\bar p) \le \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\|\,p^{(m)}\big).
$$

The equal-weight ensemble scores no worse than the average of its members. It also bounds the damage from a single member. All terms in the mean are non-negative, so for every $m$

$$
\bar p_k \ge \tfrac{1}{M}\, p^{(m)}_k ,
$$

and $\bar p_k$ is small only if every member's $p_k$ is small. In the example above, with $M = 5$ models of which four predict $p_{\mathrm{LPD}} = 0.2$ and one predicts $0.001$,

$$
\bar p_{\mathrm{LPD}} = \frac{4 \times 0.2 + 0.001}{5} = \frac{0.801}{5} = 0.1602,
$$

and the contribution of LPD falls from 1.060 for the fifth model alone to

$$
0.2 \log\frac{0.2}{0.1602} = 0.2 \log 1.2484 = 0.2 \times 0.2219 = 0.044 .
$$

Muku's part of the winning write-up states that smaller backbones worked better, and that diversity between models mattered because KL divergence is sensitive to extreme errors.
</Deep>

## Validation

Chris Deotte published a validation method, which the winning team adopted:

1. For each `eeg_id`, the votes over all its rows are summed and normalised, giving one target distribution per recording.
2. The middle 50 seconds (10,000 samples) of the recording are used.
3. The recordings are split with group K-fold by patient (5 folds).
4. Only recordings with at least 10 votes are evaluated, the subset that best matches the expert-labelled test set.

### Muku: superlet scalograms

**Montage and filtering.** Muku used a 16-channel anterior–posterior montage and, as augmentation, a Butterworth low-pass filter with a random cut-off between 30 and 40 Hz. The write-up states that high-frequency noise (for example muscle artefact) tends to raise the number of votes for Other, so the filtering affects how the input relates to the label.

**Superlets.** A superlet combines, at each frequency, wavelets with increasing numbers of cycles by a geometric mean, which improves time and frequency resolution together (see the box below). Muku computed superlets between 0.5 and 20 Hz (32 frequencies, base cycle 1, order 1 to 16) with the MIT-licensed `irhum/superlets` code, and reports that they outperformed the short-time Fourier transform (STFT).

**Image construction.** Each channel's scalogram, of size $32 \times 10{,}000$ (frequencies × samples), was stored downsampled to $32 \times 1{,}000$. One stored column then covers $50\ \text{s} / 1{,}000 = 50$ ms. At training time a centre window of 256, 512 or 768 columns (12.8, 25.6 or 38.4 s) was cut out, shifted randomly by up to ±5 s (±100 columns) as augmentation, and resized to $16 \times 256$ per channel. The 16 channels were stacked vertically into a $256 \times 256$ image ($16 \times 16 = 256$ rows).

<Deep title="From the Fourier transform to wavelets and superlets" kind="math">

**Short-time Fourier transform.** Let $x(t)$ be the signal and $g(t)$ a window function of fixed width. The STFT at time $\tau$ and frequency $f$ is

$$
X(\tau, f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\, g(t - \tau)\, e^{-2\pi i f t}\, dt .
$$

Let $\sigma_t$ be the standard deviation of $|g(t)|^2$ over time and $\sigma_f$ that of its Fourier transform over frequency, both normalised as distributions. The uncertainty principle (the Gabor limit) states

$$
\sigma_t\, \sigma_f \ge \frac{1}{4\pi}.
$$

One window has one pair $(\sigma_t, \sigma_f)$ at all frequencies, so it cannot resolve both a brief spike, which needs small $\sigma_t$, and a slow 1 Hz rhythm, which needs small $\sigma_f$ relative to 1 Hz.

**Continuous wavelet transform (CWT).** A Morlet wavelet at frequency $f > 0$ with $c > 0$ cycles is a complex exponential under a Gaussian envelope:

$$
\psi_{f,c}(t) = A \exp\!\Big(-\frac{t^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\Big)\, e^{2\pi i f t}, \qquad \sigma_t = \frac{c}{2\pi f},
$$

where $A$ is a normalising constant and $\sigma_t$ is the standard deviation of the Gaussian envelope; this is a common convention for the width. The Fourier transform of $\psi_{f,c}$ is a Gaussian centred at $f$ whose envelope has standard deviation

$$
\sigma_f = \frac{1}{2\pi \sigma_t} = \frac{f}{c}.
$$

(Measured on $|\psi_{f,c}|^2$ and on the squared spectrum instead, both widths are smaller by a factor $\sqrt 2$, and their product equals the bound $1/(4\pi)$; Gaussian windows attain the Gabor limit.)

The transform is $W_c(\tau, f) = \int x(t)\,\overline{\psi_{f,c}(t - \tau)}\,dt$, where the bar denotes complex conjugation. The envelope is long at low frequencies and short at high frequencies. For example, with $c = 7$ cycles, $\sigma_t = 7/(2\pi) = 1.11$ s at 1 Hz and $\sigma_t = 7/(40\pi) = 0.056$ s at 20 Hz. The frequency spread is $\sigma_f = 0.14$ Hz at 1 Hz and $2.9$ Hz at 20 Hz. For a fixed $c$, the product $\sigma_t \sigma_f = 1/(2\pi)$ is the same at every frequency, so a small $c$ gives good time resolution and poor frequency resolution, and a large $c$ the reverse.

**Superlets** (Moca et al., 2021). A superlet of order $o$ at frequency $f$ is a set of $o$ wavelets with cycle counts $c_1 < c_2 < \dots < c_o$; in the multiplicative form, $c_j = j\,c_1$. With base cycle $c_1 = 1$ and order 16, the cycle counts are $1, 2, \dots, 16$. The superlet response is the geometric mean of the magnitudes:

$$
S(\tau, f) = \Big(\prod_{j=1}^{o} |W_{c_j}(\tau, f)|\Big)^{1/o}.
$$

A geometric mean is large only where every factor is large. The short wavelets localise energy in time and the long ones in frequency, so the product retains energy that is concentrated in both. In adaptive superlets the order increases with frequency; Muku's setting used orders from 1 to 16 across the 0.5–20 Hz range.
</Deep>

**Backbones.** The best backbone was Swin-V2-tiny with window 16, with a cross-validation KL divergence of 0.2229. Muku also trained Swin-V2-tiny (window 8), CAFormer-S18, GC-ViT-xtiny and xxtiny, ConvNeXt-V2-atto, MaxViT-pico, InceptionNeXt-tiny, PoolFormerV2-S12 and NextViT-small.

<Foundation id="attention-transformers" />

**Learned temporal convolution.** Another of Muku's variants replaced the fixed transform with a learned one. 1D convolutions with kernel length 200 (one second at 200 Hz) are applied to each channel, and the resulting feature maps are stacked into an image for a timm backbone. The write-up cites EEGNet and the first-place solution to the G2Net gravitational-wave competition as inspiration. A GRU variant was also trained.

**Two-stage training and label smoothing.**

- Stage 1: all samples with more than one vote, learning rate $10^{-3}$.
- Stage 2: only samples with at least 10 votes, learning rate $10^{-4}$.

Samples with a single vote were excluded. Label smoothing added 0.02 to every class's vote count before the counts were normalised.

<Deep title="Additive smoothing of vote counts" kind="math">

Let $n_k \ge 0$ be the number of votes for class $k$, $K = 6$ the number of classes and $N = \sum_k n_k$ the total number of votes. With a pseudo-count $\alpha > 0$ added to each class, the smoothed target is

$$
\tilde q_k = \frac{n_k + \alpha}{N + K\alpha}, \qquad K = 6, \quad \alpha = 0.02 .
$$

The denominator is the sum of the numerators over $k$, so $\sum_k \tilde q_k = 1$. This is the posterior mean of the class probabilities under a symmetric Dirichlet prior with parameter $\alpha$ (Laplace smoothing is the case $\alpha = 1$). Here $K\alpha = 0.12$, and the effect depends on $N$:

- With $N = 3$ votes, all for seizure: $\tilde q_{\mathrm{SZ}} = 3.02 / 3.12 = 0.968$, and each other class receives $0.02 / 3.12 = 0.0064$.
- With $N = 15$ votes, all for seizure: $\tilde q_{\mathrm{SZ}} = 15.02 / 15.12 = 0.993$, and each other class receives $0.02 / 15.12 = 0.0013$.

Samples with few votes are therefore smoothed more than samples with many. Uniform label smoothing, $\tilde q = (1 - \varepsilon)\,q + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{K}\mathbf{1}$ with a fixed $\varepsilon$, would move both examples by the same amount. Every $\tilde q_k$ is positive, so the model is not trained towards predicting an exact zero, which the metric penalises without bound when the true $q_k$ is positive.
</Deep>

Muku trained with a KL-divergence loss plus an auxiliary loss, the Adan optimiser and a cosine learning-rate schedule. Augmentations were a time shift of ±5 s, a random band-pass filter on the waveforms, and XY masking (masking random bands of rows and columns) on the scalograms.

<Foundation id="data-augmentation" />

### suguuuuu: CWT scalograms at 512 × 512

suguuuuu computed continuous wavelet transforms of the 18 bipolar channels with code adapted from G2Net, using wavelet width 7, 40 scales between 0.5 and 40 Hz, and stride 16. With 10,000 samples per channel and stride 16, each scalogram has $10{,}000 / 16 = 625$ time columns, so the transform gives an array of $18 \times 40 \times 625$, which was stacked into a $512 \times 512$ image. The inputs were clipped to ±1024 and divided by 32.

He reports that 0.5–40 Hz worked better than 0.5–20 Hz, and the middle 50 seconds better than 25 or 10 seconds. MaxViT-base was the best backbone, with a cross-validation KL divergence of 0.2399, or 0.2381 with yamash's 50-second preprocessing. Training had two stages of 5 and 15 epochs, with XY masking, Mixup, the Adan optimiser and a cosine schedule. The STFT, the Kaggle-provided spectrograms, the constant-Q transform and images of plotted waveforms did not work for him.

### yamash: raw signals as an image

yamash cropped the 18 bipolar signals to 2,000, 5,000 and 10,000 samples around the centre (10, 25 and 50 s), resized the crops and concatenated them into one 2D image, with no time–frequency transform. The first convolutional layers of the 2D CNN then act as learned filters along the time axis. The early models reached a cross-validation score of about 0.24. The final set was four ConvNeXt-atto models with different seeds and band-pass settings (cross-validation 0.2452, 0.2385, 0.2457 and 0.2351) and one InceptionNeXt-tiny (0.2309).

yamash also replaced the softmax at the output with α-entmax with $\alpha \approx 1.03$, which improved both the public and the private score by about 0.004.

<Foundation id="cnn-backbones" />

<Deep title="α-entmax" kind="math">

Let $z \in \mathbb{R}^K$ be the vector of logits and $\Delta = \{p \in \mathbb{R}^K : p_k \ge 0,\ \sum_k p_k = 1\}$ the probability simplex. Softmax is the solution of an entropy-regularised maximisation:

$$
\operatorname{softmax}(z) = \arg\max_{p \in \Delta} \; \Big( p^\top z + H(p) \Big), \qquad H(p) = -\sum_k p_k \log p_k .
$$

**α-entmax** (Peters et al., 2019) replaces the Shannon entropy $H$ with the Tsallis entropy, for $\alpha > 1$:

$$
H_\alpha(p) = \frac{1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)} \sum_k \big(p_k - p_k^{\alpha}\big),
$$

which tends to $H(p)$ as $\alpha \to 1$. The maximiser has the form

$$
\operatorname{entmax}_\alpha(z)_k = \big[(\alpha - 1)\,z_k - \tau\big]_+^{1/(\alpha-1)},
$$

where $[u]_+ = \max(u, 0)$ and the threshold $\tau \in \mathbb{R}$ is chosen so that the outputs sum to one. At $\alpha = 2$ this is sparsemax, the Euclidean projection of $z$ onto $\Delta$, which sets many probabilities exactly to zero.

**Limit $\alpha \to 1$.** Write the threshold as $\tau = (\alpha - 1)\,c - 1$ for a constant $c$. Substituting,

$$
(\alpha - 1)\,z_k - \tau = 1 + (\alpha - 1)(z_k - c),
$$

and with $h = \alpha - 1$,

$$
\operatorname{entmax}_\alpha(z)_k = \big[1 + h\,(z_k - c)\big]_+^{1/h} \;\longrightarrow\; e^{\,z_k - c} \quad (h \to 0),
$$

by the limit $(1 + h u)^{1/h} \to e^{u}$. Normalisation fixes $c = \log \sum_j e^{z_j}$, which is softmax.

**Which classes are set to zero at $\alpha = 1.03$.** Let $z_{\max}$ be the largest logit and $p_{\max}$ its probability. From the closed form, $p_{\max}^{\alpha - 1} = (\alpha - 1)\,z_{\max} - \tau$, so

$$
\tau = (\alpha - 1)\,z_{\max} - p_{\max}^{\alpha - 1}.
$$

Class $k$ has $p_k > 0$ exactly when $(\alpha - 1)\,z_k > \tau$. Substituting $\tau$,

$$
p_k > 0 \iff (\alpha - 1)(z_{\max} - z_k) < p_{\max}^{\alpha - 1}.
$$

With six classes, $1/6 \le p_{\max} \le 1$, so $p_{\max}^{0.03}$ lies between $(1/6)^{0.03} = 0.948$ and 1. At $\alpha = 1.03$, a class is set to zero only if its logit is at least $0.948 / 0.03 = 31.6$ below the largest, and every class within 31.6 of the largest keeps a positive probability. Softmax at a logit gap of 33 gives a ratio $e^{-33} \approx 4.7 \times 10^{-15}$, so in the range where entmax at this $\alpha$ produces zeros, softmax already produces values that are negligible. For logits within the usual range, the two differ only in the shape of the distribution, and the write-up reports the effect on the score (about 0.004).
</Deep>

### Ensembling

The write-up describes combining the models per class by **non-negative linear regression** on out-of-fold predictions. Let $i$ index training samples, $m = 1, \dots, M$ models, $p^{(m)}_{ik}$ the out-of-fold prediction of model $m$ for class $k$ on sample $i$, and $q_{ik}$ the target. For each class $k$, the weights $\beta_{1k}, \dots, \beta_{Mk}$ solve

$$
\min_{\beta_{1k}, \dots, \beta_{Mk} \ge 0} \; \sum_i \Big( \sum_{m=1}^{M} \beta_{mk}\, p^{(m)}_{ik} - q_{ik} \Big)^2 .
$$

The constraint $\beta_{mk} \ge 0$ sets the weights of unhelpful models to zero rather than allowing them to enter with negative sign. The fitted combinations for the six classes do not in general sum to one, and the sources used for this report do not state how the combined vector was rescaled to a distribution.

## Eighth place: one combined model

Chris Deotte finished eighth with a single model. He described the target as the annotators' opinion and noted that annotators look at spectrograms and waveform plots, so his model received the same kinds of input.

- **Preprocessing** with MNE: double-banana bipolar differences, a 60 Hz notch filter, a 0.5–40 Hz band-pass filter, clipping at ±500 µV, and missing values set to 0.
- **Three backbones.** (1) Mel spectrograms computed on the GPU with torchaudio in the data loader, of the full 50 s (FFT size 2048, window 1280, hop 19, 0–20 Hz, 64 mel bands) and of the middle 10 s (FFT size 1280, window 32, hop 4), fed to TinyViT-21M at 512 pixels. With hops of 19 and 4 samples, the two spectrograms have about $10{,}000 / 19 \approx 526$ and $2{,}000 / 4 = 500$ time frames. (2) Matplotlib plots of the montage waveforms, as a neurologist would see them, fed to TinyViT-21M at 224 pixels. (3) The raw 1D signals fed to a 1D EEGNet, contributed by Nischay Dhankhar.
- **Pseudo-labels** for samples with fewer than 10 votes. With original vote distribution $q$ and the predictions $p^{(1)}, p^{(2)}, p^{(3)}$ of the three models, the new target is $\tilde q = 0.1\,q + 0.3\,(p^{(1)} + p^{(2)} + p^{(3)})$. The weights sum to $0.1 + 3 \times 0.3 = 1$, so $\tilde q$ is a distribution.
- **Combined model.** The heads of the three backbones were removed, their embeddings concatenated and a new head attached. It was trained on the samples with at least 10 votes (included twice) and on the pseudo-labelled samples (once). All 107,000 rows were used, with one row sampled per `eeg_id` in each epoch.
- **Augmentations** on the raw signals: left–right brain flip, temporal–parasagittal chain exchange, crop and rescale, inversion, and frequency dropout.

The single model scored 0.246 in cross-validation, 0.243 on the public leaderboard and 0.290 on the private leaderboard, which was within the gold-medal range.

## Comparison with other competitions

- **BirdCLEF 2024** and **CMI sleep states** are the other signal competitions in the collection. The BirdCLEF winners used 2D CNNs on mel spectrograms of audio, and the CMI winners used 1D convolutional and recurrent models on accelerometer data. In HMS, models on spectrograms, wavelet scalograms and raw-signal images all reached competitive scores, and the winning team combined all three.
- **Soft targets from annotator votes.** Most competitions give one hard label per sample. In HMS the target is the distribution of votes, and the solutions described here selected training samples by the number of votes so that the training targets resembled the expert-labelled test set.
- **Different annotators for training and test labels.** PANDA (prostate cancer grading) also had training labels from less experienced annotators (trained students at one centre) and test labels from expert panels. In HMS the winners finished training on the high-vote samples that resemble the test labels; in PANDA, top teams removed training labels that disagreed strongly with out-of-fold predictions.
