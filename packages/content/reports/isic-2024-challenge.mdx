## Clinical background

Melanoma is the most dangerous common skin cancer. When found early it can usually be removed by excision; when found late it can spread and is often fatal. Basal cell and squamous cell carcinomas are more common and less often lethal, but also require treatment.

Dermatologists examine suspicious lesions with a dermatoscope, a magnifier with polarised light that shows pigment structures beneath the surface of the skin. Earlier ISIC challenges used dermoscopic images. The 2024 challenge concerned triage before a dermatologist is involved: the competition description states the aim of identifying malignant lesions in primary care and non-clinical settings, including for underserved populations, where dermoscopy is not available. For that purpose a model has to work from images of lower quality than dermoscopic images.

<Deep title="The ugly duckling sign" kind="domain">

Most adults have many naevi (moles), and a person's benign naevi tend to resemble each other in colour, size and pattern. A lesion that looks different from the same patient's other naevi is suspicious. Dermatologists call this the ugly duckling sign and use it together with the ABCDE criteria (asymmetry, border irregularity, colour variation, diameter above about 6 mm, evolution over time).

The sign is relative to the patient. A lesion that would be unremarkable on one patient can be suspicious on another whose naevi are all small and uniform. It therefore cannot be evaluated from one image alone; each lesion has to be compared with the same patient's other lesions. Both of the top two solutions in this competition built features that make this comparison.
</Deep>

## Data

The images come from 3D total-body photography (3D-TBP). The Vectra WB360 system photographs a patient's entire skin surface with many cameras at once and builds a 3D model of it. Each detected lesion is cropped as a tile covering 15 × 15 mm of skin. The competition description compares the quality of these crops with that of phone photographs, well below that of dermoscopy. The data include every lesion from patients on three continents.

Each lesion comes with metadata: the patient's age and sex, the anatomical site, and many measurements computed by the TBP software (column names beginning `tbp_lv_`). These include the lesion's area and perimeter, colour values in the L\*a\*b\* colour space inside and outside the lesion, the contrast with the surrounding skin, border irregularity and eccentricity.

The training set contains about 400,000 lesion images, of which about 400 are malignant, roughly one in a thousand.

## Evaluation metric

The organisers required high sensitivity for a triage tool and scored only the part of the ROC curve in which the true-positive rate (TPR) is at least 0.8. The score is the area between the ROC curve and the horizontal line $\mathrm{TPR} = 0.8$. A perfect ranking has $\mathrm{TPR} = 1$ at every false-positive rate, so the maximum score is $1 \times (1 - 0.8) = 0.2$.

<Foundation id="roc-auc" />

<Deep title="Partial AUC above a true-positive rate of 0.8" kind="math">

Let $f \in [0,1]$ be the false-positive rate and $\mathrm{TPR}(f) \in [0,1]$ the ROC curve. The score is

$$
\mathrm{pAUC} = \int_0^1 \max\big(\mathrm{TPR}(f) - 0.8,\ 0\big)\, df .
$$

**An upper bound.** Let $f_{0.8}$ be the smallest false-positive rate at which $\mathrm{TPR}(f) \ge 0.8$. The integrand is zero for $f < f_{0.8}$ and at most $0.2$ elsewhere, so

$$
\mathrm{pAUC} \le 0.2\,(1 - f_{0.8}) .
$$

A model that has to flag 5% of benign lesions to reach 80% sensitivity can score at most $0.2 \times 0.95 = 0.19$.

**Which pairs are counted.** Let there be $n_+$ malignant and $n_-$ benign lesions with model scores, and assume no ties and that $0.2\,n_+$ is an integer. Let $B$ be the set of the $0.2\,n_+$ malignant lesions with the lowest scores. For a threshold $t$, the TPR is the fraction of malignant lesions with a score above $t$.

1. If $\mathrm{TPR} \ge 0.8$ at threshold $t$, every malignant lesion outside $B$ has a score above $t$, so $\mathrm{TPR} - 0.8 = |\{ i \in B : s_i > t \}| / n_+$.
2. If $\mathrm{TPR} < 0.8$, no lesion of $B$ has a score above $t$, and both sides of the same equation are zero after taking the positive part.
3. Therefore $\max(\mathrm{TPR} - 0.8, 0) = |\{ i \in B : s_i > t \}| / n_+$ for every threshold.
4. Integrating over $f$, the fraction of benign lesions above the threshold, each lesion $i \in B$ contributes the fraction of benign lesions that score below it, divided by $n_+$.

The result is

$$
\mathrm{pAUC} = \frac{1}{n_+\, n_-} \sum_{i \in B} \big|\{\, j \text{ benign} : s_j < s_i \,\}\big| .
$$

The score therefore depends only on how the hardest fifth of the malignant lesions is ranked against the benign lesions. The order among benign lesions, and the order of the other malignant lesions, do not enter. As with the full AUC, only the ranking of the scores matters, so predictions need not be calibrated and models can be combined by averaging ranks.
</Deep>

### Validation with paired statistical tests

The winner adopted a validation procedure described by daniel89 in the earlier Mercedes-Benz competition:

1. Five-fold `StratifiedGroupKFold`, grouped by patient so that no patient appears in both the training and the validation folds.
2. The whole cross-validation is repeated with 10 random seeds, for both the current and the proposed configuration.
3. The two sets of 10 scores are compared with a paired t-test (`scipy.stats.ttest_rel`), pairing runs that share a seed and therefore a data split.
4. A change is accepted if its p-value is small. Only changes that passed the test were then evaluated on the public leaderboard.

Late in the competition the author relaxed the rule, accepting p-values up to 0.2 and relying more on the public leaderboard. He reports that this improved his public score and slightly lowered his private score.

<Deep title="The paired t-test for comparing two configurations" kind="math">

Let $a_k$ and $b_k$ be the cross-validation scores of the current and proposed configurations under seed $k = 1, \dots, K$. The seed determines the fold split, and some splits give higher scores than others for both configurations. Model this with a common split effect $s_k$:

$$
a_k = s_k + \mu_a + \varepsilon^a_k, \qquad b_k = s_k + \mu_b + \varepsilon^b_k ,
$$

where $\mu_a, \mu_b$ are the mean scores of the two configurations and $\varepsilon^a_k, \varepsilon^b_k$ are the remaining random variation. The difference for seed $k$ is

$$
d_k = b_k - a_k = (\mu_b - \mu_a) + (\varepsilon^b_k - \varepsilon^a_k) ,
$$

in which $s_k$ has cancelled. With the mean difference $\bar d$ and the sample standard deviation of the differences $s_d$, the paired t-statistic is

$$
t = \frac{\bar d}{s_d / \sqrt{K}} ,
$$

which has $K - 1$ degrees of freedom under the hypothesis $\mu_a = \mu_b$.

Illustrative numbers (not from the write-up): let $K = 10$, $\bar d = 0.0004$ and $s_d = 0.0003$, while the scores of each configuration vary across seeds with a standard deviation of $0.003$ because of the split effect. The paired statistic is

$$
t = \frac{0.0004}{0.0003 / \sqrt{10}} = \frac{0.0004}{0.0000949} \approx 4.22 ,
$$

with a two-sided p-value of about 0.002 at 9 degrees of freedom. A two-sample test that ignores the pairing uses the standard error $\sqrt{2 \times 0.003^2 / 10} \approx 0.00134$ and gives $t \approx 0.30$, with a p-value of about 0.77. The pairing removes the split effect from the comparison.
</Deep>

### Gradient-boosted trees

The final model is an ensemble of CatBoost, LightGBM and XGBoost. Each library was trained with five group folds and 10 seeds, giving $3 \times 5 \times 10 = 150$ models. Default parameters and the under- and oversampling scheme came from daniel89's public notebook. The CatBoost parameters were found with Optuna on the tabular data: learning rate 0.026, L2 regularisation 18, random strength 4.7, depth 6, bagging temperature 0.874, border count 256, loss-guided tree growth, at least 38 samples per leaf, and up to 1000 iterations with early stopping after 100 rounds without improvement.

The 150 predictions are converted to percentile ranks and averaged with equal weights. All tree models together trained in under 20 minutes on a GPU. The author reports that 150 models gave little gain over 45.

### Patient-relative features

In addition to features from public notebooks, the winner added:

- the total lesion area per patient, and per patient and anatomical site;
- the local outlier factor (LOF) of each lesion among the same patient's lesions, computed on the most important features according to CatBoost. The author describes it as a measure of how abnormal a lesion is for this patient.

<Deep title="The local outlier factor" kind="math">

The local outlier factor (Breunig et al., 2000) compares the density of points around a point with the density around its neighbours. Let $d(a, b)$ be the distance between points $a$ and $b$ in feature space, $N_k(a)$ the set of the $k$ nearest neighbours of $a$, and $d_k(b)$ the distance from $b$ to its own $k$-th nearest neighbour.

1. The reachability distance from $a$ to a neighbour $b$ is
$$
\mathrm{reach}_k(a, b) = \max\big(d_k(b),\ d(a,b)\big) .
$$
Points closer to $b$ than $b$'s $k$-th neighbour are all assigned the distance $d_k(b)$, which reduces fluctuations inside dense clusters.
2. The mean reachability distance of $a$ is
$$
R(a) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{b \in N_k(a)} \mathrm{reach}_k(a, b) ,
$$
and the local reachability density is its reciprocal, $\mathrm{lrd}(a) = 1 / R(a)$.
3. The local outlier factor is the mean ratio of the neighbours' densities to the density of $a$:
$$
\mathrm{LOF}(a) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{b \in N_k(a)} \frac{\mathrm{lrd}(b)}{\mathrm{lrd}(a)} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{b \in N_k(a)} \frac{R(a)}{R(b)} .
$$

The second form follows from $\mathrm{lrd} = 1/R$. A point whose neighbourhood is as dense as its neighbours' has $\mathrm{LOF} \approx 1$. If the mean reachability distance of a lesion is 3 and that of each of its neighbours is 1, its LOF is $3/1 = 3$. Computed separately within each patient's lesions, a high LOF marks a lesion that differs from that patient's other lesions, a quantitative counterpart of the ugly duckling sign.
</Deep>

Adding LOF raised the winner's cross-validation score from 0.18149 to 0.18185. A related feature, a z-score of each lesion within clusters of lesions, did not improve the private score.

### Image models as features

Two image backbones from the `timm` library were trained on the lesion crops: EVA02-Small (`eva02_small_patch14_336.mim_in22k_ft_in1k`), a vision transformer pretrained with masked image modelling on ImageNet-22k and fine-tuned on ImageNet-1k, and EdgeNeXt-Base (`edgenext_base.in21k_ft_in1k`), a network that combines convolution and attention.

- Batches were balanced 1:1 between malignant and benign lesions.
- Each architecture was trained with several seeds under the same five-fold group split, with early stopping on validation: at most 200 epochs, patience of 10 validation checks, and validation run more frequently as training progressed.

The out-of-fold predictions of the image models became features for the trees, in three forms:

1. **Standardisation.** The predictions of each image model were standardised separately. The resulting feature ranged from about −0.44 at the 1st percentile to 4.99 at the 99th.
2. **Noise.** Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.1 was added to the image features during tree training. The author states that this counters the optimism of out-of-fold predictions. The standard deviation was chosen on the public leaderboard among values between 0.02 and 0.12.
3. **Ratio to the patient mean.** The ratio of a lesion's image prediction to the mean prediction over all of the patient's lesions, also with noise added.

<Deep title="Optimism of out-of-fold predictions after early stopping" kind="math">

The image model for fold $m$ is trained on the other folds and stopped at the epoch $e^\star_m$ with the best validation score on fold $m$. Its predictions on fold $m$ then become features. Let $\hat S_e$ be the score measured on fold $m$ at epoch $e$, a noisy estimate of the model's expected score $S_e$ on new data. Since $\max_{e'} \hat S_{e'} \ge \hat S_e$ for every $e$, taking expectations gives

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\max_{e'} \hat S_{e'}\big] \ \ge\ \max_e\, \mathbb{E}\big[\hat S_e\big] = \max_e S_e .
$$

The score measured at the selected epoch is therefore, on average, at least as high as the best expected score, and it exceeds it whenever the noise in $\hat S_e$ affects which epoch is chosen. The trees are trained on out-of-fold features that are slightly more accurate on their fold than the same models will be on test data. Adding noise to these features during tree training makes them less reliable as inputs, which reduces how strongly the trees depend on them.
</Deep>

### Features from earlier ISIC data

The winner trained an EVA02-Small classifier on images from the ISIC archive of earlier years, downloaded with `isic-cli`, to separate three classes: benign keratosis-like lesions, melanoma and naevus, after mapping the archive's diagnoses into these groups. Its predictions on the 2024 lesions became tabular features.

### Synthetic images

The author generated synthetic images of malignant lesions with a Stable Diffusion model, following the Derm-T2IM approach (Farooq et al., 2024). Image models trained with the synthetic images improved: an ensemble of image models scored 0.142 on the private leaderboard with them, against 0.140 without. Adding these models to the final ensemble did not improve it, and they were excluded.

### Changes that did not help

The write-up lists sampling hard negatives more often (selected by out-of-fold log loss), pretraining the image models on data from previous ISIC competitions, and test-time augmentation. In the last part of the competition the author's scores stayed at about 0.185–0.186 on the public leaderboard, corresponding to about 0.173 private.

## Second place: patient-wise standardisation and diverse image models

Team yakiniku also used image-model outputs as features for LightGBM, XGBoost and CatBoost.

- **Patient-wise standardisation of features.** Features were standardised within each patient, within each patient and TBP location (`tbp_lv_location`), within each patient and simplified location, and within each patient and general anatomical site. The team also used a "tabular ugly ducklings" technique proposed in the competition forum.
- **Tree ensemble.** 18 variants of each boosting library, 54 models in total, differing in their features and in how many image-model predictions (0 to 3) they use as inputs. Each variant was averaged over 5 seeds trained on the full data, with 200–300 boosting rounds.
- **Image models.** Nine image models under five training set-ups, with private scores given for each model:

| Set-up | Models (score) |
| --- | --- |
| Standard training | EVA02-Small (0.1537), DeiT3-Small (0.1534) |
| Mixup | BEiTv2-Base (0.1594) |
| Auxiliary loss predicting the tabular features | ConvNeXtV2-Tiny (0.1548) |
| Auxiliary loss predicting distances to k-means clusters of TF-IDF vectors of the free-text diagnosis field `iddx_full` | SwinV2-Small (0.1612), EVA02-Small (0.1580), ResNeXt-50 (0.1515) |
| Self-supervised pretraining with the tabular data | ConvNeXtV2-Nano (0.1607), Swin-Tiny (0.1596) |

- **Image-model training.** Undersampling of benign lesions at 1:3 or 1:5, a fixed 50–200 epochs without early stopping, augmentation from the ISIC 2020 winning solution, AdamW with learning rates of $10^{-5}$ to $8 \times 10^{-6}$ for the backbone and $10^{-3}$ for the head, warm-up followed by a cosine schedule, and automatic mixed precision.
- **Validation.** A triple-stratified, leak-free k-fold split, following an idea by Chris Deotte from the ISIC 2020 competition.

## Comparison with other competitions

The 2020 SIIM-ISIC melanoma competition provided dermoscopic images with age, sex and anatomical site, and was won by an ensemble of convolutional networks. In 2024 the images were of lower quality and each lesion came with many tabular measurements from the TBP system. The top two solutions of 2024 used gradient-boosted trees as the final model, with image models contributing features, and both represented the comparison of a lesion with the patient's other lesions as tabular features. Both years' winners averaged percentile ranks of model predictions.

Performance has to be measured again for this setting. |

### Considerations for deployment

1. **Threshold.** The metric depends only on ranking. For triage, a threshold has to be chosen on local data to reach a target sensitivity, for example referral of 90% of melanomas. The number of benign lesions referred at that threshold determines the workload, and the leaderboard does not report it.
2. **Device dependence.** Most tabular features are computed by the Vectra WB360 system. Without it, the model has only the image and demographic data.
3. **Small number of positives.** With a few hundred malignant lesions in training, estimates of sensitivity at a fixed threshold have wide confidence intervals and should be confirmed on local data.
