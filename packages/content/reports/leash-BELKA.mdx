## Scientific background

Most drugs are small molecules that bind to a protein and change its activity. Early drug discovery searches for molecules that bind a chosen target protein. The competition description gives the scale of the search: the space of drug-like molecules is estimated at about $10^{60}$, the US FDA has approved about 2,000 novel molecular entities in its history, and BindingDB, a public database of measured protein–molecule binding, holds about 2.8 million measurements. The competition asked whether a model trained on very large screening data can predict binding for molecules that were never tested.

**DNA-encoded chemical libraries (DELs)** allow hundreds of millions of molecules to be screened in one experiment. Each molecule is attached to a short DNA strand that records how it was synthesised. The whole library is mixed with an immobilised protein, unbound molecules are washed away, and the DNA tags of the molecules that remain are sequenced. Molecules whose tags are enriched are called binders (hits).

Leash Biosciences screened about 133 million molecules against three proteins and released the data as **BELKA** (Big Encoded Library for Chemical Assessment). The competition rules also permitted physics-based methods such as docking (for example DiffDock).

<Deep title="The three protein targets" kind="domain">

- **BRD4 (bromodomain-containing protein 4)** binds acetylated lysines on histones and regulates gene expression. It is studied as a target in cancer and inflammation.
- **HSA (human serum albumin)** is the most abundant protein in blood plasma. Binding to albumin is a pharmacokinetic property of a drug rather than a therapeutic goal: strong albumin binding reduces the free, active fraction of the drug.
- **sEH (soluble epoxide hydrolase)** metabolises signalling lipids; its inhibitors have been studied for hypertension and inflammation.

The three proteins have different binding sites, so a molecule that binds one need not bind the others, and a model has to learn patterns specific to each target.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Construction of a DEL molecule" kind="domain">

The BELKA molecules are built on a **triazine core**, a six-membered ring with three attachment points. One **building block** is attached at each point, each drawn from a separate pool. If the pools contain $n_1$, $n_2$ and $n_3$ building blocks, the library has up to

$$
n_1 \times n_2 \times n_3
$$

molecules made from $n_1 + n_2 + n_3$ building blocks. For example, three pools of 1,000 building blocks each give up to $10^9$ molecules from 3,000 building blocks.

This combinatorial structure has two consequences for modelling.

1. Molecules that share a building block share a large part of their structure. Under a random split of molecules, a test molecule usually has all three of its building blocks represented in training.
2. A discovery campaign needs predictions for molecules with **new** building blocks, and a random split does not measure performance on them.
</Deep>

## Data

Each training row consists of a molecule (as a SMILES string, together with the SMILES of its three building blocks), a protein and a binary label, bound or not bound. The training row identifiers run to 295,246,829 (the test identifiers start at 295,246,830). With each molecule screened against all three proteins, this corresponds to

$$
295{,}246{,}830 / 3 = 98{,}415{,}610
$$

training molecules, about 98 million. About 0.5% of rows are positive, so there are about $0.995 / 0.005 = 199$ negatives per positive.

The test set has three groups:

- molecules whose building blocks all appear in training,
- molecules that contain building blocks absent from training, and
- molecules from a **different library**, with a different core and chemistry.

The private leaderboard weighted generalisation to unseen building blocks heavily, and rankings changed considerably between the public and private leaderboards: the team second on the public leaderboard finished 13th on the private one.

<Deep title="SMILES notation" kind="domain">

SMILES (Weininger, 1988) writes a molecular graph as a string of characters, obtained by a walk along the graph.

- Atoms are written by their element symbols: `C`, `N`, `O`, `Cl`. Aromatic atoms are written in lower case: `c`, `n`.
- Bonds between consecutive atoms are single unless marked (`=` double, `#` triple).
- Branches are enclosed in parentheses: `CC(C)O` is isopropanol.
- Rings are closed with matching digits: `c1ccccc1` is benzene.
- Atoms with a charge, explicit hydrogens or stereochemistry are written in brackets: `[NH3+]`, `[C@@H]`.

One molecule has many valid SMILES strings, and canonicalisation algorithms choose one of them. Atoms bonded to each other in the molecule can be far apart in the string, for example across a branch or at the two ends of a ring closure.
</Deep>

## Evaluation metric

For each protein and each test group, **average precision (AP)** is computed from the ranking of the predicted probabilities. The score is the mean of AP over these combinations.

<Foundation id="precision-recall-fbeta" />

<Deep title="Average precision" kind="math">

Sort the $N$ test molecules of one protein and group by predicted score, in decreasing order. Let $y_{(k)} \in \{0, 1\}$ be the label of the molecule at rank $k$, $N_+$ the number of positives, $\mathrm{TP}@k$ the number of positives among the top $k$, and

$$
\mathrm{P}@k = \frac{\mathrm{TP}@k}{k}, \qquad \mathrm{R}@k = \frac{\mathrm{TP}@k}{N_+}
$$

the precision and recall of the top $k$, with $\mathrm{R}@0 = 0$. Average precision is

$$
\mathrm{AP} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \big(\mathrm{R}@k - \mathrm{R}@(k-1)\big)\,\mathrm{P}@k .
$$

Recall increases by $1/N_+$ at each rank that holds a positive and is unchanged otherwise, so

$$
\mathrm{R}@k - \mathrm{R}@(k-1) = \frac{y_{(k)}}{N_+}.
$$

Substituting,

$$
\mathrm{AP} = \frac{1}{N_+} \sum_{k=1}^{N} y_{(k)}\, \mathrm{P}@k ,
$$

the mean of the precision values at the ranks where positives occur. It approximates the area under the precision–recall curve.

**AP depends only on the ranking.** Any strictly increasing transformation of the scores leaves the ranking, and therefore AP, unchanged, so calibration does not affect the score.

**The baseline is the positive rate.** For a random ranking, the expected precision at any rank is $N_+/N$, so the expected AP is close to $N_+ / N$: about 0.005 at a positive rate of 0.5%. For ROC AUC the random baseline is 0.5. A positive at rank $k$ contributes $\mathrm{P}@k / N_+$, and precision is highest when the positives are concentrated at the top of the ranking, so AP is determined mainly by the molecules the model scores highest. In a discovery campaign only the top-ranked molecules are synthesised and tested.

Each combination of protein and test group enters the mean with equal weight, whatever the number of molecules in the group.
</Deep>

### Model

The model is a transformer encoder in the style of the TensorFlow tutorial, with **4 layers, 8 heads, key and value dimension 32, and model dimension 32.** The author reports that model dimensions of 16 and 64 were both worse, and that more than 6 layers did not help.

<Foundation id="attention-transformers" />

**Tokenisation.** The vocabulary has 43 tokens and is close to character level: atoms, ring-closure digits, bond symbols, parentheses and bracket expressions such as `[C@@]` are single tokens. Tokenisations with bigrams or trigrams, and a correct use of the atomInSmiles tokeniser, were worse; the author notes that his own use of atomInSmiles was not the intended one.

### Pre-training stage 1: masked language modelling

The model was trained as a BERT-style masked language model on SMILES strings from the training set, the test set and external data. In each sequence 15% of the token positions are selected. Of these, 80% are replaced with a mask token, 10% with a random token and 10% are left unchanged, and the model predicts the original tokens at the selected positions. The loss was a categorical focal cross-entropy. Training ran for about 100 epochs of 10,000 steps at batch size 2,048, which is

$$
100 \times 10{,}000 \times 2{,}048 = 2.048 \times 10^{9}
$$

sequences, or about 20 passes over roughly 100 million molecules.

<Deep title="The masked-language-model objective" kind="math">

Let $x = (x_1, \dots, x_T)$ be a token sequence of length $T$ over a vocabulary $V$ (here $|V| = 43$), and let $M \subseteq \{1, \dots, T\}$ be the random set of selected positions. The corrupted input $\tilde x$ replaces the tokens at the selected positions as described above (mask, random token or unchanged). At each position $t$ the model with parameters $\theta$ outputs a distribution $p_\theta(\cdot \mid \tilde x)_t$ over $V$. The cross-entropy form of the loss is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MLM}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{x,\,M}\Big[\frac{1}{|M|}\sum_{t \in M} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid \tilde x)_t\Big],
$$

where the expectation is over sequences and random selections. The winner replaced $-\log p$ by the categorical focal loss $-(1 - p)^{\gamma} \log p$ at each selected position.

To predict a masked atom, the model has to use the surrounding string: valence, ring closures and common functional groups. The objective uses no binding labels, so it can include the unlabelled test molecules, among them those with unseen building blocks.

Devlin et al. (2019) motivate the 80/10/10 rule by the absence of mask tokens at fine-tuning time. If every selected token were masked, the model would only learn to predict tokens at mask positions; leaving some selected tokens unchanged, and replacing some with random tokens, requires it to produce a representation of every visible token that is useful for predicting it.
</Deep>

### Pre-training stage 2: fingerprint prediction

With the embeddings frozen, a sigmoid head was trained for 20–50 epochs to predict each molecule's **ECFP fingerprint** (2048 bits, with chirality). The author reports a mean average precision of about 0.4 on this task. Fingerprints were computed with the scikit-fingerprints library.

<Deep title="Extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP)" kind="math">

ECFP (Rogers and Hahn, 2010), closely related to Morgan fingerprints, describes a molecule by the set of circular substructures it contains. Let $\mathcal{N}(a)$ be the set of heavy atoms bonded to atom $a$, $\mathrm{bond}_{ab}$ the type of the bond between $a$ and $b$, and $\operatorname{hash}$ a fixed function from tuples to integers.

1. **Initialisation.** Each heavy atom $a$ receives an integer identifier $h_a^{(0)}$, a hash of its local invariants: element, number of heavy neighbours, number of hydrogens, charge and ring membership, and, with chirality enabled, its stereo configuration.
2. **Iteration** for $r = 1, \dots, R$. Each atom's identifier is updated from its own and its neighbours' previous identifiers, together with the bond types:

$$
h_a^{(r)} = \operatorname{hash}\Big(h_a^{(r-1)},\ \operatorname{sorted}\big\{(\mathrm{bond}_{ab},\, h_b^{(r-1)}) : b \in \mathcal{N}(a)\big\}\Big).
$$

   Sorting makes the result independent of the order in which neighbours are listed. After $r$ iterations, $h_a^{(r)}$ identifies the substructure within $r$ bonds of atom $a$. ECFP4 uses $R = 2$, which corresponds to a diameter of 4 bonds.
3. **Collection and folding.** All identifiers from all iterations are gathered, duplicates are removed, and bit $h \bmod 2048$ is set in a vector of 2048 bits for each remaining identifier $h$.

The procedure is message passing on the molecular graph with a fixed hash in place of a learned update; graph neural networks learn the update function instead.

A fingerprint bit depends on the atoms within a few bonds of each other in the graph, and these atoms can be far apart in the SMILES string. Predicting the fingerprint from the string therefore requires the transformer to represent graph neighbourhoods, and it adds substructure information to the representation without binding labels.
</Deep>

### Fine-tuning on binding

The final stage trained the model on the binding labels for the three proteins together with external sEH data from the paper "Building Block-Based Binding Predictions for DNA-Encoded Libraries". The external data were found by hengck23 and processed by chemdatafarmer. The external molecules have labels only for sEH, so the loss was **masked** for missing labels, and a **binary focal loss** was used for the positive rate of about 0.5%.

<Foundation id="focal-loss" />

<Deep title="Focal loss at the BELKA positive rate" kind="math">

For a molecule–protein pair with label $y \in \{0, 1\}$ and predicted probability $p \in (0, 1)$, let $p_t = p$ if $y = 1$ and $p_t = 1 - p$ if $y = 0$. With a focusing parameter $\gamma \ge 0$ and a class weight $\alpha_t \in (0, 1)$, the binary focal loss is

$$
\ell_{\mathrm{focal}} = -\,\alpha_t\,(1 - p_t)^{\gamma} \log p_t .
$$

With $\gamma = 2$, a negative scored at $p = 0.01$ has $p_t = 0.99$, and its cross-entropy is multiplied by $(1 - 0.99)^2 = 10^{-4}$. A positive scored at $p = 0.1$ has $p_t = 0.1$, and its cross-entropy is multiplied by $(1 - 0.1)^2 = 0.81$. With 199 negatives per positive, the modulating factor $(1 - p_t)^{\gamma}$ reduces the share of the loss that comes from negatives the model already scores near zero.

For a multi-task batch, let $B$ be the number of molecules, $j \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ index the proteins, and $m_{ij} \in \{0, 1\}$ indicate whether the label of molecule $i$ for protein $j$ is known. The masked loss averages over known labels only:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{B} \sum_{j=1}^{3} m_{ij}\, \ell_{\mathrm{focal}, ij}}{\sum_{i=1}^{B} \sum_{j=1}^{3} m_{ij}} .
$$

A molecule from the external sEH data has $m_{ij} = 1$ only for sEH.
</Deep>

<Deep title="Masked multi-task focal loss" kind="code">

A Nolad illustration in PyTorch of the equation above; the author's implementation is in TensorFlow.

```python
import torch

def masked_focal_bce(logits, targets, gamma=2.0, alpha=0.25):
    """logits, targets: (B, 3) for BRD4, HSA, sEH; targets = -1 where the label is missing."""
    known = targets >= 0
    y = targets.clamp(min=0).float()
    p = logits.sigmoid()
    p_t = torch.where(y == 1, p, 1 - p)
    a_t = torch.where(y == 1, torch.full_like(p, alpha), torch.full_like(p, 1 - alpha))
    ce = torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, y, reduction="none")
    loss = a_t * (1 - p_t) ** gamma * ce
    return (loss * known).sum() / known.sum().clamp(min=1)
```
</Deep>

### Validation

The author held out **3% of the building blocks**. Every molecule that contains at least one of them, about 9 million molecules, went to the validation set, and the rest to training. The validation set therefore has the same property as the test group with unseen building blocks: each validation molecule contains a building block that the model did not see in training.

<Deep title="Size of the validation set" kind="math">

Suppose, as an approximation, that the held-out building blocks account for 3% of the molecules at each of the three attachment points, independently. A molecule stays in training only if none of its three building blocks is held out, which has probability

$$
0.97^3 = 0.9127 .
$$

The fraction of molecules sent to validation is then

$$
1 - 0.9127 = 0.0873,
$$

which for 98.4 million molecules is

$$
0.0873 \times 98.4 \times 10^{6} \approx 8.6 \times 10^{6},
$$

consistent with the "about 9 million" reported. Under a random split of molecules, by contrast, a validation molecule would have almost all its building blocks in training, since each building block occurs in a large number of molecules.
</Deep>

<Foundation id="cross-validation" />

Grouping the split by building block is the molecular counterpart of grouping by patient in medical imaging: in both cases samples that share a source of correlation are kept on the same side of the split.

### Approaches that did not improve the score

The author lists the following: more complex tokenisations; wider or deeper models; multiple inputs (SMILES together with fingerprints); a month of pre-training on ZINC, a database of commercially available molecules; custom losses; and gated fusion of building-block representations. Training ran on a single A100 in Google Colab.

## Comparison with other competitions

- **Stanford Ribonanza** also predicts properties from biological sequences. Its winners gave a transformer a base-pairing probability matrix computed by a physics-based folding program. In BELKA, domain knowledge entered through a pre-training target (ECFP fingerprints) rather than through an input.
- **CAFA 5** (protein function prediction) used embeddings from protein language models pretrained on large collections of natural protein sequences as main inputs. The BELKA winner pre-trained from scratch on the competition's own molecules, and the author reports that a month of pre-training on the ZINC database did not help.
- **Leaderboard shift.** The change in rankings between the public and private leaderboards followed from test groups that differed from the training distribution by design. The winner's validation set was constructed to reproduce one of those groups.
