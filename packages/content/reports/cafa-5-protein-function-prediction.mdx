## Scientific background

Sequencing has produced hundreds of millions of protein sequences, and only a small fraction of these proteins have an experimentally established function. Most function annotations in databases are inferred computationally, often from sequence similarity to a protein whose function is known. Experiments are slow and expensive. Predictions are used to choose which experiments to carry out and to give provisional descriptions of the proteins that remain uncharacterised.

**CAFA (Critical Assessment of Functional Annotation)** is a community experiment that has evaluated protein function prediction since 2010. Its evaluation is **prospective**. Participants submit predictions for proteins that lack experimental annotations, and the predictions are scored months later against the experimental annotations that curators add in the meantime. At the submission deadline the answers are unknown to everyone, including the organisers. CAFA 5 was the first CAFA to run on Kaggle. It was organised by the Function-COSI community, with CAFA co-chairs Iddo Friedberg and Predrag Radivojac, co-organisers including M. Clara De Paolis Kaluza, Parnal Joshi and Damiano Piovesan, and UniProt.

<Deep title="The Gene Ontology" kind="domain">

The **Gene Ontology (GO)** is a controlled vocabulary of about 40,000–45,000 terms that describe the functions of gene products. It is divided into three **subontologies**:

- **Molecular Function (MF):** the activity of the protein at the molecular level, for example "ATP binding" or "protein kinase activity".
- **Biological Process (BP):** the larger processes in which the protein takes part, for example "DNA repair" or "response to heat".
- **Cellular Component (CC):** the location of the protein in the cell, for example "mitochondrial inner membrane" or "nucleus".

Each subontology is a **directed acyclic graph** (DAG). A term can have several parents, connected by relations such as "is a" and "part of". For example, "protein serine/threonine kinase activity" is a "protein kinase activity", which is a "kinase activity", which is a "catalytic activity".

Under the **true path rule**, a protein annotated with a term is also annotated with all of that term's ancestors. Annotations and predictions are therefore **propagated** to all ancestors before they are compared.

Each annotation carries an **evidence code**. Experimental codes, such as IDA (inferred from direct assay) and IMP (inferred from mutant phenotype), count as ground truth in CAFA. Electronic annotations (IEA) and other computational evidence codes do not.
</Deep>

### Protein representations used by the solutions

- **BLAST** searches a sequence database for sequences that align with a query and reports a similarity score (the bit score) for each hit.
- A **protein language model**, such as ESM-1b, ESM-2 or ProtT5, is a transformer trained on millions of protein sequences to predict amino acids that have been hidden from its input. The vector it computes for a sequence (an **embedding**) is used as a feature vector for downstream models.
- **STRING** is a database of known and predicted protein–protein interactions for many species.
- **InterProScan** assigns protein domain and family signatures to a sequence.
- **FoldSeek** searches a database of protein 3D structures, including structures predicted from sequence, for structures similar to a query.

## Data

The training set contains proteins with at least one experimental GO annotation, their sequences, and their propagated annotation sets. The test superset contains about 140,000 proteins. After the deadline, the organisers collected the experimental annotations added to these proteins. A protein is evaluated in a subontology only if it had **no experimental annotation in that subontology** at the deadline and received one afterwards.

Participants could use any public data, including sequence databases, annotations with non-experimental evidence, interaction networks, literature and predicted structures.

## Evaluation metric

For each protein, precision and recall are computed over the set of GO terms whose predicted score is at least a threshold $\tau$. They are averaged over proteins and combined into an F-measure, and **Fmax** is the maximum of this F-measure over $\tau$. In CAFA 5 each term is weighted by its **information accretion**. The root term "biological process", which applies to every protein, has weight zero, and a specific term that is rarely annotated has a large weight.

<Foundation id="precision-recall-fbeta" />

<Deep title="Information accretion and weighted Fmax" kind="math">

**Information accretion** (Clark and Radivojac, 2013). Let $f$ be a GO term with parent set $\mathrm{Pa}(f)$. Estimated from the annotation database, $P\big(f \mid \mathrm{Pa}(f)\big)$ is the probability that a protein has $f$ given that it has all of $f$'s parents. The information accretion of $f$ is

$$
\mathrm{ia}(f) = -\log_2 P\big(f \mid \mathrm{Pa}(f)\big) \ \ge 0 .
$$

If every protein with all the parents also has $f$, then $P = 1$ and $\mathrm{ia}(f) = 0$. If one protein in 8 has it, then $\mathrm{ia}(f) = -\log_2 \tfrac18 = 3$ bits.

The weights add up to the information content of an annotation set. Clark and Radivojac model the annotations as a Bayesian network on the DAG, in which each term depends only on its parents. Let $T$ be a propagated annotation set, so that every ancestor of a term in $T$ is also in $T$. The probability that a protein has all the terms of $T$ factorises over the terms,

$$
P(T) = \prod_{f \in T} P\big(f \mid \mathrm{Pa}(f)\big) ,
$$

and taking $-\log_2$ of both sides turns the product into a sum:

$$
-\log_2 P(T) = \sum_{f \in T} \mathrm{ia}(f) .
$$

**Weighted precision and recall.** For protein $i$, let $T_i$ be the true propagated annotation set and $s_i(f) \in [0,1]$ the predicted score of term $f$. The predicted set at threshold $\tau \in [0,1]$ is $P_i(\tau) = \{f : s_i(f) \ge \tau\}$, propagated. Define the information in the correctly predicted terms, in all predicted terms, and in all true terms:

$$
\mathrm{TP}_i(\tau) = \sum_{f \in P_i(\tau) \cap T_i} \mathrm{ia}(f), \qquad
\mathrm{Pred}_i(\tau) = \sum_{f \in P_i(\tau)} \mathrm{ia}(f), \qquad
\mathrm{True}_i = \sum_{f \in T_i} \mathrm{ia}(f).
$$

The weighted precision and recall of protein $i$ are

$$
\mathrm{pr}_i(\tau) = \frac{\mathrm{TP}_i(\tau)}{\mathrm{Pred}_i(\tau)}, \qquad
\mathrm{rc}_i(\tau) = \frac{\mathrm{TP}_i(\tau)}{\mathrm{True}_i} .
$$

**Example.** A protein has true terms $\{\text{root}, A, B\}$ with information accretion 0, 1 and 3 bits. The predicted set is $\{\text{root}, A, C\}$, with $\mathrm{ia}(C) = 2$. Then $\mathrm{TP} = 0 + 1 = 1$, $\mathrm{Pred} = 0 + 1 + 2 = 3$ and $\mathrm{True} = 0 + 1 + 3 = 4$, so the precision is $1/3$ and the recall is $1/4$. The correct prediction of the root adds nothing to either.

**Averaging over proteins.** Precision is averaged over the $m(\tau)$ proteins that have at least one predicted term at threshold $\tau$, and recall over all $n$ evaluated proteins:

$$
\mathrm{pr}(\tau) = \frac{1}{m(\tau)}\sum_{i \,:\, P_i(\tau) \ne \emptyset} \mathrm{pr}_i(\tau), \qquad
\mathrm{rc}(\tau) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{rc}_i(\tau) .
$$

The F-measure at $\tau$ and its maximum are

$$
F(\tau) = \frac{2\,\mathrm{pr}(\tau)\,\mathrm{rc}(\tau)}{\mathrm{pr}(\tau) + \mathrm{rc}(\tau)}, \qquad
F_{\max} = \max_{\tau} F(\tau) .
$$

The final score is the mean of $F_{\max}$ over MF, BP and CC.

**Consequences for modelling.**

- Scores should be consistent with the DAG: a term's score should not exceed the scores of its parents. A predicted term is propagated to its ancestors, so a child scored above its parent places the parent in the predicted set at thresholds where its own score would not. A common post-processing step raises each parent's score to the maximum of its children's scores.
- One threshold $\tau$ is applied to all proteins, so scores must be comparable between proteins, not only ordered correctly within one protein.
- Terms near the root have $P(f \mid \mathrm{Pa}(f))$ close to 1 and weights close to 0. Most of the score comes from specific terms deep in the DAG.
</Deep>

## Winning solution

Team GOCurator from Shanfeng Zhu's laboratory at Fudan University (Shaojun Wang, Weiqi Zhai, Wei Liu, Tianyang Huang and Huiying Yan) scored 0.61623 on the private leaderboard. The laboratory develops the **NetGO** systems, which ranked at the top of CAFA3 and CAFA4. The team extended NetGO 3.0 with new components.

<Foundation id="models-and-loss" />

The training data were Swiss-Prot sequences and GOA annotations with the evidence codes used in CAFA 5, and the GO graph.

### Components from NetGO 3.0

- **LR-ESM:** logistic regression for each GO term on ESM-1b embeddings. The team reports that ESM-2 and ProtT5 embeddings were no better than ESM-1b within the ensemble.
- **BLAST-KNN:** annotations transferred from training proteins with similar sequences, weighted by similarity.
- **Net-KNN:** annotations transferred from interaction partners in the STRING network. It performed poorly for species with few annotations and was used only for the 15 species with the most annotations.
- Further NetGO components based on InterProScan domain and family signatures.

<Deep title="Nearest-neighbour annotation transfer" kind="math">

Let $q$ be a query protein, $\mathcal{N}(q)$ its set of neighbours (by BLAST bit score, structural similarity or network edges), and $w_{qj} > 0$ the similarity weight of neighbour $j$, with propagated annotation set $T_j$. The score of term $f$ is the weighted fraction of neighbours annotated with it:

$$
s_q(f) = \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(q)} w_{qj}\,\mathbb{1}[f \in T_j]}{W_q}, \qquad W_q = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(q)} w_{qj} .
$$

These scores are consistent with the DAG. Let $g$ be a parent of $f$. Each $T_j$ is propagated, so $f \in T_j$ implies $g \in T_j$:

$$
\mathbb{1}[f \in T_j] \le \mathbb{1}[g \in T_j] \quad \text{for every } j .
$$

Multiplying by $w_{qj} > 0$, summing over $j$ and dividing by $W_q$ gives

$$
s_q(f) \le s_q(g) .
$$

Annotation transfer from similar sequences is the oldest method of function prediction, based on the observation that homologous proteins often share functions. It gives no prediction for proteins without annotated relatives, which is where the embedding-based and text-based components contribute.
</Deep>

### New components

- **FoldSeek-KNN:** the same transfer rule applied to proteins with similar **predicted 3D structures**, found with FoldSeek and combined by weighted voting. Protein structure is generally conserved over longer evolutionary distances than sequence, so this search can find relatives that BLAST does not.
- **LR-MEM:** logistic regression on the concatenated representations of the protein's **description**, its **literature** and its **sequence**.
- **GOXML:** an extreme multi-label classifier (AttentionXML) applied to the literature about the protein, similar to the laboratory's earlier DeepText2GO.
- **GORetrieval:** a two-stage text method. Candidate GO terms are first retrieved from proteins whose descriptions resemble the query's description. The candidates are then re-ranked by semantic matching, with PubMedBERT, between the definition of each GO term and the literature about the protein.

The text components also use Doc2Vec and TF-IDF representations, as in DeepText2GO. The write-up states that adding a single text-based or structure-based component to NetGO 3.0 achieves about 90% of the final performance. Curators derive experimental GO annotations from published papers, so a paper that describes a protein's function can exist before the corresponding annotation is added to the database.

### Learning to rank

Each component produces a score for every protein–term pair. A **learning-to-rank** model, as in NetGO, takes these scores together with a 20-dimensional one-hot encoding of the species and learns to order the candidate terms for each protein. The species is included because the reliability of the components differs between well-studied organisms and others.

<Deep title="Ranking candidate terms with shared features" kind="detail">

A classifier for each GO term would need a separate decision function for each of tens of thousands of terms, most of which have very few positive examples. The ranking model's features are the component scores and the species, not the identity of the term. It therefore learns how far to trust each component, and in which species, with parameters shared across all terms, and rare terms use what is learned from common ones. For example, the model can learn that a term scored highly by both BLAST-KNN and GORetrieval for a human protein is usually correct.

Fmax then requires one global threshold on the ranker's output.
</Deep>

### Validation

The team did not use a temporal split. It sampled 1,000 proteins for validation so that their **species distribution matched that of the test set**, because the species mix affects which components perform well.

<Foundation id="cross-validation" />

**Compute.** Computing the InterProScan and ESM-1b features took 1–2 days, training each component 10–15 hours, and training the ranker 50 minutes.

## Second place: embeddings, py-boost and conditional probabilities on the DAG

Team u900, which included Anton Vakhrushev, the author of the py-boost library, finished second on the private leaderboard and fifth on the public leaderboard. Their solution uses sequence embeddings and annotation data only.

**Features.** The main features were **ProtT5** embeddings; some models used ProtT5 and ESM-2 embeddings concatenated. A one-hot encoding of about 30 well-represented taxa was added, with the remaining taxa merged into one category. Annotations with electronic evidence (IEA) from GOA were also used as features for predicting the experimental labels.

**Base models**, trained with 5-fold cross-validation:

- **py-boost**, a GPU library for gradient boosting with many outputs, based on the SketchBoost method (Iosipoi and Vakhrushev, 2022). The model had 4,500 outputs (3,000 BP, 1,000 MF and 500 CC terms) and trained in about 1.5 hours per fold on one 32 GB V100.
- Logistic regression with 13,000 outputs, for rarer terms.
- A neural network from a public notebook.

<Foundation id="gradient-boosting" />

<Deep title="Conditional modelling on the ontology" kind="math">

Standard multi-label training treats each term as an independent binary target. Team u900 instead trained each term **conditionally on its parents**. For protein $i$ with true propagated set $T_i$ and term $f$, the training target is

$$
y_{if} =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } f \in T_i, \\
0 & \text{if } f \notin T_i \text{ and at least one parent of } f \text{ is in } T_i, \\
\text{masked} & \text{if no parent of } f \text{ is in } T_i .
\end{cases}
$$

Let $E_{if}$ denote the event that at least one parent of $f$ is in $T_i$. The model trained on these targets estimates

$$
p^{\mathrm{cond}}_{if} \approx P\big(f \in T_i \mid E_{if}\big) .
$$

The training examples for $f$ are restricted to proteins with a parent of $f$ annotated, so the fraction of positives is higher than in the unconditional problem.

**Recovering unconditional probabilities.** By the true path rule, $f \in T_i$ implies that all parents of $f$ are in $T_i$, and in particular $E_{if}$. Therefore

$$
P(f \in T_i) = P\big(f \in T_i \mid E_{if}\big)\, P(E_{if}) .
$$

Terms are processed in topological order, parents before children, so the unconditional probabilities $p^{\mathrm{raw}}_{ig}$ of the parents $g \in \mathrm{Pa}(f)$ are already known. Assuming the parents are present independently of each other,

$$
P(E_{if}) = 1 - \prod_{g \in \mathrm{Pa}(f)} \big(1 - p^{\mathrm{raw}}_{ig}\big) ,
$$

and substituting,

$$
p^{\mathrm{raw}}_{if} = p^{\mathrm{cond}}_{if} \cdot \Big(1 - \prod_{g \in \mathrm{Pa}(f)} \big(1 - p^{\mathrm{raw}}_{ig}\big)\Big) .
$$

The roots have $p^{\mathrm{raw}} = 1$. Terms without a model use the mean conditional frequency from the training data.

**Relation to the metric.** The information accretion $\mathrm{ia}(f) = -\log_2 P\big(f \mid \mathrm{Pa}(f)\big)$ is also defined through a conditional probability given the parents. It conditions on all parents being present, where the u900 target conditions on at least one, and it is estimated once from the database, where $p^{\mathrm{cond}}_{if}$ is predicted for each protein.

**Limitations.** If the parents are positively correlated, the probability that none is present is larger than the product $\prod_g (1 - p^{\mathrm{raw}}_{ig})$, so the independence formula overstates $P(E_{if})$. The recovered child probability can also exceed a parent's. With two parents at $0.6$ and $0.5$ and $p^{\mathrm{cond}} = 0.9$,

$$
P(E_{if}) = 1 - 0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.8, \qquad p^{\mathrm{raw}}_{if} = 0.9 \times 0.8 = 0.72,
$$

which exceeds both parents, although the true path rule requires $P(f \in T_i) \le 0.5$. A final consistency step is therefore still needed. The team describes the main benefit of the formulation as ensemble diversity: its predictions differ from those of standard multi-label models.
</Deep>

<Foundation id="binary-cross-entropy" />

**Stacking with a graph network.** A **graph convolutional network** (GCN) over the GO graph combined the base models. Each protein is represented as a graph with the same adjacency, the ontology, and the task is node classification. The node features of a term are the base models' predictions for that term, a learned embedding of the term, and GO annotation features. Through message passing, each term's final score depends on the scores of neighbouring terms. Shuffling the order of the base-model identifiers was used as test-time augmentation.

<Deep title="One layer of a graph convolutional network" kind="math">

Let $A \in \{0,1\}^{K \times K}$ be the adjacency matrix of the $K$ GO terms, made symmetric. Adding self-loops gives $\tilde A = A + I$, with degree matrix $\tilde D$, where $\tilde D_{kk} = \sum_{l} \tilde A_{kl}$. Define the normalised adjacency

$$
\hat A = \tilde D^{-1/2}\, \tilde A\, \tilde D^{-1/2} .
$$

A GCN layer (Kipf and Welling, 2017) maps the feature matrix $H^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d_l}$, one row per term, to

$$
H^{(l+1)} = \phi\big(\hat A\, H^{(l)} W^{(l)}\big),
$$

where $W^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_l \times d_{l+1}}$ is a learned weight matrix shared by all terms and $\phi$ is an elementwise nonlinearity. Entry $(k, l)$ of $\hat A$ is $1/\sqrt{\tilde D_{kk}\tilde D_{ll}}$ for neighbouring or identical terms and zero otherwise, so row $k$ of $\hat A H^{(l)}$ is a degree-normalised sum of the features of term $k$ and its neighbours.

After $L$ layers, the output for a term depends on all terms within $L$ edges of it in the ontology. A term's score can therefore be raised when its children are predicted strongly, or lowered when its parent is predicted weakly.
</Deep>

<Foundation id="ensembling-tta" />

**Per-subontology stacking.** A protein is evaluated in a subontology only if it gains its first experimental annotation there. The team therefore fitted the stacker separately for each subontology, on the proteins that have annotations in it.

## Comparison with other competitions

- **Protein language models** were used by both top teams. In Stanford Ribonanza, the winners report no gain from RNA-FM embeddings of RNA sequences. In Leash BELKA, the winner pretrained its own model on the competition's molecules with masked language modelling, and pretraining on the ZINC database did not help.
- **Hierarchical labels** also occur in medical competitions in this collection (vertebrae and patient in cervical spine fracture detection, organs and "any injury" in abdominal trauma detection). CAFA 5 is the only one with tens of thousands of classes arranged in a DAG.
- **Prospective evaluation.** The test labels did not exist at the submission deadline, so they could not leak into training.
