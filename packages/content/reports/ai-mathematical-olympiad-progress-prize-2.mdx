## The problem in its field

Mathematical olympiad problems are a demanding test of reasoning: they are short, precisely stated, have verifiable answers, and require combining ideas in ways that are not routine. The **AI Mathematical Olympiad (AIMO) Prize**, a $10 million fund from XTX Markets, rewards open models that solve such problems. The grand prize requires gold-medal performance at the International Mathematical Olympiad; intermediate **progress prizes** run on Kaggle.

The first progress prize (July 2024) was won by Project Numina with a 7B model that used Python during its reasoning, scoring 29 of 50. The second, reported here, raised the difficulty to around national olympiad level and kept the rule that problems are new, written for the competition, so that no model could have seen them.

<Deep title="Where this sits relative to other results" kind="domain">

Around the same time, much larger systems reached higher levels. In 2024 Google DeepMind's AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry solved IMO problems at silver-medal level, working in the formal proof language Lean with very large compute. In 2025, Google DeepMind and OpenAI reported gold-medal-level performance at the IMO with natural-language reasoning models.

AIMO measures something narrower and more practical: what an **open-weight** model achieves on **fixed, modest hardware** in **fixed time**, with the answer rather than a proof being graded. The winning methods are therefore as much about efficiency as about reasoning.
</Deep>

## The data

The competition provided only 10 reference problems with answers. The 100 test problems (50 public, 50 private) covered algebra, combinatorics, geometry and number theory. Every answer is an integer between 0 and 999 (problems whose natural answer is larger ask for it modulo 1000), so answers can be checked by exact match.

All training data therefore had to come from outside, which made data construction the central part of the winning work.

## The metric and the setting

Accuracy: the number of problems answered exactly right. Problems arrived one at a time through an evaluation API, so a notebook could not see all problems before allocating time. The whole run had to finish within **5 hours** for 50 problems, an average of 6 minutes per problem, on Kaggle's GPUs, without internet.

The overall grand prize required at least 47 of 50 on both public and private sets; no team came close.

<Deep title="How precise is a score out of 50?" kind="math">

If a model solves each problem independently with probability $\pi$, its score on 50 problems is binomial with mean $50\pi$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{50\,\pi(1-\pi)}$. At $\pi = 0.65$ that is $\sqrt{50 \times 0.65 \times 0.35} \approx 3.4$ problems. Sampling randomness in the model adds more variation between runs of the same submission.

The winners' public scores across submissions of the same system were 32, 33 and 28. A difference of two or three problems between teams is within the noise; the ranking reflects a combination of method and luck, and the method is better judged on larger benchmarks such as the 256-problem set the team built (below).
</Deep>

## How the winning solution works

The NemoSkills team from NVIDIA (Christof Henkel, Igor Gitman, Darragh Hanley, Ivan Sorokin, Benedikt Schifferer, Ivan Moshkov and Shubham Toshniwal) scored 34 of 50 on the private set. They documented the solution in a paper (Moshkov et al., 2025) and released everything needed to reproduce it.

### Building a dataset of problems

The Art of Problem Solving (AoPS) forums contain hundreds of thousands of competition problems posted and discussed by students and coaches. Posts are informal: problems mixed with discussion, partial solutions, several problems in one post. The team used an LLM to **extract** individual problems, classify them (proof or answer, multiple choice or not), extract final answers where the discussion contained them, and remove problems overlapping with benchmarks. This produced about **540,000** unique problems.

### Generating and filtering solutions

For each problem, strong open reasoning models (**DeepSeek-R1** and **QwQ-32B**) generated up to 32 long chain-of-thought solutions (temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95), with more samples for harder problems (difficulty estimated from the pass rate of Qwen2.5-Math-72B). A solution was kept only if its final answer matched the reference answer, with equivalence judged by Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (so that $\frac{1}{2}$ and $0.5$ match); where no reference existed, the majority answer among the samples served as one. The result was **3.2 million** solutions; the submission model was trained on the 2.2 million generated by R1.

<Deep title="Why distillation from filtered samples works" kind="math">

Supervised fine-tuning minimises the negative log-likelihood of the target tokens:
$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
$$
When $\mathcal{D}$ consists of samples from a teacher $q$ restricted to correct answers, the minimiser over a sufficiently expressive family is the teacher's distribution **conditioned on success**:
$$
p^*(y \mid x) = q(y \mid x, \text{answer}(y) \text{ correct}) = \frac{q(y\mid x)\,\mathbb{1}[\text{correct}(y)]}{\sum_{y'} q(y' \mid x)\,\mathbb{1}[\text{correct}(y')]}.
$$
This is **rejection sampling**: the student learns to imitate the teacher's successful reasoning, a distribution better than the teacher's own. Filtering by final answer alone admits some solutions whose reasoning is wrong but whose answer is right; with integer answers in a range of 1,000, such coincidences are rare for hard problems but not absent.

Sampling more for harder problems matters because those are where $\sum_{y'} q(y'\mid x)\mathbb{1}[\text{correct}]$ is small, and a few samples would yield no correct solution at all.
</Deep>

### Tool-integrated reasoning

Arithmetic and case enumeration are error-prone in text but trivial in code. **Tool-integrated reasoning (TIR)** lets the model write a Python block, execute it, read the output and continue. DeepSeek-R1 could not be prompted to use tools reliably, so the team bootstrapped:

1. Take a model lightly fine-tuned on reasoning traces (LIMO) and prompt it to solve problems with code.
2. Keep only correct solutions with genuinely useful code (filtered aggressively, for example on the number of executions and whether the code contributed).
3. Train a model on them, generate again, filter again, and repeat.

This produced 1.7 million TIR solutions, of which a final 15,000 of the highest quality were used. Code blocks are delimited by `<tool_call>` tags, executed in a sandbox, and their output appended as an `output` block before generation continues.

### Training

**Qwen2.5-14B-Base** was fine-tuned on the 2.2 million chain-of-thought solutions for 8 epochs: AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$ and weight decay 0.01, 10% warm-up, decay to $10^{-7}$, batch size 1,024, with sequence packing and context parallelism in NeMo-Aligner. The **RoPE base** was raised to 500,000 to support long reasoning traces. This took 48 hours on 512 H100 GPUs; the authors note that about 20% of that compute already gives most of the final strength. Checkpoints from different stages were averaged.

<Foundation id="attention-transformers" />

<Deep title="Why raise the RoPE base for long sequences" kind="math">

Rotary position embeddings rotate each pair of dimensions $(2i, 2i+1)$ of queries and keys at position $m$ by an angle $m\,\theta_i$, with frequencies
$$
\theta_i = b^{-2i/d}, \qquad i = 0, \dots, d/2 - 1,
$$
where $b$ is the base (10,000 in the original formulation) and $d$ the head dimension. The attention score between positions $m$ and $n$ then depends on $(m - n)\theta_i$ only. Low-index pairs rotate fast and encode fine position; high-index pairs rotate slowly and encode coarse position. The slowest pair completes a full turn after $2\pi / \theta_{d/2-1} \approx 2\pi b$ positions.

Reasoning traces here average 11,000–16,000 tokens and can be much longer. If the slowest frequencies complete many rotations within the context, distant positions become hard to distinguish. Raising $b$ to 500,000 lowers every frequency (except $\theta_0 = 1$) and stretches the longest wavelength by a factor of 50, so positions tens of thousands of tokens apart remain distinguishable. The model is fine-tuned at the new base so that it adapts to the slower rotations.
</Deep>

The TIR model was obtained by continuing training on the 15,000 TIR solutions for 400 steps at a constant learning rate of $10^{-5}$.

### Merging

The chain-of-thought (CoT) model reasons in text; the TIR model uses code but produces longer outputs. Rather than choose, the team **merged** their weights linearly with mergekit: $\theta = 0.3\,\theta_{\text{CoT}} + 0.7\,\theta_{\text{TIR}}$. Both descend from the same base and the TIR model from the CoT model, so their weights lie close together and a linear combination is itself a good model.

<Foundation id="weight-averaging" />

They measured all variants on **Comp-Math-24-25**, a 256-problem benchmark they assembled from AIME 2024 (30), AIME 2025 (30) and HMMT November 2024 (62), February 2024 (68) and February 2025 (66):

| Model | maj@16 | pass@16 | Mean length (tokens) | Code calls |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CoT | 62.9 | 76.2 | 11,203 | — |
| TIR | 66.8 | 80.1 | 15,834 | 2.73 |
| Merged | **69.1** | **81.3** | 12,489 | 0.85 |

The merged model is better than either parent and produces shorter outputs than the TIR model, which matters under a time limit. It calls code less often but more usefully.

### Inference: spending five hours well

**Quantisation.** The model was compiled with TensorRT-LLM. Throughput and accuracy for different formats:

| Format | Tokens/s | Time for 50 problems | AIME24 | AIME25 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BF16 | 210 | 2 h | 82.7 | 66.7 |
| INT8 weights (W8A16) | 315 | 1 h 45 | 82.7 | 66.7 |
| INT4 weights (W4A16) | 436 | 1 h 35 | 72.7 | 60.7 |
| FP8 | 310 | 1 h 40 | 83.3 | 68.7 |
| FP8 + ReDrafter | **554** | **1 h** | 81.3 | 71.3 |

(AIME scores are majority voting over 12 samples, averaged over 5 runs.) Four-bit weights lost too much accuracy; FP8 kept it.

<Foundation id="mixed-precision-quantisation" />

**Speculative decoding.** A small **ReDrafter** head, trained on 100,000 solutions generated by the model itself, proposes 3 tokens per step; the full model verifies them in one forward pass, accepting about 65% of proposals, for a speed-up of about 1.8×.

<Deep title="Why speculative decoding gives the same outputs faster" kind="math">

Autoregressive generation needs one full forward pass per token, and at small batch sizes each pass is limited by memory bandwidth (loading the weights), not by arithmetic. Verifying $k$ proposed tokens in one pass costs about the same as generating one.

A cheap draft model proposes tokens $\tilde y_1, \dots, \tilde y_k$ with probabilities $q(\cdot)$. The target model computes its probabilities $p(\cdot)$ at all $k$ positions in one pass. Each proposed token is accepted with probability $\min\big(1, p(\tilde y)/q(\tilde y)\big)$, in order; at the first rejection, a replacement is sampled from the normalised residual $\max(0, p - q)$. Leviathan et al. (2023) show that the accepted sequence has **exactly** the distribution of sampling from $p$: speculative decoding is lossless.

For greedy decoding (temperature 0, as used here) the rule simplifies: accept the draft tokens as long as they equal the target model's argmax.

If each token is accepted independently with probability $\beta$ and at most $k$ are proposed, the expected number of tokens produced per target pass is
$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens}] = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \beta^j = \frac{1 - \beta^{k+1}}{1 - \beta}.
$$
With $\beta = 0.65$ and $k = 3$: $(1 - 0.65^4)/(0.35) = (1 - 0.1785)/0.35 \approx 2.35$ tokens per pass. The measured speed-up of about 1.8× is lower because the draft head also costs time and acceptance is not independent between positions.
</Deep>

**Parallel sampling and early stopping.** For each problem, 12 generations with different seeds run concurrently with in-flight batching. The server watches the output streams; when a generation closes a code block, the code is executed and the result appended before generation resumes. The final answer is the **majority vote** among completed generations.

- Stop early when the answers agree, for example when 4 of the first 5 finished generations give the same answer.
- Otherwise stop when 10 of 12 have finished.
- Time budget: 350 seconds per problem, with unused time added to a shared buffer that later hard problems can draw on, up to 560 seconds.

<Deep title="Majority voting and early stopping" kind="math">

If each sample independently gives the correct answer with probability $\pi$ and wrong answers are spread over many values, the majority answer of $n$ samples is correct with high probability once $\pi$ exceeds the frequency of the most common wrong answer, and the probability grows with $n$. For two options (correct versus one recurring wrong answer) with $n$ odd, the majority is correct with probability
$$
P_n = \sum_{j > n/2} \binom{n}{j} \pi^j (1 - \pi)^{n-j},
$$
which tends to 1 as $n$ grows if $\pi > 1/2$ (Condorcet's jury theorem). Wrong answers in olympiad problems are usually scattered, so majority voting helps even when $\pi < 1/2$. This is self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023).

**Early stopping** saves time where it is not needed. If the first 5 answers include 4 identical ones, the chance that the remaining 7 overturn the majority is small for any plausible $\pi$: the remaining samples would need to produce a different common answer at least 5 times. Easy problems finish after a fraction of the budget, and the saved time goes to hard problems through the shared buffer. Stopping after 10 of 12 completions avoids waiting for the longest (often looping or failing) generations.
</Deep>

### What they left out

Stronger (larger) models generated too many tokens for the time limit. A 7B model was fast but weaker; a 32B CoT model was no better than the 14B. Generative re-ranking and outcome reward models were not used. For comparison, the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B model in the same pipeline scored 23–29 on the public set, with high variance.

## How this compares with other competitions

- **AIMO-2 is the most compute-intensive solution in the collection** by far (512 H100s for training), but the part a user needs, the released 14B model, runs on modest hardware. In the other reports the deployable artefact and the training cost are of similar scale.
- **Synthetic data from stronger models** plays the role that external data plays elsewhere (ISIC archives for melanoma, EyePACS for APTOS). Here the external data were generated, not collected, and quality control by answer checking replaced expert labelling.
- **Inference-time budgeting** (quantisation, speculative decoding, early stopping) parallels the runtime engineering of the code competitions in medical imaging, where GPU decoding and model pruning were needed to fit time limits.
