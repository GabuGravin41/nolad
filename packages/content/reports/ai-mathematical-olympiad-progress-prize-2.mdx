## Background

Mathematical olympiad problems are short and precisely stated, and many have a single numerical answer that can be checked automatically. Solving them requires combining several ideas in a way that is not given by a standard procedure. The **AI Mathematical Olympiad (AIMO) Prize**, a $10 million fund from XTX Markets, rewards open models that solve such problems. Its grand prize requires gold-medal performance at the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO); intermediate **progress prizes** are run as Kaggle competitions.

The first progress prize (July 2024) was won by Project Numina with NuminaMath, a 7-billion-parameter model that executed Python during its reasoning and answered 29 of 50 problems. The second progress prize, the subject of this report, used harder problems, at about the level of national olympiads. As in the first, the problems were written for the competition and had not been published, so that no model could have seen them in training.

<Deep title="Other results in machine olympiad solving" kind="domain">

In 2024 Google DeepMind's AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry solved IMO problems at silver-medal level. They worked in the formal proof language Lean and used very large amounts of compute. In 2025 Google DeepMind (Gemini Deep Think) and OpenAI (an experimental model) reported gold-medal-level performance at the IMO with models that reason in natural language.

The AIMO progress prizes impose different conditions: the model must have open weights, it runs on fixed Kaggle hardware within a fixed time, and only the final answer is graded, not a proof. Much of the winning work therefore concerned the use of limited inference time.
</Deep>

## Data

The competition provided 110 problems: 10 reference problems with answers, and 100 hidden test problems, 50 for the public leaderboard and 50 for the private leaderboard. The problems cover algebra, combinatorics, geometry and number theory. Every answer is an integer from 0 to 999; a problem whose natural answer is larger asks for it modulo 1000. Answers can therefore be checked by exact match.

With only 10 example problems, all training data had to come from other sources, and most of the winning team's work went into constructing them.

## Evaluation metric

The score is the number of problems answered correctly. Problems were served one at a time by an evaluation API, so a notebook could not see all the problems before allocating its time. Each answer had to be returned within 30 minutes, and the whole run had to finish within **5 hours** for 50 problems, an average of 6 minutes per problem, on Kaggle GPUs without internet access.

The overall progress prize required at least 47 of 50 on both the public and the private set. No team reached it.

<Deep title="Sampling variation of a score out of 50" kind="math">

Suppose a system solves each of the 50 problems independently with probability $\pi \in [0,1]$. Its score $S$ is then binomial,

$$
S \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(50, \pi), \qquad \mathbb{E}[S] = 50\pi, \qquad \mathrm{SD}(S) = \sqrt{50\,\pi(1-\pi)} .
$$

For $\pi = 0.68$, the winner's private accuracy of 34/50,

$$
50 \times 0.68 \times 0.32 = 10.88, \qquad \sqrt{10.88} \approx 3.3 \text{ problems}.
$$

This variation comes from which problems happen to be in the set. The randomness of sampling from the model adds further variation between runs of the same submission: the winning system's public scores across submissions were 32, 33 and 28. Differences of two or three problems between teams are within this range. The team's 256-problem benchmark (described below) gives a more precise comparison of methods.
</Deep>

## Winning solution

The winning team, NemoSkills from NVIDIA (Christof Henkel, Igor Gitman, Darragh Hanley, Ivan Sorokin, Benedikt Schifferer, Ivan Moshkov and Shubham Toshniwal), answered 34 of the 50 private problems correctly. The team described the solution in a paper (Moshkov et al., 2025) and released the dataset, the model and the code needed to reproduce it.

### Problem collection

The Art of Problem Solving (AoPS) forums contain hundreds of thousands of competition problems posted and discussed by students and coaches. The posts are informal: a problem may be mixed with discussion and partial solutions, and one post may contain several problems. The team used a language model to **extract** individual problems, to classify them (proof or answer, multiple choice or not), to extract final answers where the discussion contained them, and to remove problems that overlap with benchmarks. The result was about **540,000** unique problems.

### Solution generation and filtering

For each problem, two open reasoning models, **DeepSeek-R1** and **QwQ-32B**, generated up to 32 long chain-of-thought (CoT) solutions at temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.95. Harder problems received more samples; difficulty was estimated from the pass rate of Qwen2.5-Math-72B. A solution was kept only if its final answer matched the reference answer. Equivalence of answers, for example $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $0.5$, was judged by Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct. For problems without a reference answer, the majority answer among the samples was used as the reference. This produced **3.2 million** solutions. The submitted model was trained on the 2.2 million generated by DeepSeek-R1.

<Deep title="Fine-tuning on filtered samples" kind="math">

Let $x$ be a problem, $y = (y_1, \dots, y_{|y|})$ a solution as a token sequence, and $p_\theta$ the student model with parameters $\theta$. Supervised fine-tuning minimises the negative log-likelihood of the solutions in the training set $\mathcal{D}$:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) .
$$

Let $q(y \mid x)$ be the teacher's distribution over solutions and $c(y) \in \{0, 1\}$ indicate that the final answer of $y$ is correct. Define the teacher's success probability on $x$,

$$
\rho(x) = \sum_{y'} q(y' \mid x)\, c(y') .
$$

The solutions kept for $x$ are samples from $q$ with the incorrect ones discarded, so they are distributed as $q$ conditioned on $c(y) = 1$:

$$
q_{+}(y \mid x) = \frac{q(y \mid x)\, c(y)}{\rho(x)} .
$$

The cross-entropy is minimised when the model distribution equals the data distribution, so for a sufficiently expressive model family the minimiser is $p_{\theta^*}(\cdot \mid x) = q_{+}(\cdot \mid x)$. This procedure is rejection sampling. On the training problems the target distribution places all its mass on solutions with correct final answers, whereas the teacher itself succeeds with probability $\rho(x)$.

The filter checks the final answer only. A solution with an error in its reasoning and a correct final answer is kept; the write-up does not report how often this occurs.

**Number of samples for hard problems.** With $n$ independent samples from $q$, the probability that none is correct is $(1 - \rho(x))^n$. For a hard problem with $\rho(x) = 0.05$,

$$
n = 4: \quad 0.95^{4} \approx 0.81, \qquad\qquad n = 32: \quad 0.95^{32} \approx 0.19 .
$$

With 4 samples such a problem contributes no training solution in 81% of cases; with 32 samples, in 19%. Allocating more samples to problems with low estimated pass rates keeps more hard problems in the training set.
</Deep>

### Tool-integrated reasoning

In **tool-integrated reasoning (TIR)** the model writes a block of Python code during its solution, the code is executed, and its output is appended to the text before generation continues. Computation and case enumeration can then be done by the interpreter. Code blocks are delimited by `<tool_call>` tags, executed in a sandbox, and their output is appended as an `output` block.

The team reports that DeepSeek-R1 could not be prompted to use the tool reliably, so TIR solutions were built up iteratively:

1. A model lightly fine-tuned on reasoning traces (LIMO) was prompted to solve problems with code.
2. The generated solutions were filtered strictly, by correctness of the final answer and by the number of code executions.
3. A model was trained on the remaining solutions, generated new ones, and the filtering was repeated.

This produced 1.7 million TIR solutions, from which a final set of 15,000 was selected.

### Training

**Qwen2.5-14B-Base** was fine-tuned on the 2.2 million CoT solutions for 8 epochs with AdamW (learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01, 10% warm-up, decay to $10^{-7}$) and batch size 1,024, using sequence packing and context parallelism in NeMo-Aligner. The base of the rotary position embedding (**RoPE base**) was set to 500,000 for the long reasoning traces. Training took 48 hours on 512 H100 GPUs; the authors report that about 20% of this compute already gives most of the final accuracy. Checkpoints from different stages of training were averaged.

<Deep title="Rotary position embeddings and the base" kind="math">

Let $d$ be the dimension of one attention head, $b > 1$ the RoPE base, and $m \in \mathbb{N}$ a token position. Rotary position embeddings rotate the pair of coordinates $(2i, 2i+1)$ of the query and key vectors at position $m$ by the angle $m\,\theta_i$, where

$$
\theta_i = b^{-2i/d}, \qquad i = 0, 1, \dots, \tfrac{d}{2} - 1 .
$$

The dot product of a query at position $m$ with a key at position $n$ then depends on the positions only through the differences of angles $(m - n)\,\theta_i$. The pair $i$ completes a full rotation after

$$
\lambda_i = \frac{2\pi}{\theta_i} = 2\pi\, b^{2i/d}
$$

positions, its wavelength. Two relative distances that differ by a multiple of $\lambda_i$ give the same angle in pair $i$, so only pairs whose wavelength exceeds a distance can represent that distance without this ambiguity.

**Worked numbers.** Take $d = 128$, so there are 64 pairs, and a context of $L = 32{,}768$ tokens. The longest wavelength is

$$
\lambda_{63} = 2\pi\, b^{126/128} \approx
\begin{cases}
5.4 \times 10^{4} & \text{for } b = 10{,}000, \\
2.6 \times 10^{6} & \text{for } b = 500{,}000 .
\end{cases}
$$

The condition $\lambda_i > L$ holds for 4 of the 64 pairs when $b = 10{,}000$, the value in the original RoPE formulation, and for 22 of the 64 pairs when $b = 500{,}000$. A larger base therefore gives more coordinate pairs that vary slowly enough to distinguish positions tens of thousands of tokens apart. On the team's benchmark the mean solution lengths of the final models were between 11,203 and 15,834 tokens (table below), and individual solutions are longer. Fine-tuning at the chosen base adapts the model to these rotation frequencies.
</Deep>

The TIR model was obtained from the CoT model by further training on the 15,000 TIR solutions for 400 steps at a constant learning rate of $10^{-5}$.

### Model merging

The CoT model reasons in text; the TIR model uses code and produces longer outputs. The team combined the two by **linear merging** of their weights with mergekit,

$$
\theta_{\mathrm{merged}} = 0.3\,\theta_{\mathrm{CoT}} + 0.7\,\theta_{\mathrm{TIR}},
$$

where $\theta$ denotes the full parameter vector of each model. Linear merging was the best of the merging methods the team tried. The TIR model was trained from the CoT model, which was trained from the same base, so the two parameter vectors are close; this is the usual condition under which a weighted average of weights gives a working model.

<Foundation id="weight-averaging" />

The team compared the variants on **Comp-Math-24-25**, a 256-problem benchmark assembled from AIME 2024 (30 problems), AIME 2025 (30), and HMMT November 2024 (62), February 2024 (68) and February 2025 (66). In the table, maj@16 is the accuracy of the majority answer of 16 samples, and pass@16 is the fraction of problems for which at least one of 16 samples is correct.

| Model | maj@16 | pass@16 | Mean length (tokens) | Code calls |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CoT | 62.9 | 76.2 | 11,203 | — |
| TIR | 66.8 | 80.1 | 15,834 | 2.73 |
| Merged | **69.1** | **81.3** | 12,489 | 0.85 |

The merged model had a higher maj@16 than either parent (69.1 against 66.8 for TIR). Its outputs were on average 3,345 tokens shorter than those of the TIR model, and it made 0.85 code calls per solution against 2.73. Under a fixed time limit, shorter outputs allow more samples per problem.

### Inference

**Quantisation.** The model was compiled with TensorRT-LLM. The team measured throughput and accuracy for several numerical formats:

| Format | Tokens/s | Time for 50 problems | AIME24 | AIME25 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BF16 | 210 | 2 h | 82.7 | 66.7 |
| INT8 weights (W8A16) | 315 | 1 h 45 | 82.7 | 66.7 |
| INT4 weights (W4A16) | 436 | 1 h 35 | 72.7 | 60.7 |
| FP8 | 310 | 1 h 40 | 83.3 | 68.7 |
| FP8 + ReDrafter | **554** | **1 h** | 81.3 | 71.3 |

The AIME scores are the accuracy of majority voting over 12 samples, averaged over 5 runs. Four-bit weights lowered AIME24 accuracy from 82.7 to 72.7; FP8 gave 83.3 at 1.5 times the BF16 throughput.

<Foundation id="mixed-precision-quantisation" />

**Speculative decoding.** A small **ReDrafter** head, trained on 100,000 solutions generated by the model itself, proposes 3 tokens per step. The full model checks the proposals in one forward pass. The team reports that about 65% of proposed tokens were accepted and that generation was about 1.8 times faster.

<Deep title="Speculative decoding" kind="math">

In autoregressive generation each new token requires one forward pass of the full model. At small batch sizes the time of a pass is dominated by reading the weights from memory, so a pass that scores $k$ additional positions costs about as much as a pass that scores one.

A cheap draft model proposes tokens $\tilde y_1, \dots, \tilde y_k$ with probabilities $q(\cdot)$. The target model computes its own probabilities $p(\cdot)$ at all $k$ positions in one pass. The proposed tokens are examined in order, and token $\tilde y$ is accepted with probability $\min\big(1,\, p(\tilde y)/q(\tilde y)\big)$. At the first rejection, a replacement token is sampled from the residual distribution proportional to $\max(0,\, p - q)$, and the remaining proposals are discarded. Leviathan et al. (2023) prove that the resulting tokens have exactly the distribution of sampling from $p$, so the output distribution is unchanged. For greedy decoding (temperature 0, which the team used), the rule reduces to accepting draft tokens as long as they equal the target model's most probable token.

**Expected tokens per pass.** Assume each proposal is accepted independently with probability $\beta \in [0,1)$, given that all earlier ones were. Let $J \in \{0, \dots, k\}$ be the number of accepted proposals. The first $j$ proposals are all accepted with probability

$$
P(J \ge j) = \beta^{j}, \qquad j = 0, \dots, k .
$$

Each target pass yields the $J$ accepted tokens plus one token from the target model (the replacement after a rejection, or the next token after $k$ acceptances). For a non-negative integer variable, $\mathbb{E}[J] = \sum_{j=1}^{k} P(J \ge j)$, so

$$
\mathbb{E}[J + 1] = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^{k} \beta^{j} = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \beta^{j} .
$$

Summing the geometric series,

$$
\mathbb{E}[J + 1] = \frac{1 - \beta^{k+1}}{1 - \beta} .
$$

With $\beta = 0.65$ and $k = 3$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[J + 1] = 1 + 0.65 + 0.4225 + 0.2746 = 2.347 \ \text{tokens per pass}.
$$

The measured speed-up of about 1.8 is smaller than 2.35. The draft head takes time of its own, and acceptances at successive positions need not be independent; the write-up does not separate the two effects.
</Deep>

**Parallel sampling and early stopping.** For each problem, 12 generations with different random seeds run concurrently with in-flight batching. The server monitors the output streams; when a generation closes a code block, the code is executed and its output is appended before generation resumes. The final answer is the **majority vote** among the completed generations, with these rules:

- Generation stops early when the answers agree, for example when 4 of the first 5 completed generations give the same answer.
- Otherwise it stops when 10 of the 12 generations have finished.
- Each problem has a base budget of 350 seconds. Unused time goes into a shared buffer from which later problems can draw up to 210 further seconds, for a maximum of 560 seconds.

<Deep title="Majority voting and early stopping" kind="math">

**Majority voting.** Suppose each sample independently gives the correct answer with probability $\pi$, and every wrong sample gives the same wrong answer. With $n$ samples, $n$ odd, the majority is correct when more than $n/2$ samples are correct:

$$
P_n = \sum_{j > n/2} \binom{n}{j}\, \pi^{j} (1 - \pi)^{n-j} .
$$

If $\pi > \tfrac12$, then $P_n \to 1$ as $n \to \infty$ (Condorcet's jury theorem). When wrong answers are spread over many different values, the correct answer can win a plurality vote even when $\pi < \tfrac12$, provided that $\pi$ exceeds the probability of each individual wrong answer. Voting over sampled solutions in this way is called self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023).

**Early stopping.** Suppose 4 of the first 5 answers equal $a$, and the fifth is some other answer $b$. Of the 7 remaining samples, let $k$ give $b$ and $j$ give $a$, with $k + j \le 7$. Answer $b$ would finish ahead of $a$ only if

$$
1 + k > 4 + j, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad k \ge 4 + j \ge 4 .
$$

So at least 4 of the 7 remaining samples would have to give the single answer $b$. If each remaining sample gives $b$ independently with probability $r$, the probability of this is

$$
P(k \ge 4) = \sum_{k=4}^{7} \binom{7}{k}\, r^{k} (1 - r)^{7-k},
$$

which is 0.033 for $r = 0.2$ and 0.126 for $r = 0.3$. When the first answers agree, the remaining samples change the result only with small probability, and the time they would have used goes into the shared buffer for later problems. Stopping after 10 of 12 completions removes the wait for the two slowest generations.
</Deep>

### Methods that were not used

The team reports that stronger, larger models generated too many tokens for the time limit. A 7B model was fast but less accurate, and a 32B CoT model was no better than the 14B model. Generative re-ranking and outcome reward models were not used. For comparison, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B in the same inference pipeline scored between 23 and 29 on the public set, with high variance between submissions.

## Comparison with other competitions

- **Training and deployment cost.** Training the winning model took 48 hours on 512 H100 GPUs, but the released artefact is a 14-billion-parameter model that runs on a few GPUs.
- **Generated training data.** Solutions generated by stronger models take the place that collected external data has in other competitions in this collection, such as the ISIC archive for melanoma classification or EyePACS for APTOS 2019. Filtering by final answer takes the place of expert labelling.
- **Inference within a time limit.** Quantisation, speculative decoding and early stopping serve the same purpose as the runtime engineering in the medical-imaging code competitions, for example GPU image decoding in RSNA breast cancer detection or the removal of a decoder block in the intracranial aneurysm winner's classifier.
